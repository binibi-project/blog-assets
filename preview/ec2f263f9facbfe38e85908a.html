Six ingredients in one bowl, twenty minutes of marinating, a few minutes in a hot pan: that's most of what separates bulgogi that tastes like a Seoul restaurant from beef that tastes like boiled meat with soy sauce poured over it. The kitchen starts to smell like it before the pan even goes on. Garlic and scallion work into sugar and soy until the whole bowl turns dark and a little sticky.

Once the beef hits the heat, that sugar catches at the edges before the center's even cooked through, and that's the smell that pulls people out of the next room. It's a dish most people outside Korea already know by name, and one of the easier ones to actually get right at home.

Get the cut and the marinating time right, and the pan does almost all the rest.

What is bulgogi (불고기)?

Bul means fire, gogi means meat, and the Korean dictionary defines it plainly as thin-sliced beef, marinated, then grilled over an open flame. Most home versions today trade the open flame for a hot pan, and the marinade is the part that actually carries the dish.

🥩 The recipe: bulgogi for four

Four servings, 500 grams of beef, and one bowl for the marinade. That's hansik.or.kr's official version of bulgogi, published by Korea's own Food Promotion Institute.

Ingredient Amount
Beef sirloin, thinly sliced 500g
Onion 1 (200g)
Shiitake or oyster mushroom 3 (90g)
Green onion 6 stalks (60g)
Cooking oil 1 tbsp
Soy sauce 5 tbsp
Sugar 3 tbsp
Green onion, minced 2 tbsp
Garlic, minced 1 tbsp
Sesame oil 1 tbsp
Black pepper 1/2 tsp
Sesame seeds 1 tbsp
Asian pear, grated (optional) 1/4 (150g), or 5 tbsp pear juice
Cooking wine (optional) 2 tbsp

Everything above except the pear and cooking wine is fixed, not a range to eyeball from. hansik.or.kr marks only those two as optional; the rest of the marinade is the whole recipe.

  1. Slice the beef thin, about 2mm, trim any excess fat, and pat it dry. Cut the onion into strips, slice the mushrooms thin, and cut the green onion into roughly 4cm pieces.

  2. Grate the pear, or measure out the pear juice, and mix it with the soy sauce, sugar, minced garlic, minced green onion, sesame oil, black pepper and sesame seeds into one marinade.

  3. Coat the sliced beef in the marinade and let it sit for 20 minutes.

  4. Add the onion and mushroom to the marinated beef, drop in the scallion pieces too, and toss it all together.

  5. Heat the oil in a pan and stir-fry on high heat until the beef is just cooked through, or grill it directly over an open flame, the original method the recipe also lists.

What is neobiani (너비아니)?

Bulgogi's older, more formal name, served at the Joseon-dynasty royal court and cut into broader, flatter slices than most home versions today. The dish traces back further still, to skewered beef called maekjeok in the Goguryeo era, and the everyday name and marinade spread nationwide only after Korean War refugees carried the Pyongan-style version south.

🔥 Where bulgogi usually goes wrong

The marinade rarely fails. The slicing does. Beef cut too thick stays chewy no matter how long it sits in soy sauce, and a home kitchen without a meat slicer struggles to match a butcher's blade.

The fix costs nothing: partially freeze the beef for about an hour, until it's firm but not solid, then cut it across the grain into slices about 1/8 inch (3mm) thick. Skip that step and even a good marinade can't save a piece of meat closer to a steak than a stir-fry.

The second failure point is the pan, not the marinade either. Crowd the beef in, or leave it over the heat too long, and it steams instead of searing, drying out in the process. Cook it in batches if the pan looks full, and pull it the moment the pink disappears.

What does the Asian pear actually do?

Grate it into the marinade and the beef comes out closer to a restaurant's than a plain soy-sauce soak, and the reason is chemical: pear carries enzymes that break down the same proteins that make meat tough, the way a store-bought tenderizer does. A Bosc pear or a sweet apple stands in almost as well if Asian pear isn't sold nearby; kiwi and pineapple work faster, but use more than a spoon of either and the beef turns mushy instead of tender.

Go deeper
  • If you have one more hour Grill the beef instead of pan-frying it. hansik.or.kr's own recipe lists an open flame as the original method, not just a pan, closer to what the dish's name, "fire meat," actually describes.
  • What locals argue about Dry versus wet bulgogi. Most home and restaurant versions are pan-grilled with little liquid left by the time they're done, but a hot-pot style called ttukbaegi bulgogi simmers the same marinated beef in extra broth at the table, and cooks argue over which one counts as the real version.
  • Watch for Over-tenderizing. Kiwi and pineapple break down protein faster than pear does, and a heavy hand with either turns the beef mushy instead of tender.
Check before you go
  • Slice the beef paper-thin, or buy it pre-sliced: a thick cut stays chewy no matter how long it marinates.
  • Go easy on kiwi or pineapple if you use them instead of pear: too much turns the beef mushy, not tender.
  • Cook it in batches if the pan looks crowded: an overloaded pan steams the meat instead of searing it.
FAQ
Do I need Asian pear to make bulgogi?
No. A Bosc pear or a sweet apple works almost as well, and hansik.or.kr's own recipe already lists the pear as optional. Go light if you swap in kiwi or pineapple instead, since both tenderize faster than pear does.
Can I use a thick steak instead of thin-sliced beef?
Not really. Partially freeze whatever cut you have for about an hour, then slice it yourself across the grain to about 1/8 inch, since thickness is the one mistake the marinade can't fix afterward.

This recipe survives almost any substitution except a thick cut of beef and an overcrowded pan. Slice it thin, marinate it for the twenty minutes hansik.or.kr actually calls for, and the rest mostly cooks itself. Cut the vegetables before the beef goes anywhere near the marinade, because once it's ready, bulgogi doesn't wait long either.
