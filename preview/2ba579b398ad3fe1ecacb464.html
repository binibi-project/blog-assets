Seoul's Hangang Bus: What It Costs and What Changes Dec 1

Seoul's Han River water bus costs ₩3,000 flat, just added a Seoul Forest stop, and cuts sailings from 40 to 32 a day starting December 1.

Quick answer
  • One ride costs ₩3,000: no reservation needed, pay by transit card, same price whether you ride one stop or the whole line.
  • Weekends aren't ₩3,000 forever: a ₩6,000-8,000 weekend tier is under review, not active yet, and wouldn't arrive before spring 2027.
  • Plan around Dec 1: treat the ride as the first or last stop of your day, since sailings drop from 40 to 32 a day for winter.

Ever watched a city bus pull away and wished, just once, it had been a boat instead? Step onto the Hangang Bus at Yeouido as the sun drops behind the 63 Building, and the engine noise fades into something closer to a ferry crossing than a commute. The deck's glass runs floor to ceiling, so the bridges slide past at eye level instead of overhead, and for a few minutes the Han River does the work the subway usually does underground.

That quiet is still new. The service only turned one year old this past September, and it spent a chunk of its first winter running on half its route after a boat hit the riverbed. What's left by late 2026 is a system that works, prices that are still genuinely cheap, and a December schedule change worth knowing before you show up at a pier expecting the summer timetable.

What's 기후동행패스, the card everyone pays with?

Seoul's transit pass used to be called the Climate Card (기후동행카드); it was folded into a national program called "Everyone's Card" (모두의카드) and relaunched as 기후동행패스 on September 1, 2026. Old Climate Cards stopped working for new charges that day, so a card bought before September needs swapping at a subway station if it's still in a wallet somewhere.

🚤 What does a ride actually cost, and when does that change?

A single Hangang Bus ride is ₩3,000 (about $2.25 at ~₩1,333/$1) for an adult, ₩1,800 ($1.35) for a teen aged 13-18, and ₩1,100 ($0.83) for a child aged 6-12, the same price no matter which pier you board at or get off at. Seoul's own transport portal confirms this flat structure, and a July 2026 update repeats the identical numbers, so the fare hasn't moved since the route was rebuilt. Cash doesn't work at all; tap a transit card or buy a paper ticket from the pier kiosk if you're card-less.

That flat rate won't last on weekends forever. Seoul is reviewing a ₩6,000 base fare for weekends and holidays, rising to ₩8,000 if you book a reserved seat, against a ₩5,000 reserved weekday option; none of this is active yet, and city officials say it would need a public hearing and council review before taking effect, likely around spring 2027. A Seoul Research Institute survey of 2,107 riders found a median willingness to pay of ₩8,248 a ride, which is why the city picked ₩6,000 as a number most weekend riders would still accept.

What does a "reserved" (예약) fare actually buy you?

A reserved ticket guarantees you a seat on a specific sailing instead of showing up and hoping there's room. It costs more than the standby fare, and right now it's only part of the weekend plan under review, not something most riders pay today.

Ride often, and the per-trip math changes. The city's current transit page lists Hangang Bus under its "frequent rider" tier: once regular rides on ~₩3,000 regional transport add up, a monthly ₩100,000 flat-rate pass (₩90,000 for youth, seniors or larger families; ₩80,000 for low-income or three-child households) covers unlimited trips, replacing the old Climate Card's ₩5,000 Hangang-Bus-only add-on from last year. Cross more than 33 single rides in a month and that math flips: the Plus pass already costs less than paying ₩3,000 a trip.

📍 Where do the piers go, and what's new with Seoul Forest?

Eight piers make up the network from December 1, split into an East route through Jamsil and a West route through Magok, both meeting at Yeouido. The West route links Magok and Mangwon to Yeouido; the East route runs from Jamsil through Ttukseom, the newly added Seoul Forest stop, then on to Oksu and finally Apgujeong before reaching Yeouido, so Oksu and Apgujeong are both served on every sailing instead of alternating as before.

What's the difference between the East and West routes?

Seoul split what used to be one long Magok-to-Jamsil line into two shorter routes that both stop at Yeouido. Riding from Jamsil to Magok now means getting off at Yeouido and changing boats, with an 18-minute transfer wait built into the winter schedule.

Each pier earns its stop. Ttukseom's riverside park has a café and photo-friendly lawns right by the dock, Yeouido's own pier sits next to a Starbucks, and free shuttle buses link Magok and Jamsil piers to the nearest subway station every 15 to 30 minutes on weekdays; Apgujeong pier gets its own shuttle loop on the same schedule. Bring a bike if you want: every vessel carries 20 bike-rack spaces alongside wheelchair spaces and priority seating.

⏰ What changes on December 1, and is it still safe after last year's accident?

Starting December 1, 2026 through February 28, 2027, the Hangang Bus runs fewer, more predictably spaced trips: 32 sailings a day instead of 40, with both routes settling into a flat one-hour interval instead of anywhere from 20 minutes to 90 minutes apart. The East route's hours shrink to 11:00-21:04, down from 11:00-21:30, and the West route moves to 11:22-20:42, down from 11:20-21:39. If you're transferring between routes at Yeouido, budget the newly fixed 18-minute wait rather than hoping for a quick hop.

Build the Hangang Bus into your plan as the first or last thing you do that day, not a connection you're racing to catch. A one-hour interval means missing a sailing by five minutes costs you most of an hour, something the old, denser summer schedule mostly forgave.

There's a second, slower-moving change behind the schedule, and it ties straight back to safety. Starting January 2027, Seoul will add mooring posts at all seven original piers one at a time, Jamsil first, to cut down on pier sway and make docking more stable in strong wind or high water; each pier closes to boat service while its own upgrade is underway, with exact dates posted on the Hangang Bus site as they're scheduled.

That caution traces back to a real incident. On November 15, 2025, a vessel struck the riverbed after a route deviation tied to poor buoy visibility, and the city ran only the Magok-Yeouido half of the line for months while it investigated. The fix wasn't cosmetic: Seoul surveyed and dredged the 8.9km channel near Hannam Bridge where the grounding happened, installed an alarm that sounds the moment a boat drifts off its charted course, and swapped the accident site's buoys for taller ones, up from 1.4m to 4.5m.

Full-route service came back on March 1, 2026. Since then, Seoul reports zero accidents, a 99.3% on-time rate, and 97.3% rider satisfaction from more than 3,500 people surveyed, and ridership tells its own story about the before and after.

About 104,500 people rode during the rocky partial-service months; nearly five times as many, 498,795, rode in the roughly six months after the full route came back, 83% of the service's entire first-year total. Figures from Seoul city, reported by The Fact, checked 2026-11-20

My take Reading "boat hits riverbed" in a headline is enough to make anyone cross a thing off their list, and that reaction makes sense before you look at what actually changed afterward. If I were visiting with one evening free, I'd book the Hangang Bus for the view, not for getting somewhere on time, since a one-hour interval makes it a poor substitute for the subway if you're actually trying to catch a flight or a dinner reservation. Ride it the way you'd ride a cruise you're not paying cruise prices for, and the accident history stops being the thing that decides your evening.

FAQ
Can I bring my e-scooter or e-bike along for the ride?
No. Electric personal mobility devices, e-scooters and e-bikes included, are banned from the vessel outright, a rule easy to miss since an ordinary pedal bike is explicitly welcome in the rack spaces. Roll up on an e-scooter and expect to be turned away at the ramp, not just asked to fold it up.
Why is there a numbered ticket system, and do I need one?
Grab a printed queue number from the kiosk the moment you reach the dock, because Saturday afternoons and the evening commute rush fill each departure in ticket order, not by who happens to be standing nearest the ramp. Skip that step, and the next boat can pull out three-quarters empty while people who showed up after you board ahead of you, purely because they're holding a lower number.

The Hangang Bus earned its first year the hard way, and what's running now is a steadier, smaller, more honestly scheduled version of the thing that launched with a lot of noise in 2025. Book around the hourly winter interval, bring a transit card, and treat the ride itself as the destination.
