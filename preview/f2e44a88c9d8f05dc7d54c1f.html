Kimjang: What a Family Kimchi Day Actually Costs

What a family's kimjang day costs this year, why UNESCO recognized it, and where to join a real kimchi-making festival this November.

A family's kimjang starts with more cabbage than any one kitchen can hold: crates of it stacked past the doorway, sleeves pushed up, a sheet of plastic spread across whatever yard or parking lot the group can borrow for the day. It used to take a village. These days it more often takes a folding table, three generations, and a Saturday nobody quite volunteered for but everyone shows up to anyway.

The result sits in jars that get a household through winter, and the reason a whole street used to smell like garlic and chili by dusk.

What is kimjang (김장)?

The late-autumn practice of making a household's entire winter supply of kimchi in one large batch, usually with family, neighbors or a crew of helpers, rather than a few heads at a time. The word covers both the day itself and the kimchi that comes out of it.

🥬 Why does a family spend a whole day on this?

Kimjang exists because a Korean winter used to leave almost nothing fresh to cook with, and the practice still runs on that same yearly rhythm. UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage listing, which added kimjang in 2013, describes a calendar that starts in spring, when households buy shrimp, anchovy and other seafood to salt for the paste, moves through summer for sea salt and late summer for drying and grinding red chili peppers, and finishes in late autumn, when "communities collectively make and share large quantities of kimchi".

What is jeotgal (젓갈)?

Salted, fermented seafood, usually anchovy or tiny shrimp, stirred into the chili paste. It's what gives kimjang kimchi its savory depth and helps the fermentation along, and every family has an opinion on how much to use.

UNESCO's listing calls the day "an excellent opportunity for strengthening family cooperation" and a reminder that communities need to live in step with the seasons, not against them. The knowledge itself usually moves down a single line: from a mother-in-law to her newly married daughter-in-law, which is why the exact ratio of chili to salt to seafood counts as real family heritage, not a recipe anyone writes down.

💰 What does a family's kimjang actually cost this year?

A four-person household's kimjang ran ₩338,000 (about $252) at a traditional market and ₩404,000 (about $302) at a large mart last year, both at ~₩1,340/$1, for the benchmark quantity Korea's food-trade agency uses in its annual survey: 20 heads of cabbage for a family of four, plus radish, chili powder, garlic, ginger, onion and salted seafood. That's ₩7,000 more than the traditional-market price the year before and ₩4,850 more at the mart, even though cabbage itself rose about 20% year over year; radish and salt got cheaper enough to hold the total's growth to roughly 2%.

This year's own survey won't run until closer to November, but the supply picture points down, not up. Total 2026 cabbage production is forecast at roughly 2.1 million tons, up 9.5% from 2025 and 5.9% above the normal-year average, which is the kind of number that usually shows up as a calmer cabbage stall by the time kimjang season opens. Treat last year's ₩338,000-404,000 as the floor to budget from, not a guarantee.

🎪 Can a visitor actually join a kimjang day?

Yes, and this November gives you a confirmed date: the Goesan Kimjang Festival runs November 5-8, 2026, with a main competition on November 7 from 13:00 to 16:00 at the Goesan Organic Expo Plaza. Goesan, a county in North Chungcheong Province, built the event around its own cabbage and chili peppers, and this year's edition is expanding its "experience-type" programs for families, including a drive-through kimchi-making option that doesn't require sitting down at a table for hours.

Seoul runs its own edition most years too. Last year's Korea Kimjang Grand Festival, organized by the food-trade agency aT with the city government and the Korea Kimchi Association, ran November 22, 2025 from 10:00 to 18:00 at the aT Center in Yangjae-dong, with kimjang ingredients discounted up to 50% and about 100 people, chosen through referrals from multicultural-family centers and honorary-citizen programs, making kimchi alongside professional kimchi masters. This year's Seoul dates hadn't been posted as of this writing, so Goesan is the one date on the calendar you can actually plan around right now.

My take Twenty heads of cabbage in one weekend sounds less like a hobby and more like a logistics problem, and if that's the thing stopping you, it should be. You don't need to run your own kimjang to understand why Koreans still do it. A festival gets you the smell, the technique and a jar to take home, without owing a favor back next spring the way a real family crew does. If you're only in the country for a few days in November, the festival is the door that's actually open to you.

FAQ
Can I just show up and get one of the hands-on kimchi-making spots?
Unlikely, no. A nominated roster filled those free seats last time, so a cold walk-in rarely gets picked. Spend your won at the surrounding stalls instead; nobody there checks who sent you.
Is Goesan actually easier to join than Seoul's?
Looks that way. This year's county event skips the nomination step and opens walk-up stations for visiting households, cars included. No won figure is public for those yet, so bring extra cash instead of a locked number.

None of this is really about getting 20 heads of cabbage exactly right. If you can't make it to a field or a family's yard, the Goesan festival on November 5-8 is the one date this year you can actually put on a calendar, and it comes with the market, the smell and a jar of the real thing at the end.
