Buying Medicine at a Korean Convenience Store at 2 A.M.

Korean convenience stores can legally sell only 11 approved medicines, a list unchanged since 2012, with expansion talks due in late 2026.

Quick answer
  • Only 11 medicines qualify: fever and pain relievers, one cold remedy, two digestive aids and two pain patches, sold only at registered 24-hour convenience stores.
  • It's not negotiable at the counter: clerks can ring up what's on the shelf, not recommend a dose or a different drug, since the law only permits those specific products.
  • Know your fallback: save a duty-pharmacy finder or the local emergency line before you need it, since the convenience-store shelf stops well short of anything serious.

Midnight in a part of Seoul you don't know yet, and your throat feels like you swallowed sand. Every pharmacy you pass has its shutter down, its green cross dark, but half a block up a convenience-store sign is still burning white and orange like nothing here ever really closes. The street itself has gone quiet, just the hum of a vending machine and a delivery scooter idling at the curb, and that one glowing storefront is doing all the work of telling you the city hasn't actually shut down for the night. Walk in, and the medicine really is there, just not much of it: a handful of boxes behind the register, the same handful you'll see in every branch from Busan to Seoul.

That short shelf isn't an accident of stock. It's the exact outline of a decision Korea's health ministry made in 2012 and hasn't revisited since, and it's a narrower list than most visitors expect walking in from a country with an entire drugstore aisle to choose from.

💊 What's Actually on the Shelf

Korean law lets convenience stores sell only 11 non-prescription medicines, all drawn from a list of 13 that the health ministry fixed back in 2012 and never updated. The Korea JoongAng Daily puts the current count at 11 out of those original 13. The original 13 split into four categories, five of them for fever and pain alone.

What is 안전상비의약품 (anjeon sangbi uiyakpum)?

Literally "safety stock medicine." A legal category created by a 2012 amendment to Korea's Pharmaceutical Affairs Act, letting a short, fixed list of harmless over-the-counter drugs be sold outside a pharmacy, at stores that register for it.

Two of the original 13 are gone in practice, not by law. Children's Tylenol 80mg tablets and the 160mg adult tablet quietly disappeared after their manufacturer closed the factory that made them and moved production overseas, and the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety approved the withdrawal on March 21, 2022. Nobody replaced them, so the list that's supposed to have 13 options has effectively run on 11 for years.

What is a 파스 (pasta)?

A medicated adhesive patch, closer to a stronger Salonpas than a bandage. You stick it over a sore muscle or joint, and two of them, Cheil Cool Pharp and Shinshin Pasta Arex, made the original 13-item list.

🏪 Which Stores Sell It, and What Breaks the Law

Only a store open 24 hours a day, every day of the year, and registered with its local district office can legally sell these medicines, and selling them anywhere else risks up to three years in prison or a ₩10 million fine, about $7,495 at today's rate. The registration itself is filed with the mayor, county head or district head, not the health ministry, which is part of why it's the store, not a wholesaler, that carries the legal risk. In practice that means four familiar chains: GS25, CU, 7-Eleven and E-mart24, the same four that raised their shared Tylenol price from ₩3,100 to ₩3,600 for an 8-tablet pack on March 1, 2023.

The market behind that short shelf is bigger than it looks. GS25 alone reports that 57.2% of its medicine sales happen between 6pm and 6am, well outside normal pharmacy hours, and in 2022 the whole category supplied convenience stores with ₩53.7 billion worth of product, up 21.2% from the year before.

Pancol-A alone passed ₩12.5 billion in supply value that year, its first time over ₩10 billion and an 81% jump, while Tylenol itself held above ₩20 billion for a second straight year. One pain patch, Shinshin Pasta Arex, still cleared ₩4.3 billion on its own.

🌙 When the Convenience-Store Shelf Isn't Enough

If the 11-item shelf doesn't cover what's actually wrong, the next move is a duty pharmacy, not a guess. Korean cities designate pharmacies to stay open late or on holidays specifically to fill that gap; Busan's own city health page lists them running roughly 22:00 to 01:00, with some open 21:00 to 24:00. A night, weekend or holiday visit also carries a 30% surcharge on the dispensing fee, a cost worth knowing before you're standing at the counter.

What's a duty pharmacy?

A pharmacy rostered to stay open on a night, weekend or holiday when most others are shut, so a city always has somewhere for a resident to fill a prescription or ask a pharmacist a question. Busan calls it a late-night (심야) or holiday-duty pharmacy, and the roster changes by date, so you check it rather than guess it.

Busan's own page tells residents to double-check at the national emergency medical portal or call 119 before heading out, since a listed pharmacy can still be wrong on a given night. That ten-second check is cheaper than a wasted walk at 1 a.m.

My take Getting sick while away from home is already its own small misery, before a shelf of boxes that don't read in English gets added on top. If I were the one standing there at 2 a.m., I'd trust the short list more than it looks like it deserves: acetaminophen, a cold remedy and a pain patch quietly cover most of what actually wakes a traveler up at night. If I were unsure which box to grab, I'd skip the guessing and call a duty pharmacy instead, since a clerk can ring up a box but can't weigh a symptom, and that line is exactly why the law drew the shelf this narrow in the first place.
Go deeper
  • If you have one more hour before you decide something's serious, call ahead to a duty pharmacy rather than walking to one on a hunch; Busan's own guidance says a listing can be wrong on the night you need it.
  • What locals argue about is whether 11 items is still enough. A 2018 push to widen the list stalled after the Korean Pharmacists Association pushed back, and no committee has met on it since, even though a 2023 survey of 1,000 people found 71.5% had already bought medicine at a convenience store and 62.1% wanted more choice.
  • Watch for the second half of 2026: the Ministry of Health and Welfare has said it plans formal talks on raising the cap from 11 toward the legal ceiling of 20, with anti-diarrheal medicine, burn ointment and artificial tears named as items the law already allows but hasn't added.
FAQ
Can I just ask the clerk which one to take?
Ask for a brand name, not a symptom. A convenience-store worker rings up whatever you point to, since the whole registration scheme was built so nobody with pharmacology training has to staff the counter. Learning 해열제 (fever reducer) versus 소화제 (digestive aid) before you walk in gets you further than describing how rotten you feel once you're already there.
What if the box is entirely in Korean and I can't read it?
Look for 타이레놀 (Tylenol) printed on the box; it's the one name most clerks will recognize even without English, and the active ingredient, acetaminophen, is usually printed in Roman letters on the back panel. Screenshot the Korean name on your phone before you go so you can hold it up instead of trying to pronounce it under a fluorescent light at midnight.

Korea built a narrow, deliberate gap between "nothing" and "a pharmacist," and 11 items is exactly as far as that gap goes for now. Carry the short list in your head, save a duty-pharmacy number before you need one, and let 2026's committee talks decide whether that shelf ever gets any longer.
