A 2026 law change let Google export Korea's map data, but Google Maps still can't give walking or driving directions. What locals use instead.

Landing at Incheon Airport, the instinct is automatic: pull out your phone, open Google Maps, and type in your hotel. The blue dot appears. The pin drops.

Then nothing. No route line curving through the city, no walk time, no voice telling you where to turn next. For a visitor who has used Google Maps everywhere else on earth, Seoul is the one place it quietly stops working.

It isn't a bug. It's a law, and even after that law changed, the app still hasn't caught up.

🔒 Why Google Maps Still Can't Get You There

A security law, not a technical limit, is why Google Maps has no walking or driving directions in Korea. Open the app in Seoul and search a subway station: the pin shows up, but tap "Directions" and it can only route you by public transit, never on foot or by car.

What is Korea's map-export law?

Article 16 of the Geospatial Information Management Act bars state-surveyed map data at a scale of 1:5,000 or finer from leaving the country without permission from the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure and Transport. The government's stated reason is national security: unblurred satellite imagery of military sites, it argues, would be a gift to North Korea.

The Korea Herald traces the fight back to 2007, when Google first asked for the data and was turned down. A second request in 2016 was rejected too, citing national-security concerns. Google tried a third time in February 2025; that request was deferred three separate times before a decision finally came.

Nineteen years passed between the first request and the approval. An interagency review body voted it through at the National Geographic Information Institute, not a single minister's signature.

On paper, February 27, 2026 reads like a clean win for Google. In practice, Korea's review body attached five conditions: coordinates for Korean territory have to be stripped from Google's global map and Earth products; satellite and Street View imagery must obscure military and other sensitive sites; and contour data is excluded from export entirely. Raw data also has to be processed on domestic servers by a local partner, with only government-reviewed base-map and road data allowed to leave, and Google has to build a "red button" shutoff for emergencies while keeping a dedicated map officer stationed in Korea.

One mapping-industry figure summed up the local skepticism: "Korea invests 150 billion won every year through the National Geographic Information Institute in producing precision maps. The idea of handing that over to a foreign company for free — with no compensation — makes no sense," Wi Gwang-jae, an official at a mapping and surveying firm, told KBS. Google's own VP for government affairs, Cris Turner, said the company "sincerely welcomes" the decision.

None of that has turned into a working map yet. Google confirmed on August 27, 2026 that its plan is to bring "full-functioning Google Maps capabilities to Korea, which includes turn-by-turn directions," and that it had hired a Strategic Partnerships Development Manager to help deliver it, but gave no date. Local apps Naver Map and Kakao Map already have up to three times as many users in Korea as Google's own app.

🗺️ Naver Map vs. Kakao Map: Which One Actually Gets You There

Both already do what Google Maps can't: full turn-by-turn directions for walking and driving, plus transit. Naver Map runs an English-language interface, real-time traffic and Street View; Kakao Map handles driving and walking directions, plus biking and public transit, and is generally seen as stronger for real-time bus arrivals.

Neither app is a straight upgrade over the other. Pick Naver for a walking-heavy trip through subway exits, Kakao if you're chasing buses more than trains.

At a station with ten exits, "take the subway to Gangnam" is only half the trick. You still have to guess which stairwell puts you on the right side of the street. Think of a Korean map app's exit numbers as a pin inside the pin: it doesn't just say "Gangnam Station," it tells you which exit to climb and which direction to walk once you're up top. That's the difference between circling a six-lane intersection twice and walking straight to your door.

Naver Map's interface, search filters and customer reviews now run in English too, with no separate download required. The company added AI-translated reviews in English, plus Chinese and Japanese, along with translated search filters for cuisine type and dining atmosphere; the language switches automatically to match your phone's own settings, or you can set it by hand inside the app.

What is Papago?

Naver's own machine-translation tool. Inside Naver Map, it's what turns a Korean customer review into English, Chinese or Japanese on the spot, so you can read what a local actually said about a restaurant before you sit down.

It's working. The average monthly number of daily active users on Naver's foreign-language map service rose 26.4% in September 2024 from the same month a year earlier. "We now offer most of the information foreign users need to explore new locations," said Choi Seung-rak, who heads Naver Map's services. Small cafés and newer spots can still show up under Korean-only names, so if a search comes up empty, pasting the Korean name usually finds it.

🚗 If You're Driving: Waze and T map Still Work

Google Maps' block is on turn-by-turn navigation, not on finding a place, and if you're behind the wheel, two other apps already route you there. Waze functions normally for driving navigation in Korea, sidestepping the restriction that limits Google Maps.

What is T map?

SK Telecom's own navigation app, and the one Korean drivers already default to. It pulls real-time traffic conditions from a pool of more than 20 million users, which is why taxi and delivery drivers tend to trust it over an international app.

My take If you've never had a map app fail you before, trusting Google Maps the moment you land is a completely reasonable instinct. It has worked everywhere else you've traveled. I'd swap habits before the trip, not during it: download Naver Map or Kakao Map at home, set the language to English, and keep Google Maps around only for searching place names and reading star ratings. That habit is worth keeping even after Google's own app catches up, since the fix could land quietly and without warning. Ten minutes setting up Naver Map before you fly is cheaper than twenty spent standing at an intersection guessing which exit is yours.

FAQ
How will I know if the Google app has actually caught up by the time I'm there?
Pull up two real addresses on opposite sides of town and ask the app for the way across, say Itaewon to Konkuk University. A lone bus-and-train glyph on the results screen tells you nothing has moved since this was written. A small steering-wheel symbol paired with a pedestrian figure is the sign that the ban has finally lifted. Until that second symbol shows up on your own screen, lean on Naver Map or Kakao Map to actually get you there instead, the way a commuter with no memory of the old restriction already does every morning.
I'm renting a car. Should I pick Waze or the local option?
Stick with Waze inside Seoul or Busan proper; its point-to-point routing already functions across the country. Heading toward a coastal road or a smaller county instead, switch over to T map: SK Telecom draws its live congestion picture from more than twenty million drivers already on the service, so a lane closure near a toll gate tends to reroute you faster than an outside app can manage.

For getting around on this trip, put Naver Map or Kakao Map on your phone before you land, and leave Google Maps for searching place names and reviews only. Check Google's own app again once you're here. If the turn-by-turn feature has finally shipped, it'll show up as an option on a route you already know.
