A pot of gamjatang hits the table already fighting you: red broth still rolling, a knot of pork spine breaking the surface, perilla leaves going soft at the edges before you've even picked up your spoon. Someone across the table always reaches for the ladle first, serving the broth before the meat, the way you'd pour tea for a guest before yourself. If you've ever watched a Korean drama scene where a family crowds around one steaming pot after a hard day, this is the dish doing the work, three very different versions of it in three very different parts of the city.

What is gamjatang (감자탕)?

A spicy, red-broth stew built on pork spine bones, simmered for hours with potato, napa cabbage or ugeoji (dried radish or cabbage greens), perilla leaves and perilla seed powder. The name's first syllable, "gamja," means potato, though some food historians argue it instead points to an old dialect word for the pork neckbone itself.

Treat these as three separate trips, not one route: Taejo Gamjaguk sits inside a market near Seongshin Women's University in the north, Somunnan Seongsu Gamjatang works a 24-hour counter by the Seongsu Station industrial blocks, and Bangadari Gamjaguk keeps one pot going in a quiet Jongno backstreet.

🏺 Taejo Gamjaguk: the 1958 shop that became a supermarket product

Taejo Gamjaguk (태조감자국) has run inside Seongbuk-gu's Donam Market since 1958, now in its third generation, and a local food roundup names it as a certified 백년가게. The 백년가게 (Hundred-Year Store) program is run jointly by Korea's Ministry of SMEs and Startups and the Korea SMEs and Startups Agency, certifying small businesses that have kept the same core trade for 30 years or more. The shop is at 서울 성북구 보문로34길 43, about 230m from Seongshin Women's University Station, a transfer point between Line 4 and the Ui Sinseol Line, though the exact exit isn't confirmed.

What is a 백년가게 (baeknyeon-gage)?

Literally "hundred-year store," a certification from Korea's Ministry of SMEs and Startups for small businesses that have run the same trade for at least 30 years. It's a badge for longevity and consistency, not a food-quality award by itself.

Its standing was strong enough that E-Mart's Peacock private-label line turned the shop's recipe into a packaged product: a 2021 report names "태조 감자국" among eight 백년가게 items repackaged as Peacock goods.

It's open daily 10:00-23:00 on its current Google listing, though an older write-up lists a 15:00-16:30 break; call 02-926-7008 if an afternoon visit is tight.

Order by naming one of its four sizes rather than a bone count, and think of the names as a small-to-jumbo ladder rather than four separate dishes: 좋타 (jota) is the bottom rung at ₩23,000 ($17.36), then 최고다 (choegoda) at ₩28,000 ($21.13), 무진장 (mujinjang) at ₩30,000 ($22.64), and 혹시나 (hoksina) at ₩38,000 ($28.67) at the top. Climb all the way up and you've paid ₩15,000 ($11.32) more for more bones and broth, not a different meal.

🏙️ Is Somunnan Seongsu Gamjatang worth the wait at 2 a.m.?

Yes, if the hour doesn't matter to you: the Seongsu shop has run 24 hours a day since it opened in 1983, and it's logged more than 6,000 Google ratings, the most of any gamjatang spot checked for this guide. It's at 서울 성동구 연무장길 45, about 240m from Seongsu Station Exit 1, a Line 2 stop, per Seoul Metropolitan Government's own city news desk, reporting in 2017.

The pork spine itself is a mix the shop states as 80% domestic pork and 20% Danish pork. A queue is normal at any meal-time hour here, so a 2017 city writer's advice still holds: go a little before the lunch or dinner rush, not during it.

A solo lunch set, 감자국식사, runs ₩12,000 ($9.06), while the smallest shared pot, 감자탕 소, is ₩32,000 ($24.15). A larger "ugeoji gomppaegi" bowl with extra dried greens runs ₩14,000 ($10.57). Add hand-torn sujebi dough noodles or ramyeon to the leftover broth once the meat's gone.

What is ugeoji (우거지)?

The outer, tougher leaves of napa cabbage or dried radish greens, boiled soft and added to a stew for bulk and a slightly bitter edge. A "곱빼기" (gomppaegi) order means a double portion of it.

🥘 Bangadari Gamjaguk: one pot, one dish, gone by mid-afternoon

Bangadari Gamjaguk (방아다리감자국) sells exactly one dish, a lighter gamjaguk in a soybean-paste (된장) broth rather than the spicier red style, and it closes the moment the morning's pot is empty. It's at 서울 종로구 종로39길 50, about 305m from Jongno 5(o)-ga Station, a Line 1 stop, though the exact exit isn't confirmed.

Its own Google listing shows it open Monday-Friday 11:00-16:30 and closed Saturday and Sunday, while an older write-up lists it as open daily except Sunday; the two disagree on Saturdays, so call 02-747-8741 before a weekend trip.

There's nothing to order by name beyond asking for a bowl, 감자국 하나 주세요 (gamjaguk hana juseyo), at ₩10,000 ($7.55). Since it's a single-pot kitchen with no backup batch, arriving right at opening is the only guarantee against a sold-out sign.

On paper, Bangadari Gamjaguk looks like the budget pick at ₩10,000 ($7.55), but it's also the only one of the three that can run out of food before dinner, so the lowest price comes with the narrowest window to actually get it.

My take A first gamjatang order can feel like a dare: a pile of bones, a spoon, and no clear plan for your hands. If I were choosing for a solo weeknight dinner with no one to split a pot with, I'd skip the shared sizes entirely and head to Bangadari Gamjaguk or order Taejo Gamjaguk's smallest "좋타" bowl, since both are built for one person without the leftover-pot math. If I had a group and nowhere else to be at 2 a.m., Somunnan Seongsu's 24-hour counter solves a problem the other two can't: it's open when the rest of the city's kitchens are closed.

FAQ
Eating alone, which of the three actually makes sense to walk into?
Only partly. The overnight pick in Seongsu keeps its cheapest listed size big enough for a group, not one. Luckily, the remaining pair each keep a bowl built for exactly one eater on their boards.
None of these post an English menu — how do I actually order?
Save a screenshot of the Korean name before you lose cell signal underground, then point at your phone instead of trusting a translation app's guess at an odd-sounding dish name, since a literal translation won't make sense to the staff anyway. Where there's only one thing on the menu, a nod and "하나 주세요" (hana juseyo, "one, please") gets you fed.

If you only have time for one of these, go for whichever fits your clock: Bangadari Gamjaguk before 14:00 on a weekday, or Somunnan Seongsu at any hour that actually works for you. Call ahead to Taejo Gamjaguk if the afternoon break-time question matters to your schedule.
