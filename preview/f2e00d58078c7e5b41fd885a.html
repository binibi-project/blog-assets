Korea's Café Culture: Too Many Cafes, Too Few Seats

Why Korea has 95,000+ cafes, what 카공족 means, why Starbucks banned desktops in August 2025, and what a café or study-cafe seat actually costs.

Rain on the window, a laptop glow, and a paper cup gone lukewarm two hours ago: that's the default photo of a Korean café on any given weekday afternoon. A visitor sees a cozy place to warm up. A Korean sitting two tables over sees an unpaid office, one chair in a silent, unofficial co-working space that nobody built on purpose.

☕ Why does Korea have so many cafes?

Korea had 95,337 registered coffee and beverage shops in the first quarter of 2025, and that number had never shrunk before. National Tax Service data put the drop at 743 shops from the same quarter a year earlier, the first quarterly decline since the agency started tracking the figure in 2018. That's not a shortage; it's the opposite. A strip of three or four storefronts in Seoul regularly holds two or three cafes competing for the same foot traffic, which is also why one in a given stretch is often empty while another has a line out the door.

The demand side explains the glut. South Korea's per capita coffee consumption reached 416 cups in 2024, the highest of any country in Asia, according to a U.S. Department of Agriculture brief on the Korean market. That's roughly a cup a day for every adult, child, and grandparent in the country, which is why a block without a café feels like a block that's missing something.

The 2025 dip in shop numbers doesn't mean Koreans are drinking less. It means rent and thin margins finally caught up with the oversupply.

What does 카공족 (ka-gong-jok) mean?

Literally "café-study tribe": people who buy one drink and use a café table as a workspace or study desk for hours. It's not a slur, just a label for a very visible habit in a country where 416 cups of coffee a year is normal.

💻 What is 카공족, and why did Starbucks finally say no?

Starbucks Korea banned personal desktop computers, printers, power strips, and table partitions at every one of its stores nationwide starting August 7, 2025. The company said customer complaints about "진상 카공족," nuisance-level long-stay café-studiers, had been piling up, and staff now give a verbal warning to anyone who sets up full office gear, leaves belongings on a table for a long stretch, or occupies a multi-person table alone. One community post the article cites described someone holding a seat for three hours with a desktop monitor and a power strip running to it.

Not every chain agrees that 카공족 are the problem. Twosome Place installed single-person seats in the stores cafe-studiers use most, and Mega Coffee is building a dedicated studying zone into its larger locations. Incheon National University consumer-studies professor Lee Young-ae pointed to why the two chains picked the opposite move from Starbucks: the café market is so crowded and saturated that standing out, even by welcoming the customers a rival just restricted, can build loyalty.

What's a "진상" customer?

진상 (jinsang) describes a customer whose demands or behavior cross from inconvenient into genuinely disruptive for staff and other guests. It's the word Korean coverage used for the complaints that pushed Starbucks to act, not for café-studiers as a whole.

💸 What does a seat actually cost — café vs. study cafe?

A ₩4,500 Americano at Starbucks buys you a drink, not a reserved desk, and the gap between what people pay and what they think is fair is wider than you'd guess. The Korea Consumer Agency's most recent survey found Coffee Bean the priciest of the major chains at ₩5,000, with Starbucks, Twosome Place, Hollys, Pascucci, Angel-in-us, Droptop, and Caffe Bene all clustered at ₩4,500, while the low-cost chains sat near ₩1,500.

On paper the gap looks like brand snobbery, but the agency's own number tells a different story: shoppers surveyed called ₩2,635 a fair price for an Americano, yet the average they actually paid came to ₩3,001, 13.9% above what they considered reasonable. Compose Coffee's jump to ₩1,800 in February 2025, its first increase since launching in 2014, was framed around 48-year-high bean costs, not rent for your seat. Either way, you're paying more than you think the coffee is worth, and a chunk of that gap is the chair.

A study cafe (스터디카페) prices the seat honestly instead of hiding it in the cup. Most are unmanned, kiosk-run operations where a two-hour block costs around ₩4,000 (about $2.98) and a full day runs about ₩10,000 (about $7.46), with strong Wi-Fi, outlets at every desk, and no music to argue with your focus. One Korea University student told the paper she spends more than five hours a day there during exam season, precisely because nobody around her is chatting or scrolling. If you need more than an hour of table time, a study cafe is the honest trade: you pay for the seat up front instead of hoping nobody minds.

What is a 스터디카페 (study cafe)?

A pay-by-the-hour workspace, usually unmanned with kiosk check-in, built for quiet solo study: cubicles, strong Wi-Fi, and sometimes printers or blankets. Unlike a regular café, the clock, not the coffee, is what you're buying.

My take If you've ever hesitated before opening a laptop in a café, worried you're the person everyone's quietly annoyed with, that worry is reasonable now in a way it wasn't two years ago. I'd read the room the chain gives you rather than guess: a Starbucks after August 2025 wants your laptop closed within reason, while a Twosome Place with single-seats or a Mega Coffee studying zone is telling you the opposite. If I needed more than an hour, I'd skip the guilt entirely and pay the ₩4,000 for two hours at a study cafe. It's less charming, but nobody there is deciding whether you've overstayed your welcome.

FAQ
Will every Korean café ban laptops and power strips?
No, and the newest moves actually point opposite ways at once, so treat it more like local weather than a courtroom ruling. A printer plugged into a wall outlet got one company in trouble in August; walk into a different storefront a week later and an employee might hand you a power adapter without blinking. Watch the floor plan on your way in: cubicles and solo desks are an invitation, a packed row of four-person tables usually isn't.
Is it actually rude to buy one coffee and stay for three hours?
Manners here turn out to track the clock on the wall more than any rulebook. A slow Tuesday morning forgives almost anyone lingering; order the same drink at 12:30 on a Friday and you're competing with office workers on a real lunch break who need that exact chair back. Timing your visit, not just picking the right chain, is the variable nobody mentions.

A chain Americano was never really about the coffee, and once you see the seat as the thing you're renting, the rest of this sorts itself out. Pick a study cafe for a real work session, and save the chain cafes for a drink you'll actually finish before you leave.
