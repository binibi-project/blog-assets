Jjimjilbang Etiquette: What First-Timers Get Wrong

Korea's jjimjilbangs split into a nude bath floor and a co-ed common floor in matching pajamas. What that split actually means for a first visit.

One key, two dress codes, and for the first ten minutes, no clothes at all. That's the order of operations at a Korean jjimjilbang, and it's the part that trips up almost every first-timer before the bath-floor door even swings shut. Past it, the room is loud with running water and completely indifferent to anyone's body, the kind of ordinary Koreans grow up inside and visitors usually need one full visit to stop flinching at. Once you know which floor asks for which rules, the rest of the night runs itself.

What is jjimjilbok (찜질복)?

The cotton T-shirt and shorts handed over at the front desk, color-coded by gender, worn only in the co-ed common areas. Nobody wears it, or anything else, on the bath floor.

🛁 What's actually different between the two floors?

The bath floor is nude and split by gender by regulation; the common floor is co-ed, and everyone there is dressed in the uniform the front desk hands over. Korea's public health rules spell this out directly: changing rooms and bath rooms "must be operated separately by gender". Think of the whole building as a gym locker room with a second, mixed floor stacked on top: downstairs stays single-sex and bare, upstairs is where everyone mingles in matching pajamas.

At the shoe locker near the entrance you trade your shoes for a key, which usually doubles as your clothing locker key and your running tab for snacks. Past the second locker, the rule that actually matters is simple: shower at the stool stations before any tub, every time. A shared soak only works because everyone arrives clean first.

There's one more line that's been moving: the age at which a child can no longer join the bathing side with a parent of the opposite sex. In January 2022, the Ministry of Health and Welfare proposed tightening it further, from age 5 to age 4. Travel with a four-year-old and plan for a solo bath, not a shared one.

🔥 Why do people lie on a hot floor for fun?

The heat isn't a modern spa trend — Korea has been treating it as medicine since the 1400s. The earliest record of a Korean kiln sauna, then called hanjeungso, appears in the Annals of King Sejong in the 15th century, when Buddhist monks ran state-supported sauna kilns for medicinal use, and by 1429 those kilns were already built separately for men and women.

What is ondol (온돌)?

Korea's traditional underfloor heating, originally wood-fired and still the basis for heated apartment floors today. Lying directly on a warm floor to relax or recover from illness is a habit that goes back to the Joseon dynasty.

The jjimjilbang most visitors recognize today is newer, and a recent CNN feature on Korea's bathhouse culture traces its start to one city. As Korea urbanized and apartment blocks with central heating replaced ondol-floored houses, a group of women in Busan recreated that floor heat for themselves inside a shared building — and the modern jjimjilbang was born. The government caught up in July 1995, legally recognizing jjimjilbangs and setting guidelines for how they operate. Lee In-hye, author of History of Bathing, puts the appeal simply: "having a scrub and enjoying the sauna and jjimjilbang is one of the best ways to experience the essence of Korean bathing culture".

Every jjimjilbang runs a snack counter on the common floor, and two orders show up again and again: kiln-roasted eggs, cooked right against the hot stone, and a cold bowl of sikhye, a sweet rice punch Koreans reach for once the sauna heat gets to be too much.

💸 What does an afternoon cost, and does a tattoo change that?

A day pass runs well under what a day spa charges, and a visible tattoo won't automatically stop you at the door either. At Spa Land inside Shinsegae's Centum City store in Busan, entry costs ₩20,000, about $14, for a building with 22 pools at different temperatures. The facility's manager, Choi Jae-kwang, says the goal is to be "the premier K-spa destination," and on a typical day locals and foreign visitors split the crowd about 50-50.

Dragon Hill Spa, next to Yongsan Station, is the other end of the spectrum: heated floors around 36.5°C, sauna kilns running 50°C or hotter, and a stay capped around 12 hours before you're expected to head out. Its marketing team calls it "a great place to experience our traditional ways," pulling in more than 4,000 people on a winter weekend. A quiet Tuesday afternoon looks nothing like that number.

If the basic soak isn't enough, the scrub counter is the usual add-on: a staff member in matching underwear scrubs dead skin off with a rough mitt while you lie on a plastic mat, a service most jjimjilbang list separately from the entry fee. Book it at the counter on the way in, not after you've already settled on the heated floor, since walk-up slots fill first on busy afternoons.

A visible tattoo is a different kind of gamble, since saunas are a category a 2022 Korea Herald survey flags as among the least tattoo-friendly in Korea. That reporting found a sports center in Seoul's Seodaemun-gu bans tattoos outright at its pool and gym after complaints from "parents of young children and the elderly members," and the Westin Josun Seoul requires visible tattoos be covered before guests can use its indoor pool. Roughly 13 million Koreans, about one in four, have at least one tattoo, and a Gallup Korea poll of 1,002 adults found 51% support easing those laws against 40% opposed.

No single national rule decides this for jjimjilbang specifically, so the posted sign at that day's front desk is the only answer that actually applies to you.

My take The nudity is the part every guide warns you about, and it's also the part that stops mattering fastest. Nobody on that floor is looking at you, because they're all doing the same thing you are. If I were walking in for the first time, I'd pick a slow weekday afternoon over a Saturday night, head straight for the shower stool before the tub, and save the scrub service for a second visit once the layout stops feeling foreign. The uniform floor upstairs is the easy half of the night; give the bath floor downstairs one visit before you decide how you feel about it.

FAQ
Can I just sleep here instead of booking a hotel?
Yes, and that's standard practice, not a workaround: Korea's common jjimjilbang floors are built with bunk beds and sleeping mats for exactly this. Don't expect privacy, though. It's an open hall of strangers doing the same thing, so an eye mask and earplugs do more good than a pillow preference.
Will staff make me cover a tattoo?
Neither spot named earlier shows up in that news coverage of places turning people away over ink; a gym, a hotel pool and a kids' cafe did. Ownership can still post a new house rule overnight, so that's a loose signal rather than a promise. If you're carrying a large piece, ask the front desk in person before you've paid and changed, not after.

The split that confuses every first-timer stops being confusing after about one visit: bare and single-sex on the lower floor, dressed and mixed on the floor above it. Pick an off-peak afternoon for the first try, shower before you touch a tub, then let the heated floor do the rest.
