Wear Hanbok, Skip the Palace Fee: How the Rule Works

Wearing hanbok gets you into Seoul's palaces free, but the rule on what counts is strict. What qualifies, what a rental costs, and how locals read it.

Quick answer
  • Top and bottom, both traditional: a jeogori with an overlapping collar plus a skirt or pants gets you in free; a one-piece "hanbok-style" dress or a robe worn alone usually doesn't.
  • It covers more than Gyeongbokgung: all four grand palaces, Jongmyo Shrine, and the Joseon royal tombs, no separate ticket required.
  • Check the shop's rack before you leave: ask whether the set has a closed-front jeogori, since that's the one thing the gate actually checks.

Walk through Gwanghwamun on a clear afternoon and the courtyard is full of color that has nothing to do with the season: skirts the shade of a Seoul sunset, jackets tied at the chest, a dozen small groups angling phones at each other under the gate's dark wood eaves. Half of them rented those clothes an hour ago at a shop around the corner, and none of them paid to get in.

For once, dressing up isn't the expensive part of the day. It's the shortcut.

What is a jeogori (저고리)?

The jacket half of hanbok, closed at the front with an overlapping collar and tied with a ribbon called a goreum. It's the piece officials actually check for, more than the skirt, the pattern, or the fabric.

👘 What actually counts as "wearing hanbok"?

The basic shape matters more than how traditional the fabric looks: a jeogori with an overlapping front collar, paired with a skirt or trousers, is what the gate is checking for. Think of it less like a costume contest and more like a dress code at a formal restaurant: nobody is grading the embroidery, they're checking that the two required pieces are both actually on your body. Guidelines from Korea's Gungneung Cultural Heritage Headquarters accept both classic hanbok and the looser "lifestyle hanbok" style, as long as that two-piece structure holds. A jeogori worn over jeans, or a hanbok skirt paired with a plain T-shirt, can be turned away at the gate.

One-piece dresses cut in a hanbok style, sometimes called cheollik dresses after the long robe they borrow from, are the most common rejection. The guidelines are specific: "Hanbok is recognized only when a jeogori with an overlapping collar and a separate skirt are worn". Outerwear worn alone over regular clothes, like a gonryongpo or a dopo robe, fails the same test, since officials compare it to wearing a coat over underwear.

Since July 2019, the gender of the hanbok no longer has to match the wearer's. The change followed a recommendation from Korea's National Human Rights Commission, so a man in a skirt hanbok or a woman in pants hanbok qualifies on the same terms as anyone else, provided the basic structure is still there.

🏯 How does the free entry actually work, and what does a rental cost?

Show up in a qualifying hanbok and you walk through the regular ticket gate without buying anything, no reservation or separate line needed. The benefit covers all four grand palaces (Gyeongbokgung, Changdeokgung, Changgyeonggung, Deoksugung), plus Jongmyo Shrine and the Joseon royal tombs, since the Korea Heritage Service's Gungneung Cultural Heritage Headquarters launched the policy in 2013. What started as 300,000 to 400,000 visitors a year has grown past 2 million.

What is Jongmyo (종묘)?

The Joseon dynasty's royal shrine, where memorial tablets for kings and queens are kept. It's one of the same sites covered by the free-entry rule.

Skip the hanbok and the day fee is still small: 3,000 won at Gyeongbokgung and Changdeokgung, 1,000 won everywhere else the policy covers, a rate that hasn't moved since 2005.

A rental itself runs roughly ₩15,000 to ₩60,000 (about $11 to $45) for a few hours, more than the ticket you're saving, so treat the free entry as a nice bonus, not the reason to do this. Shops clustered around Gyeongbokgung, Anguk, and Insadong price basic traditional sets from about ₩20,000 to ₩40,000, while a premium or designer line at a shop like Maison Danha in Bukchon runs ₩40,000 to ₩80,000.

A shop a five-minute walk from the palace almost always has styles cut to pass the gate without a second thought, since that's most of its business. A costume rented for a party or a photo shoot elsewhere in the city is a riskier bet.

🎏 What are locals actually arguing about?

The debate isn't whether tourists should wear hanbok, it's whether rental shops have stretched the word past recognition. One hanbok maker told a reporter that much of what now shows up at the palaces has drifted so far from the real garment that it barely qualifies as traditional clothing anymore, and the Gungneung Cultural Heritage Headquarters admits it's wrestling with "국적불명 한복," hanbok of no identifiable origin, without wanting to crush small rental businesses over it.

What is "fusion hanbok" (퓨전한복)?

A rental-shop style that keeps hanbok's color and silhouette but swaps in zippers, shorter jackets, or non-traditional cuts for comfort and easy photos. It's popular with visitors and increasingly controversial with the officials who decide what gets in free.

A sharper version of the same argument flared up this September, when Chinese influencers filmed themselves at Gyeongbokgung in hanfu, China's own traditional dress. Professor Seo Kyung-duk said wearing your own country's clothing while traveling isn't the problem; the risk is that footage shot at Korea's most recognizable palace convinces foreign viewers that hanfu and hanbok are the same thing, when "our hanbok and China's hanfu are clearly different garments". Officials managing the free-entry program make a related, calmer request to everyone, hanbok included: the point was never to dress up purely for a discount. "애초 이 제도가 한복을 권장하고 제대로 입도록 유도하려는 취지였던 만큼 전통적인 방식을 가급적 따라달라" — please follow the traditional way where you can, since the policy was meant to encourage hanbok, not just free tickets.

My take If you're worried a "real" Korean will look at your rental and roll their eyes, that worry is pointed at the wrong target. Officials aren't bothered by tourists enjoying hanbok. They're bothered by shops renting out costumes so far from the garment's structure that the word stops meaning anything, and by anyone passing off a different country's clothing as Korean on camera at a Korean palace. Rent from a shop within sight of the gate you're visiting, pick a set with a proper closed jeogori, and you're on the right side of both arguments without trying.

FAQ
What if the night-viewing tickets are already sold out?
That's exactly when the costume earns its keep. A local report on last year's capped-at-3,300-a-day night program said cancellations occasionally reopen on the booking platform NOL Interpark, but added a simpler workaround: show up dressed the part and the staff will let you through without hunting for a refreshed listing.
Is this only a tourist thing, or do Koreans actually do it too?
Nobody checks a passport at the gate, only the outfit, and that's deliberate. A Cultural Heritage Administration representative put it plainly: younger Koreans increasingly treat an afternoon in hanbok as "a fun and cultural experience" in its own right, independent of where a visitor happens to be from. The same official noted a split in taste behind the counter, too: overseas renters tend to reach for the flashiest, most elaborate design on the rack, which is part of why those showier cuts keep drifting furthest from the standard shape.

The ₩3,000 you save at Gyeongbokgung's gate barely covers the rental, so go for the version of the day where you're not rushing past Gwanghwamun in sneakers. Pick a shop a short walk from the palace, ask to see the jeogori before you pay, and give yourself the extra twenty minutes a tied goreum and borrowed shoes actually take.
