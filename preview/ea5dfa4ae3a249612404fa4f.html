How to Make Yukgaejang (Korean Spicy Beef Soup) at Home

Kimchimari's amounts for yukgaejang, the step that turns a pot to mush, and why this version skips the ingredient most foreign cooks fear.

Quick answer
  • One pot, twelve bowls: Kimchimari's cup and tablespoon amounts for the broth, the beef and the seasoning, with no dried gosari required.
  • The real failure point isn't spice: it's pulling the brisket before it's tender enough to shred, or letting the pot run so long everything turns mushy and the broth reduces too far.
  • Act on it: simmer the brisket a full hour until a fork meets only slight resistance, then taste the broth again before adding any extra salt.

Steam fogs the window before you taste anything. Late October in Seoul means the evenings have turned cold enough that a hot, spicy soup stops feeling optional, and yukgaejang is one of the easiest ones to make at home. It's a shredded-beef soup built for exactly this kind of evening: scallion and chili float on top of a broth dark enough to fog your glasses before the spoon even reaches your mouth. The home version only asks for a pot, an hour of mostly unsupervised simmering, and a recipe that measures its seasoning instead of guessing.

The recipe below follows Kimchimari, the site run by Korean home cook JinJoo Lee, with her amounts kept as she gives them.

🥣 What goes into Kimchimari's yukgaejang?

The ingredient list skips the one thing that scares most foreign cooks off this soup: dried gosari. Kimchimari's version lists it as an optional add-in only, and leaves it out of the recipe she actually cooks, noting that reconstituting it takes hours of soaking and trimming. What's left is a short, grocery-store list: a cut of beef, a bag of bean sprouts, a few bunches of scallion, and a seasoning shelf most Korean pantries already keep stocked.

Broth Amount
Beef brisket, fat trimmed 2 lb (900g)
Water 16 cups (1 gallon)
Green onions 3 bunches
Fresh mung bean sprouts 1 bag (12 oz)

Sea salt 2 tsp
Sugar ½ tsp
Garlic, chopped 4 tsp
Garlic powder 1 tsp
Black pepper ½ tsp

What is guk ganjang (국간장)?

A soup soy sauce brewed lighter and saltier than the everyday bottle most kitchens keep, used specifically so it doesn't darken a clear or red broth the way regular soy sauce would. Kimchimari notes that regular soy sauce (jin ganjang) works as a substitute if guk ganjang isn't on the shelf, though it gives the broth less of that savory depth.

🔥 How to make it, and where it usually goes wrong

The soup fails less from the spice and more from timing: pull the brisket too soon and it won't shred, leave the pot on too long and it turns to mush. Kimchimari's own notes warn against cooking everything long enough to go mushy, and against letting the broth reduce too far along the way.

Here's the method, one action per step:

  1. Rinse the trimmed brisket and set it in a pot large enough for the full 16 cups of water plus room to boil.

  2. Bring it to a boil over medium-high heat, skimming off the foam as it rises.

  3. Reduce to medium-low and simmer about 1 hour, until a fork meets only slight resistance.

  4. While it simmers, cut the green onions into thirds, splitting any thick white parts lengthwise first.

  5. Once the broth turns cloudy, lift out the brisket and let it cool.

  6. Tear the cooled meat into pieces along the grain.

  7. Season the torn meat by hand with the soy sauce, gochugaru, sesame oil, salt, sugar, both garlics, sesame seeds and pepper.

  8. Mix in a couple of handfuls of the green onion.

  9. Return the meat and the rest of the green onions to the pot, boil 10 minutes, then add the bean sprouts.

  10. Lower the heat and simmer 20 minutes or more, tasting around the 10-minute mark to adjust the salt or chili powder.

A beaten egg or two, stirred in just before the heat goes off, is optional but common for extra richness. Taste the broth before you add any salt beyond the recipe's own amount; it reduces as it simmers, and ten more minutes on the stove can turn a well-balanced bowl into an overpowering one. A pot that still tastes flat at the ten-minute check almost always wants more gochugaru rather than more salt.

🌶️ The name admits what this soup replaced

The name points straight at what this soup replaced: gaejang, a dog-meat stew. Wikipedia describes yukgaejang as a beef-based substitute for gaejang, created for people who didn't want to eat dog, with recipes for the dish appearing as early as the Kyugonyoram, a Hangul cookbook from the early 1800s.

What is gaejang (개장)?

The older dog-meat ("개") stew that yukgaejang was created to replace, for cooks and diners who didn't want to eat dog. Once beef took its place, "yuk" (meat, here standing for beef) was added to the front of the name to mark the swap.

What is gosari (고사리)?

Dried bracken fern, rehydrated before cooking and added to some versions of yukgaejang for its chewy texture. A Korea Herald recipe calls for 30 grams of dried gosari, which yields about 1 cup once soaked and simmered tender.

Kimchimari's version skips gosari entirely, but it's still the ingredient that separates a "full" yukgaejang from a simpler one in a lot of Korean kitchens. My take A ten-item seasoning list and a pot that needs an hour of mostly unwatched simmering can look like more commitment than a weeknight deserves, and that hesitation is fair. If I were making this for the first time, I'd let the brisket run the full hour rather than check it early, and I'd season the broth in two passes: the recipe's amounts first, then a taste-and-adjust round ten minutes later rather than dumping everything in at once. The beef is forgiving once it's torn; the salt, once it's in the pot, isn't.
Go deeper
  • If you have one more day Kimchimari notes the soup tastes better the next day once the flavors settle, and keeps a few days in the fridge or longer in the freezer, so a pot made the night before only improves.
  • What locals argue about Gosari or no gosari. Kimchimari leaves it out for the sake of a shorter prep time, while the Korea Herald's version treats 30 grams of dried gosari as standard, and home cooks split over which one counts as "real" yukgaejang.

FAQ
Can I swap in a different cut of beef?
Yes. A Korea Herald piece on this same dish calls for shank, sometimes sold at a Korean butcher as satae, and treats it exactly the same way as the cut above: cooked low and slow until it pulls apart on its own. Either option needs roughly the same stretch on the stove before it's ready.
Mine came out orange, not the deep red I remember from a restaurant. Did I do something wrong?
No, that color is a choice, not a mistake. This version leans mild on purpose, and a restaurant's darker, oilier bowl usually comes from doubling down on chili flakes bloomed in oil; JinJoo Lee's own page says to scale both up if that's the look you're after.

Make the full pot even for one person; this is a soup that's meant to turn into tomorrow's lunch, and freezer bags handle the rest. Simmer the brisket the full hour, season in two passes, and taste before you reach for more salt.
