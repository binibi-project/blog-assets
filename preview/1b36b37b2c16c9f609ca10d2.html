Myeongdong, Seoul: What the Famous Shopping Street Costs

Myeongdong's street and Cathedral cost nothing to walk, but a Nanta ticket runs ₩50,000-70,000, and cash still beats cards at some street stalls despite a 2024 mandate.

Quick answer
  • The street and the Cathedral are free: Myeongdong-gil, the main shopping stretch off Exit 6, and Myeongdong Cathedral a short walk further both charge nothing to walk into.
  • The real spend is narrow and specific: a chicken skewer or gimbap roll runs a flat ₩5,000, and the one planned splurge is the Nanta comedy-cooking show at ₩50,000-70,000 a seat.
  • Card payment is still a coin flip: carry cash as the backup plan, since a 2026 check found some stalls still steering customers away from the card readers the district made mandatory in 2024.

Six o'clock on a Tuesday, and Exit 6 of Myeongdong Station already spills into a wall of K-pop playlists and shopping bags. Neon cosmetics signs stack three stories above the street, a sheet-mask sample lands in your hand before you've taken ten steps, and somewhere under all that noise a church bell from 1898 is still keeping its own schedule. Myeongdong has spent more than a century changing what it sells without ever losing the crowd, and that's the real reason it's still worth the subway ride.

The catch isn't finding something to buy. It's knowing which few things here are actually worth paying Myeongdong's prices for.

🚇 Getting there, and what the day actually costs

Ride Line 4 to Myeongdong Station and leave by Exit 6: the main shopping street opens right there, no ticket needed for the street itself. A one-way T-money fare is ₩1,550 (about $1.17), or ₩1,650 for a single-journey paper ticket. Exit 5 leads instead to the Shinsegae and Lotte department stores, and Exit 9 comes out at Myeongdong Cathedral. If you're starting from Gangnam Station, Line 2's loop runs the whole way to Euljiro 1-ga Station with no transfer: that station sits right at the north end of the Myeongdong shopping district, closest to the main Lotte Department Store branch.

The city made this official a while ago: Myeongdong was designated a special Tourism Promotion Area in March 2000 and is a stop on Seoul's own sightseeing bus route.

What is a "tourism special zone" (관광특구)?

A district the city government formally designates to concentrate shopping and visitor services, usually with extra signage, information centers and event funding. Myeongdong was one of the earlier areas to get the label, and it's still one of the most visited.

A 2010 Korean news estimate put Myeongdong's floating population at roughly 2,000,000 people a day, and a November 2011 survey of nearly 2,000 foreign visitors found 13.4% named shopping in Myeongdong as their single favorite thing to do in Seoul. Those numbers are old, but nothing about the evening crowd on Myeongdong-gil suggests the area has gotten any quieter since.

🛍️ What's actually worth buying, and what to skip

Treat the skincare counters as the real reason to come, not the clothing racks that repeat every half-block. Department stores and boutiques moved into Myeongdong through the 1970s and made it popular with young shoppers, and its K-beauty flagship stores like Olive Young now run on that same steady visitor traffic.

What is Olive Young?

A Korean health-and-beauty retail chain that stocks dozens of domestic skincare and makeup brands under one roof, sample testers included. Nearly every Korean neighborhood has a branch, but Myeongdong's are sized and stocked for a steady stream of visitors rather than a local top-up run.

Street food runs on a fixed-price board now: a chicken skewer or a single gimbap roll is ₩5,000, a fruit juice is ₩5,000-10,000, and steak or grilled lobster runs about ₩20,000, per a reporter's visit in March 2026. The Jung District office required those price tags, and card payment alongside them, from April 1, 2024, after visitors complained about being charged more than locals. Two years on, the same March 2026 visit found some stalls had quietly covered their card-payment signs and turned reluctant the moment a customer actually tried to pay by card. Keep a few ₩5,000 notes loose in a pocket rather than finding that out mid-order.

For currency exchange, counters cluster around Exits 5 and 6 of Myeongdong Station as well as Exit 6 of Euljiro 1-ga, and it's worth comparing the rate posted at a few shops before choosing one.

⛪🎭 The two stops actually worth planning around

Step inside for free: Myeongdong Cathedral, built in 1898, is the oldest Catholic cathedral in Korea, and it charges no admission. Its brick spire has watched over this street since before the shops did, and during the political demonstrations of the 1980s and 1990s it was one of the city's best-known gathering points.

Published visiting hours disagree across sources, with some listing the main church as open only a few hours a day and others giving a much wider window, so the safe plan is a weekday late-morning visit rather than gambling on Sunday Mass times. Go on a weekday if quiet is the point, since the plaza outside fills up fastest right after Sunday services let out.

The second is a ticket, not a church: book ahead and you'll see three seat grades at Nanta's Myeongdong theater, not one flat price. Think of A-seat as the general-admission option and VIP as a front-row upgrade; the venue sits on the third floor of the UNESCO Building at 서울 중구 명동2가 50-14, and as of this fall it runs weekday shows at 17:00 and 20:00.

What is Nanta (난타)?

A long-running non-verbal comedy show built around four chefs racing to finish a wedding banquet, using kitchen knives and pots as the entire percussion section. No dialogue means no language barrier, which is a big part of why it's stayed a fixture for visiting groups.

Even the cheapest A-seat costs more than two street-food dinners at the ₩5,000 price-board rate, so it only earns its place if a loud, physical comedy show is genuinely something you'd choose, not just something to fill an evening.

My take The worry I'd have in a visitor's shoes isn't whether Myeongdong is "too touristy," it's whether an evening here turns into spending real money on things you could get cheaper five minutes away. If I were planning the day, I'd put the free stops first, Myeongdong Cathedral and a walk down the main street, and save actual spending for the one thing that's genuinely scarce here: the skincare selection, or a Nanta ticket if physical comedy is your kind of night. Street food is the easiest trap, since it's priced for a crowd that keeps showing up anyway.

FAQ
What's my recourse if I get overcharged somewhere in the area?
Skip the argument and file it instead. The Korea Tourism Organization's 1330 hotline is built for exactly this kind of language-barrier dispute, and nationwide complaints on it climbed from 902 in 2023 to 1,543 in 2024, then to 1,744 for all of last year, a total the first seven months of this year had already topped. Dial 1330 domestically, or +82-2-1330 from abroad.
Is there a cheaper backup close by?
Namdaemun has been trading on the same plot since 1414, centuries before anyone built a tourist economy around this corner of Seoul. Fewer cosmetics giveaways, but a calmer crowd and gentler prices.

Myeongdong earns the subway ride for what's actually free here, the street itself and a 128-year-old cathedral, not for what it sells at a markup. Spend on the skincare or the Nanta ticket if either one genuinely interests you, and walk one more stop to Namdaemun for the snacks.
