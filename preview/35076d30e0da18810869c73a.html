Gyeongbokgung: The ₩3,000 Ticket That's About to Rise

Gyeongbokgung's current ₩3,000 fee, its closed day, the guard ceremony times, and why it's the one palace still skipping free Wednesdays.

Twice a day, a line of guards in red and blue marches out to the plaza in front of Gwanghwamun and swaps places with the ones already standing there, the same handover reenacted at the same two hours since long before most of the tourists filming it were born. Stand close enough and you'll hear the drum before you see the flags. It's the kind of free, five-minute moment that makes people forget to check anything else about the visit, like what the ticket costs or which day the gate doesn't open at all.

That's the gap this guide fills. The ceremony is the easy part; the fee, the hours and the one free day that doesn't apply here are the parts visitors get wrong.

What is Gyeongbokgung (경복궁)?

The main royal palace of the Joseon dynasty, built in Seoul in 1395 and rebuilt in the 1860s after a 16th-century fire. It's the largest of the city's five palaces and the one with the main gate most people picture when they think "Seoul palace."

🎟️ Is Gyeongbokgung actually about to cost more?

Not yet, but the ₩3,000 (about $2.25) ticket you'd buy today won't stay that price much longer. YTN reported in August 2026 that the Korea Heritage Service will publish new admission fees for the palaces and royal tombs in November, taking effect Jan. 1, 2027, the first increase in 20 years. Gyeongbokgung and Changdeokgung currently both charge ₩3,000 for an adult; Changgyeonggung and Deoksugung charge ₩1,000.

Some visitors skip the fee altogether. Children six and under, adults 65 and older, hanbok wearers who meet the official hanbok guideline, and national-merit or disability status holders (plus one companion) all enter free, with no ticket to buy at all.

What is hanbok (한복)?

Traditional Korean dress, worn today mostly for holidays, photos and palace visits. Rental shops cluster around every palace gate, and wearing one gets you free entry here, though the outfit has to match the palace's own hanbok guideline or you can still be turned away at the gate.

The last Wednesday of every month is also free, but that's where Gyeongbokgung starts to lag behind its neighbors. Since May 2026 the Korea Heritage Service has been expanding free admission from once a month to every Wednesday, starting with the royal tombs, then Deoksugung in August, then Changdeokgung, Changgyeonggung and Jongmyo this October. Gyeongbokgung was left off that list on purpose, with officials citing visitor crowding and safety, and no new date set. If you assumed every palace runs on the same free-Wednesday schedule now, this is the one exception.

⏰ When should you go, and when do the guards march?

Hours shift with the season, the palace is closed every Tuesday, and the guard ceremonies run on their own separate clock. From January and February through November and December it's open 09:00-17:00 with last admission at 16:00; March through May and September through October it runs 09:00-18:00, last admission 17:00; June through August it stays open until 18:30, last admission 17:30.

A visit today, in mid-October, falls on the 09:00-18:00 schedule with a 17:00 cutoff for last entry, so there's more daylight to work with than a winter trip would give you.

Two different guard performances run inside that window, and they're easy to mix up. The Royal Guard Changing Ceremony (수문장교대식) goes twice a day, at 10:00 and 14:00, about 20 minutes each. The separate Gwanghwamun Gate Guarding Ceremony (광화문 파수의식) runs at 11:00 and 13:00, about 10 minutes each, right in front of Gwanghwamun.

What is the 수문장교대식 (Royal Guard Changing Ceremony)?

A reenactment of the old palace guard handover, performed by costumed guards rather than real soldiers. It's one of two separate guard performances at Gyeongbokgung, and the one with the longer run time.

🚇 Getting to Gyeongbokgung by subway

Take Line 3 to Gyeongbokgung Station and come out Exit 5; it's about a five-minute walk to the gate. A single T-money fare across Seoul's subway is ₩1,550, up ₩150 since Korea JoongAng Daily reported the city's June 28, 2025 fare hike; paying cash for a single-journey ticket costs ₩1,650 instead.

That ₩10,800 is the floor, not the ceiling: it skips the hanbok rental some visitors add on purpose, since wearing one erases the admission line entirely.

My take A ₩3,000 ticket is cheap enough that nobody plans around it, and that's exactly how people end up planning around the wrong thing instead. The fee isn't what costs you a good visit here; showing up on a Tuesday, or missing both guard ceremonies by an hour in either direction, is what does. Build the day around the 10:00 or 14:00 slot first, let the rest of the palace fill in around it, and treat the ₩3,000 as the one line in this whole trip you don't need to think twice about, at least until January.

FAQ
Do I need to book a spot for either guard performance ahead of time?
No booking system turns up anywhere in the schedule, official or otherwise, so the only plan is showing up a little early and standing where you can see. Arrive after either performance has already started and you've largely missed it: neither one is long, and nobody pauses a sword drill to wait for latecomers.
I can only come on the last Monday of a month. Should I skip the museum next door?
Yes, most likely, so confirm beforehand. One Monday a month now sits closed on its own, added in March following reports of equipment strain and crowd-safety worries inside a building that used to run seven days a week without a break. Save that errand for another day; no such closure applies anywhere else on these grounds.

The entry fee is the smallest decision in this whole visit, and it's about to go up anyway. Pick a guard-ceremony time first and build the rest of the afternoon around it, since that's the part an early or late arrival actually costs you.
