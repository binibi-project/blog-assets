How to Make Andong Guksi (Korean Soybean Noodle Soup)

A real Andong guksi recipe: the soybean-powder dough ratio, why Andong itself barely serves it, and how to make it at home.

The noodle soup called Andong guksi isn't really something you can find much in Andong anymore, and that gap is most of why it's worth learning to make at home. In the hills north of the city, a food critic visiting one old head-family house in the summer of 2019 wrote down a line from its elderly jongbu, the woman who had run that kitchen for decades: "the men would catch the sweetfish, boil it down, and that became the broth," she told him, describing a dish her household once saved for ancestral rites. English-language pages about Andong guksi today mostly repeat that it's "the original form of kalguksu" without saying what actually separates the two, and a 2020 feature in Imaeil (매일신문) fills in both gaps: the dough, and how rare the real thing has become even where it was born.

🫘 Why soybean powder, and not an egg, is what makes it Andong

The dough, not the broth, is what separates Andong guksi from ordinary kalguksu. A 2020 Imaeil feature on Andong's remaining noodle shops says the one real difference is soybean powder mixed into the flour, usually making up 30-40% of the dry mix; push it higher and the dough smells of raw soybean and tears easily while you roll it. The paper also notes that real Andong-style bowls skip the extras common in Seoul, no egg strands, nothing to make the noodles glossy, because the point of the dish was always the plain, slightly rough bite of the dough itself.

Why "guksi" and not "guksu" (noodles)?

Guksu (국수) is standard Korean for noodles; guksi is the local Gyeongsang-region word for the same thing, and it's the form Andong natives use for their own dish.

That gap between the real thing and its reputation runs deeper than the dough. The same Imaeil feature counts how few restaurants in Andong itself still serve a dish under the name "Andong guksi," fewer than you can count on one hand, while noodle shops carrying that sign line up across Seoul in Mapo, Gangnam and Jongno instead. Wheat itself used to be the limiting ingredient: in the Joseon era it was expensive enough that a bowl of noodles was a special-occasion food for yangban households, and getting served guksi at someone's ancestral rite was something a guest could brag about afterward.

Geonjin guksi vs. nureun guksi

Geonjin guksi (건진국시) is boiled, rinsed in cold water and served in a chilled broth; nureun guksi (누른국시), the version most people actually cook and eat today, goes straight from the pot into warm broth. Geonjin guksi is the rarer, more labor-intensive one, traditionally made with broth boiled down from dried sweetfish for ancestral-rite tables; it's largely disappeared from home kitchens.

Andong cooks traditionally roll that dough out thin enough to let light through it, the way a food writer once compared it to hanji paper. That thinness is also why a stand mixer doesn't really help here; it's a rolling-pin job, same as the original recipe calls for.

If your grocery store only stocks plain soy flour, that's not quite the same thing: look for a bag labeled "roasted soy powder," made from grilled and crushed soybeans, since that's the roasted version Andong-style dough is actually built on.

FAQ
Can I cook extra and portion it out across the week?
Not really, not without losing the bite. Leftovers left sitting in their own broth swell within hours, so plan for a single sitting instead of stretching one pot across several meals.
Is "Andong guksi" the same thing as "Andong guksu"?
They're two names for one dish, and nobody has settled which spelling wins. A reporter who spent an afternoon eating it with a food critic in 2019 never picked a side in his own write-up, since locals use both terms interchangeably.

🥣 How to make it, and where it usually goes wrong

Start the dough before anything else, since it needs at least 30 minutes to rest. Mix the flour and soybean powder in the ratio above, add the salt, then work in the warm water a little at a time until it forms a rough ball.

  1. Knead the dough about 10 times until it's smooth, wrap it well, and let it rest at least 30 minutes, or up to a day in the fridge.

  2. Simmer the dried anchovies and kelp, plus the dried shrimp if you're using it, in the 950 ml of water for 30 minutes. Pull the kelp out after 15-20 minutes, before it turns slimy.

  3. Strain out the solids, season the broth with the guk ganjang and salt, add the onion, and simmer about 5 minutes until it turns translucent.

  4. Roll the rested dough out thin on a floured board, fold it a few times, and slice it into thin strips with a knife; toss the cut noodles in flour right away so they don't clump.

  5. Add the zucchini (and potato, if using) to the broth, bring it back up to a boil, then add the noodles and cook 3-5 minutes, tasting at the 3-minute mark.

  6. While the noodles cook, pan-fry the minced beef plain with a pinch of salt so it's ready the moment the pot is done.

  7. Ladle into bowls, top with the beef and chopped green onion, and let everyone stir in their own soy-scallion sauce at the table rather than seasoning the whole pot.

Most of what goes wrong happens before the noodles ever hit the pot. A dough that's too wet won't hold the thin roll Andong guksi is known for, so add water in small amounts and stop as soon as the dough comes together, even if it still looks a little rough. The other common miss is timing: homemade noodles this thin cook fast, and three minutes at a real boil is usually enough, so walking away for a phone call is how a careful dough turns into mush in the bowl.

My take The guksi came right after a morning spent walking through a few of Andong's old seowon on that September 2026 trip, and next to the richer, chili-dark jjimdak we ate later, it was the plainer of the two dishes, the one that let the dough do the talking. Weigh the soybean powder the first time instead of guessing, so you have a real number to adjust from on the second batch. If you only have the ingredients and the afternoon for one Andong noodle project, this is the one that actually teaches you something about the region's cooking, not just its flavor.

Make the dough once at the lower 30% soybean ratio before deciding you want it stronger, and skip the egg if you're tempted to add one for looks. The plain version is the one that still holds a place in a handful of kitchens in Andong itself, which is a better reason to get it right than any amount of garnish would be.
