SRT Is Gone: What Korea's Train Merger Means for You

Korea folded SRT into KTX on September 1, 2026. What changed for fares, the booking app, and your Seoul-Busan ticket.

Quick answer
  • SRT no longer exists as its own railway: Korail absorbed it on September 1, 2026, and the former SRT trains run as KTX-Sancheon now.
  • Fares dropped to the old SRT level: a Seoul-Busan standard-class ticket fell from ₩59,800 to ₩54,400.
  • Book through KORAIL+ from now on: delete your old SRT bookmark and use KORAIL+ for any trip departing after September 1, 2026.

Suseo Station used to feel like a separate country from Seoul Station. Different company, different app, a cream-and-purple train with its own logo pulling away from a platform that Korail didn't touch. I remember a coworker in Gangnam explaining it to a visiting cousin like a secret handshake: take the SRT if you're leaving from the south side of the city, it's usually cheaper.

That whole conversation is obsolete now. The trains still pull out of Suseo on schedule, but the name painted on the side has quietly changed, and the sales pitch that used to come with it is gone too.

If you read a travel blog from before this year, it probably told you to compare SRT and KTX fares before booking. That advice is now wrong, and worse, it's wrong in a way that still sounds plausible. Here's what actually changed, and what it means for the ticket you're about to buy.

🚆 What happened on September 1

Korea's two high-speed rail operators became one. Korea's Fair Trade Commission approved Korail's acquisition of SR Corporation, the company that ran SRT, and Khan.co.kr reported that the integrated operation began on September 1, 2026. SBS's own coverage of the launch day quoted the line plainly: "통합 코레일이 오늘 공식으로 출범을 하거든요" ("the merged Korail officially launched today").

SR had run since 2014, when the government split it off from Korail to bring competition to the rail industry. That 12-year experiment is over. The former SRT trains, which were always the same KTX-Sancheon rolling stock under a different coat of paint, now simply run as KTX-Sancheon. A live timetable for the Suseo-Busan line confirms it in practice: the trains due out on November 1, 2026 are listed as KTX 301, KTX-Sancheon 303 and KTX-Sancheon 367, with no SRT brand anywhere on the board.

What is KTX-Sancheon?

Sancheon (산천, "mountains and rivers") is the second-generation KTX train, which entered service in March 2010. SR ran the same model as SRT trains between Suseo and Busan or Mokpo from December 2016. After the merger, every one of those trains simply became KTX-Sancheon again, whether it used to say KTX or SRT on the side.

The booking side moved first. A single app, KORAIL+, launched on August 3, 2026, built on the existing Korail Talk app so travelers could reserve both KTX and SRT tickets in one place ahead of the full merger. Tickets for the newly integrated trains, running from September 1 onward, went on sale through KORAIL+ and the Korail website starting August 5 at 10 a.m..

If you already had a Korail account, it carried over automatically; SRT-only members have to convert separately through the SRT website to keep their points and purchase history. The SRT app itself hasn't been shut off, but since September 1 it only takes non-member bookings, so mileage and member discounts don't apply there anymore, which leaves little reason to keep using it.

What is KORAIL+?

The single booking app for Korea's high-speed and intercity trains since the merger, replacing the separate Korail Talk and SRT apps for new reservations. It covers KTX, KTX-Sancheon, KTX-Eum and KTX-Cheongnyong on one calendar.

💸 What a ticket costs now

Prices moved down to match SRT's old fares, not up. The clearest example is the country's busiest route:

On paper that looks like a flat cut, but it's really a leveling: Korail committed to bringing its KTX fares down to the level SRT already charged, with the full average 10% reduction phased in across three years and a fare ceiling set by the transport ministry so prices can't drift back up on their own. The ₩5,400 saved on one Seoul-Busan ticket won't change your trip, but double it for a round trip and it's close to another meal in Busan. If you're booking a KTX ticket on a route SRT also used to cover, check the current price before assuming the old, higher number still applies — on Seoul-Busan it no longer does.

The merger also added capacity rather than just shuffling the same seats around: Korail committed to at least 15,000 more seats a day on weekdays and 17,000 more on weekends, with 23 to 26 additional daily services. Loyalty also unified. SRT never had its own mileage program, and now every route, former SRT included, earns the same 5% Korail mileage.

📍 Does Seoul Station or Suseo still matter?

Pick the station closer to your hotel, not the one you think is faster, because the speed gap that used to matter is mostly gone. When SRT launched in 2016 it was marketed as a handful of minutes quicker to Busan than KTX from Seoul Station, with reports at the time citing figures anywhere from about 6 to 10 minutes. Current timetables don't back that up as a hard rule anymore: a same-day Suseo-Busan run takes about 2 hours 28 minutes to 2 hours 36 minutes depending on the train, while Seoul Station's fastest nonstop departures land in a similar 2 hours 38 to 2 hours 44 minutes. Pick a train that stops at every station on the way, and either departure point can stretch past 3 hours, so the schedule matters more than the station.

What has changed is the route map. As part of the merger plan, Korail committed to running larger, 955-seat trainsets out of Suseo and adding services that detour through Seodaejeon and Gupo, aimed at giving southern Seoul easier access to Busan-bound trains without crossing town to Seoul Station first. Seoul Station was set to gain added departures across several lines, including the Gyeongbu, Honam and Jeolla corridors, plus two more daily KTX-Eum runs on the Jungang Line. The Transport Ministry also floated circular service patterns, such as a single train running Seoul to Busan to Suseo to Pohang and back to Seoul, something that wasn't possible when the two networks ran separately.

Seoul Station's first Busan-bound train leaves around 05:13-05:27 depending on the day, with the last around 22:28; Suseo's first leaves at 05:30 and its last at 22:40. Both run dozens of trains a day, so the honest answer is to book whichever station actually saves you a subway transfer.

My take I live in Gangnam-gu, and Suseo-dong is one of its 12 neighborhoods, so the "take SRT, it's closer" advice used to be genuinely useful to me. These days I'd tell a visitor staying anywhere near Gangnam, Suseo, or Jamsil to check Suseo departures first simply because it skips the trip north to Seoul Station, not because the ticket is some separate, cheaper product anymore. The fare difference that used to justify picking a side is gone; what's left is just geography.

FAQ
I had a discount pass or commuter ticket under the old SRT system. Does it still work?
It's being folded in, not canceled. Korail says SRT's old discount programs and commuter passes are being brought into the combined system rather than dropped, alongside the shared mileage rate. The announcement didn't spell out the exact replacement product for every SRT discount, so if one isn't showing up in KORAIL+ yet, ask at a staffed ticket window before assuming it's gone for good.
Will fares go back up once the three-year pledge ends?
Not guaranteed. Regulators limited increases only through the commitment period, and nothing in the approval rules out an adjustment once that window closes, so treat today's figure as current, not permanent.

The old reflex, picking SRT for the fare and KTX for the brand name, doesn't apply anymore: it's one network, one price list, and one app. Book through KORAIL+, pick the station nearest where you're actually staying, and double-check the seat map before assuming anything from an older guide still holds.
