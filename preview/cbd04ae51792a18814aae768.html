How to Make Jeyuk-bokkeum (Korean Spicy Pork Stir-Fry) at Home

Korean Bapsang's 900-gram pork ratio, the pan-crowding mistake that skips the caramelized edges, and why it's Korea's go-to office lunch.

Quick answer
  • 900 grams of pork, one marinade: Korean Bapsang's gochujang-honey-apple marinade scales for 4 servings, built to coat thin-sliced pork shoulder plus onion and scallion.
  • Cook it in two batches, not one: crowding a hot pan traps steam and keeps the pork pale instead of caramelized, the single most common first-try mistake.
  • Act first: marinate the pork for at least 30 minutes before the pan ever heats up, so the sauce has time to cling instead of pooling at the bottom.

Jeyuk-bokkeum doesn't try to impress anyone, and that's exactly why it ends up on more Korean lunch tables than almost anything fancier. A hot pan, a few spoonfuls of red paste, and thin pork slices that go in pale and come out dark at the edges, blistered where the sugar in the marinade caught the heat first.

The smell hits before the dish does: garlic and ginger first, then something sweeter underneath it, apple or plum syrup working against the chili. It's not a dish built for an occasion. It's the one a Korean coworker orders without opening the menu.

🥩 The 900-gram ratio, and the two-batch rule

Korean Bapsang's version scales from one marinade over 900 grams of thinly sliced pork shoulder or butt, enough for 4 servings. A mix of shoulder or butt with some pork belly works too, not just one cut on its own, so a leftover end of belly from another meal doesn't have to go to waste. The sauce leans on gochujang for body and gochugaru for straight heat, backed by soy sauce, rice wine, sugar, honey, sesame oil, garlic, ginger, grated apple.

Pork and vegetables Amount
Pork shoulder or butt, thinly sliced 900 g (2 lb)
Onion, sliced 1/2 medium
Scallions, cut into 2-inch pieces 3
Marinade Amount
Gochujang 6 Tbsp
Gochugaru (Korean chili flakes) 1-2 Tbsp

Sugar 2 Tbsp

What does "jeyuk" mean?

Jeyuk (제육) is pork prepared for a quick stir-fry or grill, as opposed to samgyeopsal's thick, unseasoned belly slices. Bokkeum (볶음) just means stir-fried, which is how the dish shows up on Korean restaurant menus as a whole category of quick, high-heat cooking.

The garlic and ginger aren't just for flavor. They're there to cut the "porky" smell raw pork can carry, which is also why the marinade leans on two full tablespoons of minced garlic rather than one. The grated apple is optional, and maesil cheong (Korean plum syrup) works as a straight swap at 2 tablespoons if that's what's in the pantry.

Toss the sliced pork with the marinade, add the onion and scallion, and let it sit for about 30 minutes before anything touches a hot pan. Then grill or pan-fry over medium-high heat, cooking in two batches instead of one, until the meat is slightly caramelized at the edges.

A crowded pan is the one mistake that ruins an otherwise correct recipe. Pile all 900 grams in at once and the moisture the pork releases has nowhere to go, the same way crowding a sheet pan keeps roasted vegetables limp instead of browned; it steams the meat gray instead of letting the sugar in the marinade catch and darken. Split the pork into two batches and give each one room in the pan, even if that means the second batch goes in while the first one's resting on a plate.

If slicing at home rather than buying pre-sliced bulgogi pork, partially freezing the shoulder for about an hour first makes it far easier to cut thin and even.

🍽️ Why it's Korea's go-to lunch, not just a home dish

Jeyuk-bokkeum is what Korean food writers call a "국민 반찬," a people's side dish, and it keeps ranking near the top of delivery apps, cafeteria menus, and office lunch orders. It shows up at baekban restaurants and in convenience-store lunchboxes, and its popularity has only grown with Korea's meal-prep habit of cooking a big batch and reusing it through the week.

A 2022 Incruit survey of 1,004 Korean office workers named jeyuk-bokkeum one of four go-to lunch dishes, alongside kimchi-jjigae, jjajangmyeon, dwaeji-gukbap, and tracked its average restaurant price by region.

A Korean coworker reaching for jeyuk-bokkeum at lunch isn't usually choosing it for anything unusual about the flavor. It's dependable, it cooks fast, and unlike a bowl of noodles, it reheats into something just as good the next day instead of going soft and sad in the fridge.

Leftovers don't go to waste, either. Korean home cooks reheat extra jeyuk-bokkeum into a rice bowl with a fried egg on top, chop it fine for fried rice with kimchi and corn, or dry it out slightly and roll it into kimbap so it doesn't make the rice soggy.

🛒 Buying the pork and swapping what you can't find

Ask for "pork shoulder, thinly sliced for bulgogi" at a Korean grocery counter, or at a regular butcher counter if there's no Korean market nearby. Korean markets abroad commonly sell this cut pre-sliced for exactly this kind of dish, which skips the slicing step entirely.

Three ingredients are worth sourcing from an Asian grocery aisle rather than substituting: gochujang, gochugaru, sesame oil. Regular mirin stands in for Korean cooking rice wine without changing the method.

What's maesil cheong (매실청)?

A Korean green plum syrup, sweet with a mild tartness, often used the way honey or sugar is used elsewhere but with a little more edge. It's the straight substitute for grated apple in this marinade, at 2 tablespoons.

My take A marinade with ten ingredients and a cut of meat that needs slicing before it even starts can look like more of a commitment than a weeknight deserves. If I were making this for the first time, I'd skip worrying about the apple versus maesil cheong question entirely and focus on the one step that actually decides how it turns out: cooking the pork in two batches instead of crowding it into one pan to save a few minutes. The marinade forgives a swapped ingredient; a steamed, gray batch of pork doesn't recover once it's happened.
Go deeper
  • If you have one more day Marinate the pork the night before instead of the morning of. The sliced pork and marinade can sit together in the fridge well past the 30-minute minimum without the dish suffering for it.
  • What locals argue about Apple or maesil cheong. Both do the same job, cutting the chili with something sweet and a little acidic, and home cooks split over which one actually tastes more like a restaurant's version.
  • Watch for The honey and sugar in the marinade. They're also what caramelizes fastest in a hot pan, so a batch left too long chasing a deeper color can go from browned to burnt in the same minute.
FAQ
What do I actually serve it with besides rice?
Wrap it in lettuce or perilla leaves with a dab of ssamjang, the way many Korean households eat it instead of plating it over rice alone. Crown daisy (ssukgat) makes a good addition to the wrap if it's in season and available.
Can I grill this instead of using a pan?
Yes — the written instructions list grilling as an equally valid option alongside a stovetop skillet, so an outdoor grill or a tabletop one works without changing the marinade or timing at all.

Cook it in two batches, taste the marinade balance before it ever hits the pan, and the rest of the dish takes care of itself. A ₩7,881 average Seoul restaurant plate from a 2022 survey was never really the point; a pan at home gets four servings out of the same ingredients, plus leftovers that turn into tomorrow's lunch.
