Seoul's Ddareungi Bike Share: What a Visitor Actually Pays

Ddareungi costs ₩1,000 an hour with no account needed, covers 2,799 stations, and the one fine locals argue about never lands on cyclists.

Quick answer
  • A one-hour ride costs ₩1,000: buy it as a guest with a card, no Korean ID or account required.
  • The dock is rarely the real problem: it's the app's card registration and SMS step that trips visitors up, not finding a bike.
  • Set up payment before you need it: register your card in the app on hotel Wi-Fi the night before, so a failed verification text doesn't strand you at an empty dock.

Ever stood outside a palace wall, phone battery dropping, and eyed a row of white-and-green bikes nobody else seemed to want? Seoul parks thousands of them at almost every subway exit and park gate, free for the taking the moment a card goes through. The part you only notice watching a local do it is how little ceremony is involved: tap a phone, pull a bike loose, pedal off mid-sentence.

Fifteen minutes later you're riding along the Han River with actual wind on your face, which no subway car has ever managed. Whether you're doing the same by sundown depends less on your legs than on whether the app's payment screen cooperates first.

"QR-type" vs. "LCD-type" Ddareungi bike — what's the difference?

Both rent the same way in the end, but two console generations are still mixed into the fleet. A QR-type bike unlocks when the app's camera scans the code on its handlebars; an older LCD-type bike needs its bike number typed into the app first, then a rental code punched into the small screen mounted on the bars

💰 What does Ddareungi actually cost, and do you need to join first?

A one-hour day pass costs ₩1,000 (about $0.75 at ~₩1,337.5/$1), and nobody has to prove they live in Korea to buy one. Seoul's own public bike fee schedule lists the exact same ₩1,000 rate whether the rider is a registered member or a one-time guest, and the same holds for the ₩2,000 two-hour and ₩3,000 three-hour options. Going past the pass's limit adds ₩200 every 5 minutes.

That guest access isn't new or fragile. Since noon on July 7, 2017, non-members, foreign tourists included, have been able to skip identity verification, account sign-up, and a deposit entirely, paying only by mobile micropayment or credit card. The catch is that guest pricing only covers a day pass; a season pass still needs a registered account.

The 3-hour tier is barely a year old: Seoul added it on November 13, 2025. The network behind these passes is bigger than it looks from one station: about 45,000 bikes across 2,799 rental stations by the end of 2025, up from 2,719 in 2022, and 5.06 million people have signed up as members over the service's first decade.

📍 Where do you actually find one, and how does unlocking work?

Stations sit at almost every subway exit and park gate, so the real question in Seoul isn't whether one exists nearby, it's whether it has an open slot. Tourists are a small but fast-growing share of who's asking: foreign riders logged 50,599 rides between January and September 2025, up from 20,163 in the same months of 2019.

Unlocking takes one of two forms depending on which console the bike happens to have, QR scan or typed code, and either way the app's own map shows which nearby docks still have bikes or open slots before you walk over. A dock reading empty on the map is usually accurate within a minute or two, not a glitch worth arguing with.

Payment inside the app has also widened recently: KakaoPay and Samsung Pay were added as options, with NaverPay scheduled to follow. All three assume a Korean-linked account, though, so a foreign-issued card through the regular checkout screen is still the more dependable route for a short visit.

⛑️ What's the one rule nobody enforces, and where does this fall apart for a visitor?

Korean law requires a helmet for anyone cycling on a road or bike lane, but a Ddareungi rider who skips one faces no fine at all. The 2018 Road Traffic Act amendment that made helmets mandatory left the enforcement decree without a penalty clause for bicycles, a gap the city never closed. Compare that to an e-scooter, where riding bare-headed costs ₩20,000.

Member vs. non-member pricing — does it actually matter here?

For a day pass, no: Seoul's fee schedule charges members and non-members the identical ₩1,000/₩2,000/₩3,000 rate. The difference only shows up if you'd rather buy a week or a year at once, since those season rates are member-only.

The other place a visitor can get tripped up has nothing to do with the bike itself. Seoul's short-term Climate Companion Card, the 1-to-7-day transit pass sold since October 1, 2026, covers subway and bus but explicitly leaves out Ddareungi and the Han River ferry. Assuming that pass covers a bike ride is the kind of mistake that only shows up once you're standing at the dock with a card that won't scan.

My take First time on a bike in traffic that doesn't move the way it does back home isn't a confidence builder, and it's fine to admit that before you've pedaled a single block. If I were visiting with only a few days to spare, I'd test one along the Han River path first, away from intersections, before trusting a Ddareungi through actual city streets. Once the app and the card are both working, the real decision left is just how much walking you're willing to trade for ₩1,000 an hour.

FAQ
Can a child ride along, or is this strictly an adult, solo thing?
Solo riders must be 13 or older, but a guardian can now buy a family pass, launched in April 2025, to bring a younger child along under supervision. About 30,000 of these sold in the pass's first six months, and 78% of that buying happened on weekends.
What if every dock near me is completely full when I need to return the bike?
Seoul tracks the busiest stations by name and staffs 330 of them directly during commute hours, and it pays riders 100 mileage points for docking or renting at a station running over 200% or under 30% of capacity at those times. In practice, that means checking the app's live map for the next-closest station with space is worth doing before you start circling the block.

Ddareungi is one of the few things in Seoul that's actually cheaper than it looks on paper, and the app is the only part worth testing before you actually need it to work. Register a card the night before, on hotel Wi-Fi, and the rest of the trip is just picking a dock with an open slot.
