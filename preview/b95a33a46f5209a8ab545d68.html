How to Make Jjajangmyeon (Korean Black Bean Noodles) at Home

Beyond Kimchee's gram-measured black bean noodle recipe, the two-minute step most home cooks skip, and why Chinese black bean paste won't give you the same bowl.

Quick answer
  • Fry the chunjang first: 2-3 minutes alone in hot oil, before the pork or vegetables go anywhere near the pan, is the step that keeps the sauce from tasting bitter.
  • Chinese black bean paste won't give you this dish: chunjang is sweeter and darker, and the two recipes behind this post both warn against swapping it in.
  • Measure, don't eyeball: weigh the chunjang in grams, since how salty a brand turns out varies enough to throw off the whole pot.

Most home attempts at jjajangmyeon go wrong in the first two minutes, not the last twenty. The usual move is to tip the black bean paste straight into the pan with the pork and vegetables, the way you'd stir a jar of pasta sauce into whatever's already cooking. Skip the step where the paste gets its own few minutes alone in hot oil, and what lands on the noodles tastes thin and faintly sour instead of the deep, almost caramelized black you get from a real Chinese-Korean kitchen. Nothing about a jar of chunjang looks like it needs cooking twice, which is exactly why this step gets skipped.

🫘 Why the sauce, not the noodles, decides this dish

The sauce succeeds or fails in the first two to three minutes, when raw chunjang hits hot oil by itself, before anything else goes into the pan. Beyond Kimchee's recipe heats 4 tablespoons of oil in a wok, adds the paste, and stir-fries it alone for 2-3 minutes before straining it onto a plate and cleaning the wok. Korean Bapsang's version does the same thing in a smaller saucepan, frying the paste with oil, sugar and optional oyster sauce for the same 2-3 minutes.

What is chunjang (춘장)?

A thick, nearly black fermented paste made from soybeans and wheat flour, with caramel coloring added during aging. It looks related to Chinese black bean paste but tastes sweeter, and the two aren't meant to swap for each other.

Skip the frying and the flavor stays flat; both recipe writers single out this step as the one that removes the paste's natural bitterness. The paste looks cooked already, straight from the jar, which is exactly what makes the shortcut tempting and wrong.

🍜 Beyond Kimchee's gram recipe, step by step

Measure the chunjang and oil by weight, not by eye, since brands vary enough in saltiness to throw off the whole pot. Beyond Kimchee's recipe, built for 4 servings and a 20-minute prep plus 20-minute cook, lists exact amounts for everything:

Ingredient Amount
Jjajangmyeon noodles, fresh or dried 4 servings
Chunjang (black bean paste) 75 g
Oil 60 ml
Lard (or more oil) 30 g
Chopped leek or green onion 60 g
Sugar 8 g
Pork, cut small 225 g
Cabbage, chopped 150 g
Onion, chopped 1 small
Zucchini, chopped ½
Oyster sauce 15 ml
Water 360 ml
Chicken bouillon powder 4 g
Cornstarch + water 8 g + 45 ml

Source: Beyond Kimchee's jjajangmyeon recipe, checked 2026-11-16.

After the fried paste comes off the heat, the rest moves fast: fry the leek with the sugar until it caramelizes slightly, cook the pork until it's no longer pink, add the cabbage and onion until they soften, then the zucchini. Stir the reserved paste and the oyster sauce back in so everything is coated, pour in the water and bouillon, boil for 3 minutes, then thicken it with the cornstarch slurry until it turns glossy. Boil the noodles separately while the sauce simmers, and pull them the moment they're done; overcooked noodles lose the chew that makes this dish worth making at home.

Korean Bapsang's version lands in the same place with slightly different ratios: 5 tablespoons of chunjang, 1 tablespoon each of sugar and optional oyster sauce, a cup of stock or water (half a cup more for a thinner sauce), for 3-4 servings. Go light on the oyster sauce: Beyond Kimchee's own tip is about 1 tablespoon for the full batch, since more tastes too salty against the paste's sweetness.

🔥 The paste you can't swap in, and what a bowl costs today

Chinese black bean paste is not a substitute, even though the jars look similar on a shelf. Chunjang is sweeter and darker than its Chinese cousin, and both recipes behind this post warn against swapping it in if you want the Korean flavor. Doenjang (soybean paste) and black bean garlic sauce land even further off, since both carry flavors chunjang doesn't have.

Jarred, pre-roasted chunjang is the easier buy: it's sold at Korean grocery stores and online, with Lion one of the better-known brands on store shelves outside Korea. Pre-roasted paste still benefits from a quick fry, though it needs less time than raw paste since the bitterness is already mostly gone.

What is ganjjajang (간자장/건자장)?

The dry version of this dish: the same fried paste and stir-fried pork and vegetables, but no water, stock or cornstarch slurry added afterward. The sauce stays thick and clings to the noodles instead of pooling in the bowl.

Noodles matter less than the sauce, but they're not interchangeable either. Fresh jjajangmyeon noodles sit in the refrigerated section of Korean grocery stores, and the frozen or dried backups are labeled udon, jajangmyeon (우동 짜장면), or jungwhamyeon (중화면). Buy whichever of those three you can actually find; all three are built to hold up under a thick sauce the way spaghetti isn't.

This dish was invented in Korea, not imported whole from China. Chinese immigrants settled near Incheon's Jemulpo Port after it opened in 1883, and an Incheon restaurateur adapted the Chinese noodle dish zhajiangmian into something sweeter and less salty for Korean taste in the mid-1950s, with more onion, more minced meat, and extra water and starch to tame the paste. It stayed a special-occasion meal, the kind ordered for graduations and exam days, until it became affordable enough for everyday lunch sometime after the 1970s.

It's Korea's most popular home-delivery dish today, and tradition has single Koreans eating it together on Black Day, April 14, as a counterpoint to the couples' holidays earlier in the year. A 2010 count put daily sales above 7 million bowls nationwide, a scale that tracks with how often it shows up on delivery apps rather than restaurant tables alone.

What is danmuji (단무지)?

Yellow pickled radish, the standard side dish at any Chinese-Korean restaurant table. Some diners add green onion kimchi or a fried egg on top of the noodles instead, but danmuji is the one nobody skips.

My take If you've never used a wok and the nearest Korean grocery store is an hour away, this is still worth trying before you write it off. The paste is the one ingredient actually worth a special trip or an online order; everything else in the ingredient table is sitting in a regular supermarket already. I'd rather see someone nail the two-minute chunjang fry with a cheap saucepan than skip the whole dish because they don't own the "right" equipment.
Go deeper
  • If you have one more day Make the sauce ahead. Readers of Korean Bapsang's recipe report it holds up to 5 days refrigerated, reheated with the cornstarch added again once it's back on the stove.
  • What locals argue about Wet or dry. Regular jjajangmyeon comes glossy and saucy; ganjjajang skips the water and stock entirely and stays thick enough to cling to the noodles instead.
  • Watch for Oversalting. Paste brands vary enough that the same 5-tablespoon measure can taste sharp in one jar and mild in another, so taste before adding the full amount of oyster sauce on top.
FAQ
No Korean grocery store nearby. What's the move?
Order through a site that ships pantry goods internationally rather than waiting for a local shelf to carry it. A single container holds enough for a long stretch of occasional cooking at this post's measurements, so treat it as a once-a-year purchase, and transfer leftovers into a sealed jar instead of leaving a loose lid in the door of the fridge.
Does it have to be pork?
No. Swap in chicken, beef, or shrimp and squid without changing anything else about the method, just add shellfish last so it doesn't turn rubbery. A mushroom-and-tofu version skips meat entirely and still soaks up the same dark, sweet coating.

Buy real chunjang once, fry it properly every time, and the rest of this recipe is just chopping. The two minutes in hot oil are the whole difference between a bowl that tastes like a Chinese-Korean restaurant and one that tastes like a jar of paste someone forgot to cook.
