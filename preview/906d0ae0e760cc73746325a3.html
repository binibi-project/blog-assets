Why There's No 4th Floor: Korean Superstitions Explained

Why Korean buildings skip the 4th floor, why a red pen still unsettles people, and why moving day can cost 10-20% more on the lucky dates.

Four is the one number Korea quietly erases. Step into almost any mid-rise elevator here and the panel skips straight from 3 to 5, or swaps the fourth-floor button for a plain "F." Ask why, and the answer traces back to a single syllable, a word that sounds exactly like the word for death. It shows up in airport gate numbers, in the cash amounts people avoid handing over at weddings, and in a story about electric fans that a government agency was still telling people in this century. None of it gets explained to a first-time visitor, which is usually how a Korean friend ends up flinching at something as small as a red pen.

🛗 Why is there no 4th floor button?

Many Korean buildings skip the number 4 because 사 (sa), the word for "four," is pronounced exactly like 死, the word for "death." Korea JoongAng Daily reports that elevators are the most visible example: the panel often jumps straight from 3 to 5, or the fourth-floor button is simply marked "F" instead of a number. The habit reaches far past elevators, too.

Why does "four" sound dangerous in Korean?

사 (sa), the Sino-Korean word for four, is pronounced the same as 死, the word for death. The two characters look nothing alike on paper, but the sound is identical, and that coincidence alone is strong enough that entire buildings get designed around it.

It isn't just buildings bending around a sound. The same instinct shows up at weddings and funerals, where guests avoid handing over cash amounts built around the number 4. China and Japan treat the number as unlucky for the same pronunciation reason, which is part of why the habit has stuck around Korea for so long.

✒️ Two rules every Korean kid learns: no red pens, no fans overnight

Writing a living person's name in red ink is considered a bad omen in Korea, a habit traced back to how red ink marks the names of the dead. The Korea Herald explains that family registers have long recorded a deceased person's name in red, a practice believed to ward off evil spirits. One widely repeated theory pushes the origin further back: the Chinese emperor Qin Shi Huang reserved the color red for his own name and banned everyone else from using it, and by the time that prohibition reached Korea it had turned into a warning that a name written in red invites death.

Is red ink ever okay to use?

Yes. The one accepted exception is a 도장 (dojang), the personal stamp many Koreans still use in place of a signature on official paperwork. Red ink from a seal carries none of the taboo; it's specifically a name handwritten in red that makes people uneasy.

Teachers still tend to avoid red pens on a student's name, and a gift card is one more place the color quietly disappears. Nobody will say anything if a visitor breaks the rule by accident; it's the kind of thing you only learn after a Korean friend's face changes for half a second.

No mechanism makes "fan death" true, and yet a Korean government agency was still warning against it as recently as 2006. The belief that a running fan in a closed room can cause fatal suffocation or hypothermia traces back to at least a 1927 newspaper report, decades before anyone tried to test it.

When someone finally did, it didn't hold up. Dr. Suh Hee-sun of Gachon University Gil Hospital has said not a single confirmed case of fan-caused death has ever been reported anywhere in the world, and former KAIST professor Rim Chun-taek ran his own two-hour test in a sealed room with a fan running, tracking his blood pressure, heartbeat and body temperature the whole time. None of it changed. The Korean Consumer Protection Board still issued a warning against sleeping with a fan on in 2006.

Belief or not, the habit survives in the product itself. Standing and desk fans sold in Korea still commonly ship with a built-in timer, the small, practical trace of a fear nobody can quite let go of.

📦 Why does moving day cost more on some dates?

Movers in Korea charge 10-20% more on "son-eobneun-nal," the lunar dates believed to be free of mischievous ghosts, simply because demand for them spikes. Son (손) is short for sonnim, the Korean word for "guest": here it refers to an unwelcome one, a wandering spirit believed to interfere with moving house, tending a grave or starting repairs.

What is son-eobneun-nal (손 없는 날)?

Literally "the day without son," a set of dates on the lunar calendar believed to be free of wandering evil spirits. Koreans have traditionally picked these dates for moving house, visiting a grave or starting renovations, and the custom still shapes which days a moving company books up first.

Dates ending in a 0 or a 9 on the lunar calendar count as free of son: the 9th, 10th, 19th, 20th, 29th or 30th of each lunar month. Moving companies fill up on those days first, and that crunch is what pushes quotes 10-20% above an ordinary date. So what does that number mean for a reader planning a move? Picking any other date on the calendar is one of the simplest ways to bring a quote down, no haggling required.

The ritual side of it is still very alive for some families. Mover Yoon Jae-sang, 36, couldn't land a "ghost-free" date for his own move, so his mother gave him a fallback instead: carry a rice cooker full of rice into the new home ahead of time, since that alone can count as "moving in". Her other advice was to scatter uncooked red beans or salt through the house first to keep ghosts away, and to pick up sirutteok, a steamed red-bean rice cake, because red beans are believed to chase spirits off. Yoon skipped handing the rice cake out to his new neighbors this time, saying the old customs are fading among people his age.

My take If you're moving to Korea for the first time, the superstition that actually touches your life isn't the fan or the red pen. It's the lunar calendar your landlord or movers are quietly working around. Nobody will judge you for booking an ordinary Tuesday instead of a "ghost-free" date, and nobody will explain the price gap unless you ask about it directly. Skip the ritual if it doesn't mean anything to you: an ordinary date is one of the easiest ways to bring a quote down. Save the rice-cooker-first tradition for whenever it means something to someone actually in the room.

FAQ
Do younger Koreans actually believe any of this?
Belief looks mixed, but the underlying anxiety doesn't fully disappear. Chung-Ang University folklore professor Im Jang-hyuk has said visits to fortune-tellers tend to rise during economic downturns and elections, which suggests the impulse behind these customs outlasts literal belief in any one of them.
Is there a superstition that actually affects how a home gets sold or rented?
Yes. Some Korean real estate agents hang a pair of scissors on a listing's front door, a folk belief meant to help the home sell or rent quickly. People who tried it said online that sales closed within a week or a month afterward.

None of this is enforced anywhere, and nobody is going to stop you from taking the stairs to a floor labeled "F" or writing a note in red pen. The habits stick around anyway, in a timer dial on a fan, a blank gate number at the airport, a moving company fully booked on a date you've never heard of. If you're renting or moving here, ask your landlord or mover whether the date you want is a son-eobneun-nal before you lock it in.
