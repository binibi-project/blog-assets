By nine at night the alley behind Sindang Station smells like scorched sugar and chili before you've even found the sign. Steam fogs the plastic awnings, a pot rattles against a gas burner somewhere close, and someone's laugh carries over the hiss. This is the block where Korean tteokbokki is said to have become a dish people line up for, not just a street snack, and it still runs on the same gas-burner, cook-it-yourself ritual that built it.

What is tteokbokki (떡볶이)?

Cylindrical rice cakes stir-fried or simmered in a sauce built on gochujang (fermented chili paste). It started as a street snack; in a sit-down shop like the two below, it usually arrives as jeukseok-tteokbokki (즉석떡볶이), a raw pot you finish cooking yourself at the table.

🏺 Where did Seoul's tteokbokki actually start?

Most of the story traces back to one woman, Ma Bok-rim (마복림), and a 1953 accident at a Chinese restaurant near here. Seoul's own city media desk tells it this way: she dropped a piece of rice cake into a bowl of jajangmyeon, liked how it tasted with the black-bean sauce, and started mixing gochujang with chunjang to stir-fry rice cakes from a street stall of her own. Gas stoves spread through the area in the 1970s, which let shops cook the dish table-side instead of at a cart, and by the 1980s enough of them had opened nearby to form the tteokbokki town that still stands.

Ma Bok-rim died in 2011 at 91, and her original shop is still running a few blocks from Woojeong and Minine, now kept going by her family. A Dong-a Ilbo report from July 2026 found that shop's staff still fielding more foreign visitors than ever, helped along by Korean food clips spreading online, even as the wider neighborhood turns into a nickname locals call "힙당동" (Hip-dang-dong) for its new cafes and bars. The tteokbokki town is one old thread in a neighborhood that's otherwise changing fast around it.

🌙 Woojeong (우정): the one that runs past midnight

Woojeong (우정 즉석떡볶이) is the later-closing of the two, open from noon until 02:30 the next morning on every day except Wednesday. It sits at 서울 중구 퇴계로76길 55, inside the tteokbokki-town block itself, and its listed phone is 02-2233-0669.

The shop's own menu, as listed on its Naver Map page, runs a shared jeukseok-tteokbokki pot for two at ₩17,000 (about $12.73), stepping up to ₩21,000 for three and ₩26,000 for four. A cheese version is ₩26,000 and a jjajang (black-bean) version is ₩17,000, and the other half of the menu is chicken feet: ₩10,000 for either the mild or spicy style.

What is dakbal (닭발)?

Grilled or braised chicken feet, usually tossed in a fiery gochujang glaze. It's a late-night, soju-paired side in shops like this one, ordered alongside the tteokbokki rather than instead of it.

Order by pointing or saying "즉석떡볶이 2인 주세요" (jeukseok-tteokbokki i-in juseyo, "instant tteokbokki for two, please"). Google Maps lists Woojeong at 4.1 from 650 reviews, checked 2026-10-30.

🍢 Minine (미니네): the earlier opener, one door down

Minine (미니네) opens earliest of the two, 11:00 to 01:00, and rests only on Mondays. Its address is 서울 중구 다산로 219-1, close enough to Woojeong that the two shops sit on the same short stretch of the alley. The phone number on its Naver Map listing is 02-2232-4425.

Minine's menu prices a basic shared set for three at ₩23,000 (about $17.23) and for four at ₩32,000, built around tteokbokki (₩4,000 on its own), fish cake, jjolmyeon noodles and ramyeon added as extras. It also serves chicken feet: ₩10,000 with bone, ₩15,000 boneless.

Say "3인 세트 주세요" (sam-in set juseyo, "the three-person set, please") if you're a small group. Google Maps lists Minine at 4.2 from 157 reviews, checked 2026-10-30.

Neither shop turned up in a search of Korean TV food programs (SBS's The Master of Living, tvN's Wednesday Food Talk and others); both earn their place here on reviews and rating alone, not a broadcast appearance.

On paper Minine looks pricier, but it's also built for one more person: split ₩23,000 three ways and it undercuts Woojeong's ₩17,000 two-person pot per head.

To get there, take Sindang Station on Line 2 or Line 6 and leave by Exit 8, which Seoul's subway system marks as the stop's own tteokbokki-town exit. From there, turn left at the first corner and walk about two blocks; the signs and the smell do the rest of the work. Both shops sit inside that same block, close enough to each other that you can stand outside one and see the other's sign.

There may not be an English menu at either shop, so screenshot the Korean names before you go. If the counter looks confusing, just point at the number on the menu board that matches your group size.

My take A shared pot sounds romantic right up until you're standing there alone, wondering whether to order the two-person size anyway. Travel with just one other person and I'd book Woojeong's smaller pot over splitting Minine's three-person set; bring a third, and Minine's math already works in your favor. One more thing worth planning around: eat first and drink after. A tongue already stinging from gochujang makes the soju that follows a rougher ride, not a smoother one.

FAQ
Will a foreign credit card work at either shop?
Carry cash. Neither listing names a card reader, just Zero Pay, a local mobile-QR system most foreign-issued wallets can't open. Small bills save you from asking a street stall to break a large note.
Should we brace for the kind of line a famous food alley usually brings?
Probably not. Unlike spots that blew up overnight from one broadcast segment, these two built their standing the slow way, through ordinary diners leaving ratings across several years rather than a single viral clip. A weekday evening should mean a table with no wait.

The alley rewards showing up hungry and in no hurry, since the pot keeps cooking at your table long after it's served. Between the two, Woojeong's hours give you the most room to work with on a packed Seoul day. Prices and hours were checked on 2026-10-30; they change, so double-check before you go.
