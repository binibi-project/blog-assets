Seolleongtang in Seoul: Where Locals Actually Eat It

Three Seoul seolleongtang shops rated 4.0+ on Google Maps: the city's oldest restaurant in Insadong, plus two Mapo kitchens a subway ride apart.

Quick answer
  • Three shops, three eras: a 1900s original in Insadong, plus two Mapo kitchens from 1949 and 1977, all rated 4.0 or better on Google Maps.

  • Order by name, not by guessing: say "설렁탕" clearly, since it's easy to mix up with the similar-looking gomtang on the same kind of menu.

By eight on a weekday morning, the alley behind Seoul's old central post office already smells like boiled bone, and the stools inside are full before most of the street has opened its shutters. Nobody at the counter is taking photos of the bowl in front of them. It's cloudy and plain-looking, closer to dishwater than anything that belongs on a menu cover, and that's sort of the whole point. A shop that's served the same bowl since before Korea had paved roads in this neighborhood doesn't need to dress it up anymore.

What is seolleongtang (설렁탕)?

Ox bones and beef, with organ meat sometimes added, boiled for hours into a milky white broth and ladled over rice with wheat noodles. It arrives nearly flavorless; salt and pepper, plus a pile of scallions on the table, are how you finish seasoning it yourself.

The most common origin story ties the dish to Seonnongdan, the altar northeast of the old city walls where Joseon kings prayed for a good harvest and then plowed a token furrow themselves to show farming mattered. One account from King Sejong's reign says a sudden rain trapped the royal party there, and the ox used in the ceremony was boiled whole in plain water to feed the hungry officials, with the broth later called seonnongtang and then seolnongtang before the pronunciation drifted to today's seolleongtang. Seoul itself treats the story as the leading theory rather than settled fact, which is honest, since no Joseon-era document actually names the dish.

🏛️ Imun Seolleongtang: the city's oldest bowl, in Insadong

Start in Insadong at 이문설농탕 (Imun Seolleongtang), registered as Seoul's first licensed restaurant, and order the ₩15,000 (about $11.17) bowl. The address is 서울 종로구 우정국로 38-13, a short walk from the alleys around Jonggak Station, though the exact exit isn't confirmed. A fourth generation of the same family now runs the counter.

Sources don't agree on exactly when it opened: Seoul's Future Heritage registry and a 2012 business magazine both say 1904, while a 2017 Seoul Shinmun column gives 1905. What every account agrees on is the "first licensed" claim: the shop is registered as the first restaurant Seoul city ever issued a food-service permit to. Marathon gold medalist Sohn Kee-chung was once a regular.

It's open 08:00-21:00 Monday to Saturday, with a break from 15:00 to 17:00, and closes at 20:00 on Sundays with no break; the shop also closes on public holidays. The regular bowl is ₩15,000, the larger size is ₩18,000, and a hanwoo (Korean beef) version runs ₩20,000. Google Maps lists it at 4.0 from 2,645 reviews, checked today.

What's the difference between seolleongtang (설렁탕) and gomtang (곰탕)?

Both are milky white, which is why visitors mix them up. Seolleongtang traditionally simmers bone, organ meat and trimmings together and comes with noodles; gomtang is usually just beef meat simmered in water, served over rice alone. A shop that calls its dish gomtang, even one famous for a very similar-looking bowl, isn't technically serving seolleongtang.

🍚 Mapo-ok: the Michelin bowl by Mapo Station

Near Mapo Station, order the ₩19,000 (about $14.15) hanwoo yangji-tang (Korean beef brisket soup) at 마포옥 (Mapo-ok), open since 1949. The address is 서울 마포구 토정로 312, 2nd floor. Unlike the bone-heavy bowls elsewhere on this list, Mapo-ok leans almost entirely on beef brisket and beef trimmings for its broth, which is why regulars describe it as richer and less gamey than a classic bone-only seolleongtang.

It carries a Michelin Guide Seoul Bib Gourmand, the guide's tier for good food at a reasonable price, and it's also on Seoul's Future Heritage list of old restaurants. It's open 07:00-22:00 and runs year-round with no closed day. A children's-size bowl is ₩15,000, the regular hanwoo yangji-tang is ₩19,000, and the "premium" version is ₩26,000. Google Maps rates it 4.2 from 1,560 reviews, checked today.

🌙 Mapo Yangji Seolleongtang: the 1977 kitchen that moved to Gongdeok

Near Gongdeok Station in Dohwa-dong, order the ₩14,000 (about $10.43) bowl at 마포양지설렁탕 (Mapo Yangji Seolleongtang). A Seoul Shinmun column places it specifically in the Dohwa-dong blocks by Gongdeok Station, and notes the shop actually started near Mapo Station before moving a short distance to its current spot. The address is 서울 마포구 새창로 6, 1st floor, though the exact exit isn't confirmed.

The Seoul Shinmun dates it to 1977, while a Michelin Guide feature on seolleongtang gives 1974, so call it about 49-52 years running either way. Esquire Korea's write-up calls it a shop with "48 years of tradition" behind a surprisingly tidy storefront, and singles out the broth for having no gamy smell and a slightly thick body, plus side dishes (including scallion kimchi) good enough that diners order extra to take home. A regular bowl is ₩14,000 and the larger size is ₩18,000. Google Maps rates it 4.1 from 1,968 reviews, checked today.

What is kkakdugi (깍두기)?

Cubed radish kimchi, fermented with the same chili-and-garlic seasoning as regular kimchi but crunchier. It's the standard side dish at almost every seolleongtang counter, and its sour, salty bite is what cuts through the broth's richness.

On paper all three bowls look like the same pale soup, but Mapo-ok's menu is built almost entirely around beef brisket rather than the bone-and-organ mix the other two use, so it eats closer to a rich beef broth than a classic bone-heavy seolleongtang. If you only want one style, decide whether you want "classic bone broth" or "rich beef broth" before you pick a stop, not after.

Say "설렁탕 하나 주세요" (seol-leong-tang ha-na ju-se-yo, "one seolleongtang, please") at any of the three. If a server asks whether you want it 특 (special, more meat) or the regular size, "보통으로 주세요" (bo-tong-eu-ro ju-se-yo) gets you the smaller bowl listed in this guide. None of the three has a confirmed English menu, so screenshot the Korean name before you go.

A short menu board with a cut of meat you don't recognize is its own kind of stress.

My take If a short, unfamiliar menu board makes you nervous, start at Imun. It's the plainest option of the three, closest to a textbook bowl, and over a century of regulars have already worked out every way it can go wrong so you don't have to. Save Mapo-ok for when you want something richer and don't mind paying ₩19,000 for it. Mapo Yangji is the easy one to fit into a longer Mapo afternoon, since it sits closest to a transit hub with four lines running through it.
Go deeper

  • What locals argue about Whether Mapo-ok's beef-brisket bowl even counts as "real" seolleongtang, or whether it's closer to a gomtang that borrowed the name. The broth and the bone are what usually settle the argument, not the sign out front.
  • Watch for Break times. Imun closes its doors from 15:00 to 17:00, which is easy to forget if you're used to the other two staying open straight through the afternoon.
FAQ
Visitors sometimes compare this to Vietnamese pho. Is that fair?
Barely, beyond the pale color. Pho simmers star anise and cinnamon straight into its stock, over thin rice vermicelli. A Seoul bowl holds neither spice, and wheat noodles play a minor role here, not the lead. One more split worth knowing before your spoon hits the broth: pho usually comes pre-seasoned, while a Seoul bowl stays deliberately bare until your own hand reaches for the shaker.
Can I call ahead to check a kitchen isn't closed for its break?
Yes. 02-733-6526 reaches the Insadong counter, 02-716-6661 the one by Mapo Station, and 02-716-8616 the one near Gongdeok. The first number is worth dialing most: it's the only kitchen on this route with a documented midday closure.

None of these three needs a trend behind it to stay full. A dish this plain survives a century only because enough people keep coming back for a bowl that asks nothing of them except to show up hungry.
