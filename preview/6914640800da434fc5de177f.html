By early evening the Myeongdong alley smells like gochujang caramelizing over a flame, sharp with garlic, the cabbage going soft and sweet in the sauce. Someone's spatula scrapes against an iron pan loud enough to hear from the next table. Banners of Winter Sonata's lead actors once hung along this same stretch, back when the drama put Chuncheon on every fan's map, and the alley has been feeding people long before that and long after. It's a short train ride from Seoul for a meal worth planning a whole afternoon around.

The dish traces to 1960, when a pork galbi cook in central Chuncheon swapped in chicken after pork got hard to source, according to Maeil Shinmun's history of the alley, and the city built an entire identity, now a tourism campaign too, around what came next.

What is dakgalbi (닭갈비)?

Boneless chicken marinated in gochujang and garlic, stir-fried with cabbage, sweet potato and rice cake on a wide iron plate at the table. The name borrows "galbi" from grilled short ribs, though the dish itself has no ribs in it.

🍗 Three dakgalbi shops locals actually queue for

Start with 명동우미닭갈비 (Myeongdong Woomi Dakgalbi), one of the four shops that opened Myeongdong alley in 1968. It's at 강원특별자치도 춘천시 영서로 2345, rated 4.3 on Google Maps from 1,068 reviews, checked 2026-11-06. A Chuncheon food feature puts dakgalbi here at ₩16,000 a serving and makguksu at ₩9,000, reported October 13, 2026. Esquire Korea separately dates this branch's own run to 1970.

It's open daily 10:00-20:50 with no listed break. Esquire singles out the fried rice, stir-fried right on the emptied pan until the bottom turns crisp and scorched.

Order with "닭갈비 일 인분 주세요" (dak-gal-bi il-in-bun ju-se-yo, "one serving of dakgalbi, please"). Phone 033-255-1919.

What is makguksu (막국수)?

Buckwheat noodles in a cold, tangy sauce, Gangwon-do's answer to a hot pan of chicken. Most dakgalbi shops here sell it as a cooling second course, and a few locals argue it's the better order.

For the older style, go to 원조숯불닭불고기 (Wonjo Sutbul Dakbulgogi), an old-established house grilling chicken over charcoal, the cooking method that predates Myeongdong's iron-plate version. It's at 강원특별자치도 춘천시 낙원길 28-4, rated 4.2 from 1,769 reviews, checked 2026-11-06. Boneless dakgalbi (250g) and soy-sauce dakgalbi both run ₩15,000, bone-in dakgalbi ₩16,000, and makguksu ₩8,000, per a menu listing from October 2025.

It's open 10:30-21:00 and closes on Wednesdays. There's no table break listed, but with only six tables, reviewers describe real waits at peak hours, and some join a remote line through the Catchtable app before walking over.

Order with "숯불닭갈비 일 인분 주세요" (sut-bul-dak-gal-bi il-in-bun ju-se-yo, "one serving of charcoal dakgalbi, please"). Phone 0507-1362-5326.

The third stop, 춘천명물닭갈비 (Chuncheon Myungmul Dakgalbi), started in 1987 as a student hangout by Gangwon National University's back gate before moving to Samcheon-dong in 1990. It's at 강원특별자치도 춘천시 옛경춘로 813, rated 4.1 from 1,432 reviews, checked 2026-11-06. Its per-serving price is not confirmed; menu photos weren't available to check directly.

It's open 11:00-21:30, last order 20:30, closed Tuesdays. Phone 033-255-2069.

None of the three has a confirmed TV food-show appearance, though all three clear the 4.0-rating, 30-review bar on their own review counts alone.

🔥 Charcoal or iron plate: what locals argue about

The charcoal version came first. In 1971, an iron plate about 11mm thick started showing up in Myeongdong's kitchens, and vendors stir-fried chicken with cabbage, sweet potato and rice cake on it instead of grilling it over coals. The two styles still split the city: dakbulgogi on a wire mesh over charcoal, which locals treat as the original, and dakgalbi on the iron plate, which is what most visitors picture.

In the 1970s and '80s, a 150g portion cost just 100 to 500 won. It was cheap and filling enough that students and soldiers made it a staple, which is how the dish earned its nicknames "college-student galbi" and "common-person's galbi". That history is most of why a ₩16,000 plate still reads as a treat rather than fine dining, even now.

🚆 Getting to Chuncheon from Seoul

Take the ITX-Cheongchun from Cheongnyangni Station: ₩8,600 for an adult standard seat, about 65 minutes, with 18 departures on a weekday. The first weekday train leaves at 06:09 and the last at 23:07, per a timetable effective since May 2025. A separate schedule snapshot from a train-timetable aggregator dated June 2026 showed the same ₩8,600 fare and a similar 18-trip weekday count, so the price looks stable into this year.

The regular Gyeongchun Line commuter train also runs this route for ₩3,250, but takes about 95 minutes, half an hour longer than the ITX. The intercity bus from Dongseoul Terminal to Chuncheon Station costs ₩13,100 and takes about 2 hours 9 minutes, per a schedule page last updated November 1, 2026, making it both slower and pricier than either train option.

The ITX wins on both time and price, and it books out on weekend mornings, so grab a seat the moment your Chuncheon plan is set rather than at Cheongnyangni's platform.

One more budgeting note: the festival price isn't the real price. Chuncheon ran its Makguksu and Dakgalbi Festival along the Gongji-cheon waterside walkway from October 14 to 18, 2026, with 19 local shops (8 makguksu specialists, 11 dakgalbi specialists) selling dakgalbi at ₩9,900 and makguksu at ₩6,900. The city also handed out ₩3,000-off dakgalbi coupons from September 14 through October 25, 2026, usable at 87 offline and 64 online businesses.

Both programs have already ended. A regular dakgalbi serving at Myeongdong Woomi or Wonjo runs ₩15,000-16,000, about 50-60% more than the festival's ₩9,900. Next fall's festival should bring the discount back, but for a visit any other time of year, price your trip at the shop rates above.

My take Chuncheon's on the short list of Korean cities I've actually been to in the last two years, though that trip wasn't built around a dakgalbi crawl, so take this as a local's homework rather than my own verdict on all three. If I only had one meal in town, I'd start at the charcoal plate at Wonjo: it's the style that came first, and tasting it next to the iron-plate version most visitors already know makes the city's whole argument over "real" dakgalbi make sense. Save Myeongdong Woomi for when you have an extra hour to walk the alley itself; the street is half the reason people make the trip.
Go deeper

  • What locals argue about Charcoal versus iron plate. Chuncheon's own cooking-style history goes charcoal-grilled first, iron-plate stir-fry second, and plenty of natives still call the grilled version the only "real" dakgalbi.
  • Watch for The closing days. Wonjo rests on Wednesdays and Myungmul on Tuesdays, while Myeongdong Woomi stays open daily, so a single-day plan hinges on which shop you build it around.
FAQ
Is there an English menu?
Not confirmed anywhere in this lineup, so come prepared: screenshot what you plan to eat before you leave the hotel, then simply hold up your phone and nod instead of attempting the pronunciation cold.
Which weekday is safest for a spontaneous plan?
Monday, Thursday or Friday. Pencil in a midweek Tuesday or a hump-day Wednesday instead, and your spontaneous plan quietly loses a venue before you've even left the platform.

A train ticket, a short walk, and a pan that's still sizzling when it hits your table: that's most of what a Chuncheon dakgalbi trip actually requires. Go to Wonjo if you want the dish's own backstory on your plate, and to Myeongdong Woomi if you want the alley that gave the city its name for it.
