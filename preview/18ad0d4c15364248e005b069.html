National Museum of Korea: Free Entry, Hours, What to See

Free admission may end in 2027: the walk from Ichon Station, Wed/Sat night hours, the pagoda, and the Room of Quiet Contemplation.

Quick answer
  • Free right now, not guaranteed forever: the permanent halls and Children's Museum cost nothing today, but the government is weighing admission fees starting in 2027.
  • Go Wednesday or Saturday evening if you can: those two days stay open until 21:00, four hours later than the rest of the week.
  • Take Exit 2 at Ichon Station: follow the underground Museum Nadeulgil passage on a wet day, no need to surface until you're inside.

Wednesday evening, a little past seven, and the crowd that filled the Open Plaza at noon has mostly gone home. Walk in from the underground passage and the first thing in view is the Ten-Story Pagoda from Gyeongcheonsa Temple, carved stone nearly six centuries old, standing three floors tall under a skylight gone dark outside. Somewhere upstairs, behind a door with no sign, a room built to smell faintly of cinnamon is waiting, and it holds exactly two things.

That's the pitch for coming here at all: a museum big enough to lose an afternoon in, free enough that losing it costs nothing, at least for now.

🚇 How much does a visit actually cost, and is it really free?

Free, for the moment: permanent exhibition halls, the Children's Museum, and any free special exhibition cost nothing, while paid special exhibitions are ticketed on their own. The museum's own hours page lists Monday, Tuesday, Thursday, Friday, Sunday at 09:30-17:30 with last admission at 17:00, then stretches Wednesday and Saturday to 09:30-21:00, last admission 20:30. It closes January 1, Seollal, Chuseok, plus the first Monday of four months, March, June, September or December; the next one lands December 7, 2026.

Getting there is simple. Take subway Line 4 or the Gyeongui-Jungang Line to Ichon Station, come out Exit 2, and the museum's own directions put the walk at about 150 meters toward Yongsan Family Park. An elevator sits near Exits 1 and 2, and on a rainy day the underground Museum Nadeulgil passage runs straight from the station to the museum grounds, open 07:00 to 23:00. Blue Bus 400 and 502 both stop at "National Museum of Korea, Yongsan Family Park," useful if the subway doesn't reach your starting point.

That table adds up to a ₩6,101 (about $4.57) day, and nearly half of it is coffee, not entry. The free part of that math has a shelf life, though.

The museum dropped its ₩2,000 adult ticket in May 2008 to pull in more visitors, and it worked: 2025 brought more than 6.5 million people through the doors, the highest total since the museum opened in 1945. The first half of 2026 alone was already up 39.7% year over year.

On March 30, 2026, the Ministry of Planning and Budget said it was "seriously considering" charging admission again from 2027, part of a wider push to raise long-flat fees at palaces and museums. A foreigners-only fee was floated and dropped, since foreign visitors made up just 3.7% of the total in 2024.

🗿 What's worth seeing if you only have one visit?

Start with the two objects every local recommends first: the Ten-Story Pagoda just past the entrance, and the Room of Quiet Contemplation one floor up. Walk through the Open Plaza doors and the pagoda is the first thing in your eyeline, a ten-story tower of carved marble reaching up through the Great Hall. Think of it as a 14th-century stone diary: an inscription on its first story dates it to 1348, carved in the fourth year of Goryeo's King Chungmok, with scenes from Buddhist legend worked into panel after panel.

It's National Treasure No. 86, and the detail that turns it from pretty to personal is this: Japan's minister of the Imperial Household had it dismantled and shipped out of Korea in 1907, and it only came home in 1918, after journalists in Korea and abroad kept the theft in print. On Wednesday and Saturday nights, a 12-minute light show called "Lighting the Sky Pagoda" projects those same carved stories back onto the stone, at 19:00 from October through February and 20:00 the rest of the year.

What changed about National Treasure numbers in 2021?

Korea's heritage agency dropped the old numbering, including No. 86, on November 19, 2021, because people kept reading the numbers as a ranking of importance rather than a filing system. English guides, including the museum's own site, still use the old numbers since there's nothing simpler to replace them with.

One floor up, the Room of Quiet Contemplation holds exactly two objects: both are gilt-bronze Pensive Bodhisattva statues, each sitting with one ankle crossed over the opposite knee and a fingertip just touching the cheek. National Treasure No. 78 dates to the late sixth century and No. 83 to the early seventh, both from the Three Kingdoms period, and the right-hand statue stands 93.5 centimeters tall.

Korea.net's feature on the room describes a star-patterned ceiling and red clay walls that give off a faint scent of cinnamon and cypress, with no labels or display cases at all, just a curator's claim that it's a room where "visual, olfactory and auditory senses are integrated". A QR code at the entrance answers anything you're curious about, so skipping the guided tour costs you nothing here.

🌇 Mirror Pond, the Children's Museum, and the rest of your day

Step outside after the galleries and Mirror Pond is the payoff: an oval pond up to 120 meters across, with a celadon-roofed pavilion in the middle and the Great Hall reflected whole on a still day. Climb the stairs at the Open Plaza entrance after dark and there's a well-known photo angle that lines up N Seoul Tower behind the building, the kind of shot that makes a free afternoon feel like a proper outing.

What is Cheongjajeong (청자정)?

The pavilion in the middle of Mirror Pond, built in 2009 with a roof tiled in celadon green to echo Goryeo-era pottery. It's a modern addition rather than a historic structure, there purely to give the view back toward the museum something to frame.

If kids are along, reserve a free ticket for the Children's Museum before you go. It runs five 80-minute sessions a day, each capped at 320 people, with exhibits built mainly for ages 5 to 9 and a separate play area for children 36 months and under. Free guided tours in English run weekdays at 10:30 and 13:00, and weekends add a Japanese tour at the same times plus a Chinese tour at 13:00, all meeting at the Korean Empire exhibit on the first floor.

Hungry partway through, there's a small cafe near the Room of Quiet Contemplation that leans into the theme: its menu has included a persimmon glutinous rice cake and a baked snack shaped around the pensive pose itself. A food court on the first floor runs a wider Korean lineup, with tteokbokki alongside soups and rice bowls.

My take A free museum is easy to treat as optional, the plan you cut first once the day runs short because nothing was paid for in advance. Go on a Wednesday or Saturday evening instead of a weekend afternoon, and give the Room of Quiet Contemplation the fifteen minutes it's built for before deciding the rest of the museum is skippable. If a 2027 ticket price really does land, the smart version of "free" is the one you use this year, not the one you keep meaning to get to.

FAQ
Is it stroller- or wheelchair-friendly?
Yes. Visit Seoul's own listing notes accessible parking and elevators on site, plus wheelchair and stroller rentals available at the information desk for the day.
Is there anything to do outside once galleries wear thin?
Yes, and it rewards even a quick lap. The grounds hold wooded paths and a small waterfall, with gardens along the way, a change of pace before heading back in.

Go on a weekday evening if your schedule bends at all, and treat the Room of Quiet Contemplation as the one stop not worth trading away for a faster lap. Prices and hours were checked on 2026-10-30; they change, so double-check before you go.
