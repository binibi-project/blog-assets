How to Make Andong Jjimdak (Korean Braised Chicken)

A real Andong jjimdak recipe with gram measurements, the dried chilies that stand in for gochujang, and where to find dangmyeon noodles outside Korea.

Quick answer
  • No gochujang in this one: Andong jjimdak gets its color and heat from dried whole chilies and soy sauce, not chili paste.
  • One chicken feeds four: a single cut-up bird plus potatoes and dangmyeon noodles stretch it into a full dinner, the way it was built to.
  • Buy the noodles ahead: order dangmyeon online before you shop for everything else, since it's the one ingredient a regular supermarket won't carry.

Most Andong jjimdak recipes written for cooks outside Korea reach for a spoonful of gochujang before anything else goes in the pot, which skips the one choice the dish was actually built around. In the old market alley in Andong, the smell over a bubbling pot is soy, garlic and charcoal, not the sharp red heat that chili paste gives tteokbokki a few stalls over. The sauce goes dark and glossy instead, sweet before it turns spicy, built to coat a whole cut-up chicken without burying what's underneath. A visitor smells chili and assumes gochujang, but what's actually simmering is a handful of dried whole red chilies, which cook out fast and leave the soy sauce in charge of the flavor, so getting that one swap right at home is most of the work.

What's the difference between jjimdak (찜닭) and dakbokkeumtang (닭볶음탕)?

Both are chicken braised with potato and vegetables, but dakbokkeumtang leans on gochujang and gochugaru for a red, spicy sauce, while Andong-style jjimdak is soy-based and gets its heat from whole dried chilies instead. Jjim just means steamed, stewed or braised in sauce, and dak means chicken.

🍗 Why doesn't Andong jjimdak use gochujang?

Because the dish was built in the 1970s and 80s around stretching one chicken, not around chili paste. Andong's old traditional market had a chicken alley that mostly sold fried whole chicken, and in lean years cooks there looked for a way to feed a group from a single bird. Potatoes and vegetables went into the pot to stretch the portion further, along with soaked dangmyeon noodles, and the whole thing simmered in a soy-based sauce and was often served on a shared tray big enough for a table of university students or soldiers on leave to empty between them.

The dish got popular enough locally that the alley's name changed from tongdak golmok (fried-chicken alley) to jjimdak golmok (jjimdak alley). It stayed a regional specialty until the early 2000s, when the Bongchu jjimdak chain turned it into a nationwide hit out of Seoul. About 30 restaurants still cook it side by side in the old market's jjimdak alley today, each with its own small differences on the same base.

Oyster sauce can drop out in favor of extra soy sauce, and the sweetener can shift from corn syrup toward rice syrup or honey depending on what's already in the house. Order the dangmyeon before you shop for anything else: a 12 oz bag runs $3.79 online, enough for about four batches of this recipe, so it's a one-time errand rather than a weekly one.

What is dangmyeon (당면)?

Noodles made from sweet potato starch, sold dried and soaked before cooking. They turn chewy and glassy-clear once cooked, which is why English-language recipes often just call them glass noodles.

🍲 How to make it, and where it usually goes wrong

Start the noodles soaking before you touch the chicken, since everything else moves faster than they do. Soak the dangmyeon in warm water for about 30 minutes, then drain well.

  1. Cut the chicken into small pieces and trim the excess fat.

  2. Cut the potatoes into roughly 1.5-inch chunks and leave them in water so they don't brown. Cut the carrot and onion the same way, and do the same with the mushrooms too.

  3. Put the chicken in a large pot with 3 cups (710 ml) of water and every braising-liquid ingredient except the sesame oil and sesame seeds. Boil uncovered over high heat for about 10 minutes, skimming off the foam as it rises.

  4. Add the potatoes and the dried chilies to the pot, along with the carrot and onion. Stir in the mushrooms, then add the garlic and ginger. Cover and cook for 8-10 minutes; there will still be a lot of liquid, which the potatoes and noodles soak up later.

  5. Stir in the fresh chili (if using), scallions and drained noodles. Cook uncovered for 3 more minutes, then stir in the sesame oil and finish with the sesame seeds.

You can stop after step 4, hold the pot, and finish with step 5 right before serving, which is useful if guests are running late.

The sauce usually goes wrong at the very end, not at the start. If it's thinner than you want after step 5, adding more syrup only makes it sweeter, not thicker; the fix is more noodles or potatoes, a longer simmer, or a small cornstarch slurry stirred in off the heat.

Boneless thighs are the easiest swap for a whole cut-up chicken, but even the recipe's own author calls them "not the best," since bone-in pieces render more fat into the broth and carry more flavor through the long simmer. The noodles are the part most likely to go soft and clumpy if you're not watching the clock. A 30-minute soak already does most of the softening, so three minutes in the pot at the very end is enough; walk away during that step and they'll turn to mush by the time you plate it.

My take I ate Andong jjimdak in Andong itself on a September 2026 trip with friends, right after a morning spent walking through a few of the city's old seowon. What stuck with me wasn't the heat, which was milder than the sauce's deep red-brown color suggests, but how the dried chilies disappear into the broth instead of sitting on top of it the way gochugaru flakes do in most other Korean stews. If you're cooking this at home for the first time, start with the lower end of the chili count. You can always add more next time, and a pot that's too spicy on the first try is a harder sell to anyone else at the table than one that needed one more chili.

FAQ
Does jjimdak have to be this spicy, or is there a milder home-style version?
Plenty of households skip the heat almost entirely. The recipe's author says her own mild version reminds her of the chicken her mother used to braise at home, back when the family kept and raised its own birds; one of her brothers stopped eating chicken altogether after those years. If yours turns out gentler than a restaurant plate, that's closer to how plenty of Korean kitchens actually make it.
What's the fastest fix if the color comes out too light?
A single spoonful solves it. Some cooks stir in instant coffee, caramel syrup or dark soy near the finish, purely to push the shade a few tones deeper. A paler pot isn't a mistake; it's simply the plainer, honest result of leaving that spoonful out.

Skip the gochujang, trust the dried chilies, and don't try to rescue a thin sauce with more syrup. Make it once at the milder chili count before deciding how much heat earns a permanent spot in your version of the recipe.
