More than a century of frying oil has a smell, and Gwangjang Market has it by nine in the morning, well before the crowd that came for a Netflix episode shows up. Steam off the grills mixes with sesame oil and the flat, mineral smell of raw beef being sliced two stalls over. Under the market's tin roof, a woman who has flipped the same pancake for decades doesn't look up when a line forms behind her.

Opened in 1905 as Korea's first permanent market, the place has outlasted colonial rule, a war and a hundred newer food courts. Seoul's own city government page still introduces the market through the same three dishes locals actually come for: mung bean pancakes, mayak gimbap and raw beef. Everything else, the fabric stalls, the hanbok tailors on the second floor, came later.

The three stops below aren't the loudest carts, and none of them is hard to find once you know the name. They're the ones locals still queue for after the tour groups have moved on to lunch somewhere with chairs.

What is bindaetteok (빈대떡)?

A savory pancake of ground mung beans, usually mixed with pork and bean sprouts and fried flat and crisp. It's older and heavier than the wheat-batter pajeon most menus abroad translate as "Korean pancake," and Gwangjang Market is where most Seoulites first think to get it.

🥞 Sunhee's: the pancake stall that only makes two things

Order the ₩6,000 mung bean pancake, plain or with the ₩4,000 meat patty on the side, because that's the entire menu. Sunhee's Bindaetteok (순희네 빈대떡) sits inside the market at 서울 종로구 종로32길 5, about three minutes from Exit 8 of Jongno 5-ga Station on Line 1. It's open daily 10:00 to 21:00, last order 20:30, with no listed weekly closure.

An assorted plate of two pancakes and one meat patty runs ₩16,000, and the stall also sells its own mayak gimbap at ₩5,000 a roll if you want to compare it against the specialist stall below. Order with "빈대떡 하나 주세요" (bin-dae-tteok ha-na ju-se-yo, "one pancake, please"). Whether an English menu exists is not confirmed, so having the Korean name ready helps.

🍙 Is the mayak gimbap stall worth hunting down?

Yes, but go by the phone number, not the name, since half the market sells a version of the same roll. The stall people mean when they say "the original" is a mother-daughter operation at 서울 종로구 동호로 403-23, about 99 meters from Exit 7 of Jongno 5-ga Station. It runs 09:00 to 21:00 daily and closes early once the day's rice is gone; its listed weekly closed day is not confirmed, so a Monday or Friday trip is not a sure thing either way.

What is mayak gimbap (마약김밥)?

Literally "drug gimbap," a thin, filling-light roll of rice and pickled radish dipped in a mustard-soy sauce. The name is a joke about how addictive it is, not a warning label, and it's the roll that made this market's food alley famous beyond Seoul.

A roll is ₩4,000, and the same stall sells rice-cake tteokbokki for ₩3,000 and a bowl of banquet noodles for ₩5,000 if you want a second dish. Ask for "마약김밥 주세요" (ma-yak gim-bap ju-se-yo).

🥩 Yukhoe Jamae-jip: the one dish that scares off first-timers

Order the ₩23,000 yukhoe if raw beef doesn't worry you, or the ₩11,000 yukhoe-over-rice if you want the same beef with training wheels. Yukhoe Jamae-jip's main branch (육회자매집 본점1호점) is at 서울 종로구 종로 200-4, 제우빌딩, inside the market's yukhoe alley, a short walk from Jongno 5-ga Station; the exact exit isn't confirmed on the shop's own listing, so use the market's general Exit 8 and ask for the yukhoe alley once inside. It's open 09:30 to 21:20 and closed every Monday.

What is yukhoe (육회)?

Raw beef, hand-cut into thin strips, dressed in sesame oil, sugar and garlic, and usually topped with a raw egg yolk and pear. It's Korea's answer to steak tartare, sold at its own dedicated alley inside Gwangjang Market rather than at a general restaurant.

This is the stall to save for the day you're already curious about raw beef, not the one to talk a nervous friend into. The pear underneath cuts the richness, and the egg yolk is meant to be mixed in, not eaten separately. Order with "육회 주세요" (yuk-hoe ju-se-yo).

The texture is the part that surprises first-timers, not the taste. It's closer to a cool, slightly sweet beef salad than to sashimi, and the sesame oil coats each strip so nothing tastes raw in the way people brace for. Eat it soon after it arrives; ten minutes in the open air and the pear starts to soften.

My take I eat out often in Gangnam, Samseong and Yeoksam, including places with a Michelin sticker on the door, so a ₩23,000 plate of yukhoe reads as inexpensive by that comparison, not cheap on its own terms. If I only had one stop to make, I'd take the mayak gimbap first since it travels well and costs the least to try, and save the yukhoe for whenever I'm hungry enough to want a full plate of it.

FAQ
Is Gwangjang Market's food alley only good early in the day?
No, but two of these three stalls can sell out or take a break before evening, so earlier is safer if you have your heart set on one dish.
Do I need to speak Korean to order?
No. Point at what you want and use the short phrases above; none of the three stalls has a confirmed English menu, but all three are used to tourists pointing at the case.

None of these three stalls need a blog post to stay busy, and they won't get any less crowded because of this one. Start with whichever is closest to your hotel or your day's plans, and treat the other two as reasons to come back.
