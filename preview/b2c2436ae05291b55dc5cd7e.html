Korea's Age Law: What Changed in 2023, What Didn't

Korea's 2023 law made actual age the legal default, but drinking, smoking, school entry and military service still run on the older year-based count.

Quick answer
  • Korea's legal age became "actual age" (만 나이) on June 28, 2023: the Civil Act and Administrative Basic Act now default to counting from your own birthday, not the first of January.
  • Most people's paperwork age dropped by one year, some by two: nothing about anyone's actual birth date changed, only the number written on forms.
  • Three things never moved: drinking, smoking or serving in the military still run on your birth year, so check which age a line wants before you fill in a number.

Set a bowl of tteokguk in front of a Korean grandmother on New Year's morning and she'll ask the question she's asked every January 1 of her life: did you finish it? For generations, that bowl of sliced rice cake in broth was how a person turned a year older, whether their actual birthday landed in March or November. There's something strangely moving about an entire country aging together on the same morning, millions of people sharing one birthday. Then, in the middle of 2023, the law stopped agreeing with the custom, and Korea quietly became a country running two different ages on the same calendar.

The confusion didn't come out of nowhere. For decades, three separate ways of counting a person's age ran side by side across Korea, from government paperwork to playgrounds to bar entrances, and almost nobody agreed on which one applied where.

🎂 Why did Korea have three ages in the first place?

Korea ran three separate age systems at once for decades, and each one answered a different question. The oldest is 세는나이, counting age: a baby is already 1 at birth, and everyone gains a year together every January 1, regardless of when they were actually born. The gap this creates is sharp. A baby born on December 31 could turn 2 years old the very next morning, a detail Korean reporters have used for years to explain the system to confused readers.

What is 세는나이 (counting age)?

A person is 1 at birth, and everyone gains a year together on January 1, no matter their actual birthday. It's the age you hear in casual conversation and K-dramas, and the one the 2023 law didn't touch.

The second system, 만나이 (actual age), starts a newborn at 0 and adds a year on their own birthday, the way most of the world counts age. The oddest part of this story is that 만나이 was already Korea's official legal standard, written into the Civil Code back in 1962; daily life simply never followed it.

What is 만나이 (actual age)?

Age counted from 0 at birth, adding a year on each individual birthday. This is the international standard, and since June 2023 it's also Korea's legal default almost everywhere, from contracts to forms to most of the civil code.

That's also why a bowl of tteokguk carries so much weight at New Year. Eating it was treated as the moment a person officially aged under the counting system, so much so that people still ask each other "did you eat your rice cake soup?" to mean "did you turn a year older?". The custom outlived the confusion it helped cause.

📜 What exactly changed on June 28, 2023?

On June 28, 2023, actual age became Korea's single legal default, and most people's paperwork age dropped by one year, some by two. The Ministry of Government Legislation wrote the calculation and display rules directly into both the Civil Act and the Administrative Basic Act, closing a decades-old gap between what the law said and what people actually did. Anyone whose birthday hadn't arrived yet that year lost two years at once; everyone else lost one.

None of this touched a single person's actual birth date. It just changed which number goes on a contract, a hospital form or a government ID, and it was designed to end the legal disputes that kept cropping up when one law meant counting age and another meant actual age for the exact same word, "나이".

The push had real public backing behind it. A Korea Research poll in December 2021 found 71% of 1,000 adults surveyed wanted the counting-age system scrapped in favor of actual age, against just 15% opposed, and the government's own review found 81.6% support once the bill was drafted.

🍺 What still runs on the old count?

Three things never switched over: buying alcohol or tobacco, the military fitness exam, and starting elementary school all still use an older, year-based age. That age is called 연나이, and it's simpler than it sounds: take the current year, subtract your birth year, and that's your number, no birthday required.

What is 연나이 (year age)?

Current year minus birth year, with no regard for whether your birthday has happened yet. A handful of laws, mainly around alcohol and tobacco sales or conscription, still use this number instead of actual age.

A government briefing quotes Justice Minister Lee Wan-gyu explaining why these laws were left alone: some systems "need to be run in yearly units," since sorting an entire school grade or an entire conscription class by exact birthday would be unworkable. The Youth Protection Act still defines a minor as anyone under 19 by 연나이, so the formula is current year minus 19. Run that for 2026 and it's 2007: anyone born in 2007 or earlier can legally buy alcohol or tobacco this year, whether or not their birthday has happened yet. Conscription fitness exams follow the identical formula and the identical cutoff year.

School entry works differently from the other two but still ignores exact birthdays: a child who turns 6 by actual age sometime during a given year starts first grade the following March 1, regardless of whether that birthday was in January or December.

None of this changed how classmates talk to each other, either. A birthday-based actual age means two kids in the same classroom can technically be different ages for a few months of the year, but Korean reporters covering the law were blunt about it: "친구끼리 호칭을 다르게 쓸 필요는 없다", there's no need for friends to start using different terms of address with each other. The law reset paperwork, not playground etiquette.

My take If you're a visitor trying to keep these three ages straight, stop trying to be precise about it in small talk and save the precision for paperwork. A visa form, a lease or a bank contract wants your actual-birthday age now, full stop. A cashier checking ID, a conscription office or a school enrollment form wants your birth year instead, and insisting on the "new" age won't change what their system asks for. Honestly, I'd just ask which one a form means before filling in a number, because plenty of Koreans still mix the three up too.
Go deeper
  • If you have one more question Ask before you write anything down. Most Korean paperwork already prints 만 나이 next to the blank, so a clerk will tell you outright which age a given line wants.
  • What locals argue about Whether the law can actually soften Korea's age-based hierarchy culture. A Korea University linguist, Shin Ji-young, has argued it can't: "나이에 따른 서열은 결국 언어에 영향을 받은 건데 사회적 합의인 언어 문제를 법으로 고치려는 건 잘못됐다", age-based rank comes from language habits, and a law can't fix a social-language problem.
  • Watch for More exceptions disappearing over time. The Ministry of Government Legislation says it's still reviewing individual laws that use 연나이, one by one, to see which ones can move to actual age next, though no date has been announced for any further changes.
FAQ
Does this change when I can collect Korea's national pension?
No. Pension law was tied to each person's own birthday long before the 2023 reform, so when retirees start drawing a payout hasn't shifted by a single day. The fix only mattered for the three exceptions covered above, not for retirement benefits.
Do I need a new resident registration card or driver's license now?
No reissue needed. Those cards print your birthdate, not a calculated age, and birthdates didn't change; only how officials read the word "age" in a law did. A card issued years before June 2023 still works exactly as before.

Three numbers for one person sounds like bureaucratic overkill, and in a lot of ways it is. But most of the confusion clears up once you remember the rule of thumb: paperwork wants your actual-birthday age now, the bar and the draft board want your birth year, and casual conversation barely changed at all. Next time someone in Korea asks your age, give the number on your ID and let them sort out which rule applies.
