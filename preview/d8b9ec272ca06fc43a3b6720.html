How to Make Tteokbokki at Home: A Real Recipe

A tteokbokki recipe in exact quantities, the gochujang and rice cakes you can order abroad, and the one mistake that turns rice cakes mushy.

One red sauce, a handful of ingredients from a Korean grocery store, twenty minutes on the stove: that's most of what separates a good bowl of tteokbokki from a hard, flavorless one. Done right, the sauce turns glossy and a little sticky, sweet before it's spicy, spicy a beat after that. Rice cake goes in stiff and comes out soft enough to bite through without resistance, and the first piece out of the pan is always hotter than it looks. It's built for chopsticks, a paper cup of the broth on the side, and no real plan to stop eating until the pan is empty.

Cooking it at home skips the line at the cart, and the only real skill it asks for is patience with the rice cakes themselves.

What is garaetteok (가래떡, ga-rae-tteok)?

Plain, chewy rice cake shaped into long cylinders. Cut into finger-length pieces, it's the "tteok" in tteokbokki, and the same cake sliced thin makes the rice cake soup eaten at Lunar New Year.

🍲 The recipe: tteokbokki for three

Twenty minutes of prep and twenty more on the stove get three servings on the table, and most of that second twenty is just simmering. This is Korean Bapsang's home version, rated 4.65 out of 5 stars by 370 people who've cooked it.

Ingredient Amount
Tteokbokki rice cake (garaetteok) 1 lb (450g), about 24 pieces
Fish cake (eomuk) sheets 1-2 sheets
Green cabbage 4 oz
Scallions 1-2 stalks
Garlic, minced 1 tbsp
Anchovy broth or water 3 cups
Gochujang 3 tbsp
Gochugaru 1-3 tsp
Soy sauce 1 tbsp
Sugar 2 tbsp
Corn syrup or oligo syrup 1 tbsp
Boiled eggs (optional) 2

Serves three, and the sauce measurements above make a thicker, classic version rather than the extra-saucy style some stalls serve. If you want more broth to spoon over the rice cakes at the end, the same recipe scales the liquid up to 4 cups without changing anything else.

What is gochujang (고추장, go-chu-jang)?

Fermented Korean chili paste made from red pepper, glutinous rice and fermented soybeans. It's sweeter and thicker than the dry chili flakes called gochugaru, and it's what turns a pot of plain rice cakes into tteokbokki's glossy red sauce.

  1. Soak the rice cakes, about 20 minutes for fresh ones and longer for refrigerated or frozen ones, then cut the cabbage and scallions and slice the fish cake into roughly 2-inch pieces.

  2. Bring the anchovy broth or water to a boil, then stir in the gochujang, gochugaru and soy sauce, followed by the sugar and syrup, until the gochujang dissolves completely.

  3. Add the rice cakes and boil until they're soft and the sauce has thickened, about 8-10 minutes, stirring often so nothing sticks to the bottom.

  4. Stir in the cabbage and garlic, then add the fish cake, and keep it going another 4-6 minutes.

  5. Add the scallions and the boiled eggs, if using, with 2-3 minutes left, then taste and adjust before taking the pan off the heat.

🌶️ Where tteokbokki usually goes wrong

The rice cakes fail before the sauce ever does. Refrigerated and frozen rice cakes dry out and go hard, and the fix is simply more time in water before they touch the pan: "some refrigerated (and frozen) rice cakes are very dry and hard. Try soaking longer if yours are like that". Skip that step and you get rice cakes with a raw, chalky core no amount of extra simmering fixes.

The second failure point is leftovers, not the cooking itself. Tteokbokki left in the fridge overnight and reheated in a microwave turns tough and rubbery; the fix is a saucepan instead: "your best option is to simmer the leftovers in a saucepan, covered, with some water or broth over medium low heat". If you want a cleaner-tasting, spicier sauce on the next batch, the same recipe offers a leaner ratio: 2 tablespoons gochujang, 2 tablespoons finely ground gochugaru and 1.5 tablespoons soy sauce, no sugar or syrup added.

What is eomuk (어묵, eo-muk)?

Korean fish cake, ground white fish pressed into sheets or skewers and simmered rather than fried a second time at home. It brings a chewy, savory contrast to the sweet-spicy sauce, and it's sold pre-cooked, so it only needs to heat through.

My take I don't cook much at home, so tteokbokki for me has always meant whichever cart or bunsik counter is closest, not a pot on my own stove. If I were making this for the first time, the step I'd take most seriously is the one before the pan even goes on the heat: soaking the rice cakes properly, since a hard, undercooked rice cake ruins the dish in a way no amount of sauce fixes afterward. If a friend abroad asked me for one Korean recipe to start with, this is the one I'd send, mostly because it forgives almost everything except that.

  • Watch for The sauce thickening too fast once the rice cakes go in. Keep the heat at a steady boil rather than high, and stir often so the bottom doesn't scorch before the rice cakes soften.
Check before you go
  • Soak the rice cakes first: refrigerated or frozen ones run dry and hard, and a longer soak fixes it before the pot ever goes on.
  • Buy the coarse gochugaru, not the fine kind meant for marinades, if you're adding extra heat beyond the gochujang.
  • Reheat leftovers in a covered saucepan, not the microwave, or the rice cakes turn tough.
FAQ
Can I make tteokbokki without gochujang?
Not easily. The paste carries the sauce's color and sweetness as well as its body, and even the leaner, spicier variation on this recipe still uses gochujang as its base, just with more gochugaru added on top.
Do I need fish cake, or can I leave it out?
You can leave it out. It's one of the two additions, along with cabbage, that most home versions include, and skipping it just makes for a plainer, all-rice-cake plate rather than a failed one.

This recipe survives almost any shortcut except a hard rice cake and a sauce rushed off the heat too early. Soak the tteok properly and let the sauce thicken on its own, and the rest of the pot mostly cooks itself. Start the rice before the pan goes on the stove, because tteokbokki doesn't wait well once it's ready.
