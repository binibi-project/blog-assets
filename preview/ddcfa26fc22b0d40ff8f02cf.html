It's close to midnight in Seoul and a fan in another time zone entirely is refreshing a notice page, waiting for a single line to appear: the version to buy, the window to buy it in, the date winners get told. She already knows the odds aren't good. She buys anyway, because the alternative is knowing for certain she wasn't in the drawing at all.

That purchase, repeated by thousands of fans at the same hour, is what a K-pop fansign actually runs on: not a ticket window, but a lottery dressed up as a pre-order.

What is a cutline (컷라인)?

Fan slang for the rough number of albums a buyer needs to have a realistic shot at a given group's fansign. It's a community estimate, not a published rule, and it moves with how popular the group is that week.

🎟️ How does a K-pop fansign lottery actually work?

Buying one copy of the right album during the entry window earns exactly one entry, and more copies buy more entries, not a better guarantee. Most fansigns run as a random drawing: a store or the agency's own shop sells a specific album version for a set period, and every unit sold in that window becomes one ticket in the pool. A smaller number of events, usually for newly debuted acts with a thinner fanbase, run first-come-first-served instead, where the first 100-150 buyers after a set time simply get a seat.

A 2025 fansign notice from BIGHIT MUSIC for TOMORROW X TOGETHER spells the lottery rule out in plain terms: one entry per album unit ordered in the window, not one per order, and cancelling a qualifying order cancels the entry that came with it. Only the product version the notice names as eligible counts. Buy the wrong version, including a digital-only release, and you're not in the drawing at all.

📋 What did a real 2025 fansign actually look like?

One hundred total winners, drawn from a three-week window, for TOMORROW X TOGETHER's fourth full album. Entries for the July 2025 event opened June 26 at 11:00 AM KST and closed July 19 at 11:59 PM KST.

Winners were announced two days later, on July 21 at 8:00 PM KST. The agency ran two separate draws off the same album, 50 winners for a video call fansign and 50 for an in-person one, and fans were allowed to enter both.

What is a video call fansign (영통팬싸)?

A fansign held over a live video call instead of a table and a pen. The member signs nothing; you get a set number of minutes, face to face on screen, and the call moves to the next winner the second the clock runs out.

A winner can't just send a friend in their place either. The notice requires the actual winner to show up with a valid photo ID that matches the entry, and it states plainly that entries can't be transferred.

💸 How many albums does it actually take, and what should you budget?

Nobody can tell you a real number, and that's the point: more albums raise your odds, but a single copy has won before, and a stack of 200 has lost. Back in 2016, Soompi gathered fan estimates for a handful of groups: a "minimum" of 15-20 albums for a shot at the full seven-member INFINITE, 30-40 for a smaller subgroup with fewer applicants, and for EXO, one fan who got a seat with a single album next to another who bought 200 and didn't. Those are fan-reported anecdotes from a specific moment nearly a decade ago, not a published formula, and today's cutlines move with whatever group and comeback you're chasing.

If the budget side of that worries you, that's a reasonable reaction, not an overreaction: a standard physical album in Korea has run upward of ₩20,000 (about $13 at ~₩1,500/$1) since at least 2020, and buying toward a cutline means multiplying that by however many copies the community guesses you'll need. Set a number you're willing to spend before you open the entry page, not after you've already bought five albums chasing a sixth.

One practical edge, if you're buying several: stores sometimes split their winner slots across locations, so ten albums at one store beats five at two different ones.

My take The wait outside a notice page at midnight is its own kind of stress, and the money worry underneath it is a reasonable one, not an overreaction. The math is the same wherever you live: you're not really betting on winning, you're betting on having something to show for the money either way, since the album is yours regardless of the draw. If I were setting a budget, I'd fix the number before entering, then treat any win as a bonus on top of albums worth buying anyway, not the reason to buy them.

FAQ
Forgetting the odds for a second, what trips people up logistically?
Age, mostly, for a relative buying under their own name. TXT's mid-2025 paperwork quietly set a birth-year cutoff tied to the performance date, old enough to knock out a younger sibling or cousin nobody thought to check. Forfeiture is the other trap: once picked, nobody else can step into that spot for you, no matter the excuse.
Can someone outside Korea realistically win one of these?
The pool doesn't care where a buyer lives, so yes, on the picking side. Soompi's reporting notes two things worth knowing: branches outside Seoul sometimes draw fewer local entrants, nudging the odds slightly, and several sellers took phone orders during the sale and mailed discs to buyers who couldn't show up in person to purchase. Getting on a plane between an announcement and a signing a few days later is usually the harder obstacle than the drawing itself.

There's no version of this where more money buys certainty, only better odds on a draw that still comes down to a number being picked or not. If you're going in for the first time, read the eligible-version line twice, set your spending limit before the entry page loads, and budget for the album in your hands either way, not the slot you might win.
