How to Make Bossam (Korean Boiled Pork Wraps) at Home

Korea Herald's 600-gram pork belly method, the doenjang-or-coffee debate, and why bossam is the dish Koreans braise on kimjang day.

Quick answer
  • One pot, 600 grams of pork belly: Korea Herald's recipe from food creator Cookim simmers it in salted, sugared water for about an hour, nothing more specialized than a stockpot.
  • Let it cool before you slice it: cutting into hot pork tears the slices instead of giving you clean, thin pieces you can actually wrap.
  • Act first: rest the boiled pork in its own liquid until it's just warm, not steaming, before the knife comes out.

Bossam gets filed under "kimjang-season side dish," and that undersells it. Boiled pork belly, sliced thin while it's still faintly warm, wrapped in a cold cabbage leaf with a spoonful of fresh, barely-fermented kimchi on top: it's one of the few Korean dishes built entirely around contrast, hot meat against a cold crunch, plain pork against a sauce that bites back. Nobody dresses it up. The whole appeal is that it doesn't need to be.

It also travels well past kimjang. A plate of sliced pork, a pile of lettuce or cabbage leaves, a bowl of ssamjang and whatever kimchi is in the fridge is most of what a Korean dinner table needs on a night nobody feels like cooking something complicated.

🥩 Korea Herald's 600-gram method

Boil 600 grams of pork belly in a pot of lightly salted, sugared water for about an hour, and the hardest part of this recipe is already behind you. Korea Herald's recipe, by food content creator Cookim, keeps the ingredient list short: one halved onion, one green onion cut into pieces, a tablespoon each of salt and sugar, whole peppercorns, and a liter of water.

What is ssamjang (쌈장)?

The dipping sauce that goes with almost every Korean wrap, bossam included: doenjang (fermented soybean paste) mixed with gochujang (chili paste), usually with a little garlic, sesame oil and sometimes chopped scallion stirred in. It's also sold ready-made, jarred under the name ssamjang at Korean grocery stores.

Add the onion and green onion to a pot of water with the salt and sugar, bring it to a boil, then add the pork and simmer over low heat for about an hour. Once the hour is up, pull the pork out, let it cool, and slice it thin. Cutting it while it's still steaming is the one shortcut not worth taking: the recipe's own tip is to let it cool first, since that's what gives clean slices instead of ragged ones.

🔥 The doenjang-or-coffee debate, and what actually matters

Most Korean cooks boil the pork in a broth seasoned with doenjang, but not everyone thinks the extra step is worth it. A Korea Herald column on bossam technique put it plainly: "most cooks boil their pork belly for bossam in a broth seasoned with Korean soybean paste," and some go further and add coffee or other spices specifically to cut the smell of the pork, though the columnist herself found the additions unnecessary and recommends a low-moisture method instead.

A bigger-batch version from the Korean-American food blog Korean Bapsang leans into the full aromatic approach: about 1.1 kilograms of pork belly (2.5 pounds) simmered with doenjang, ginger, garlic, bay leaves and a choice of instant coffee, brewed coffee or beer, for 45 to 50 minutes once the pot returns to a boil, with tenderness checked starting around the 40-minute mark.

Two legitimate methods, not a right and wrong one: Korea Herald's version skips the aromatics entirely and still gets tender pork in about an hour; Korean Bapsang's leans on doenjang and coffee for a deeper color and a milder smell. Checked 2026-11-03.

The step that actually decides the dish isn't the aromatics, it's whether the pork is cooked through. Undercooked pork belly stays tough and awkward to bite through even after it's sliced thin, and the fix is simple: keep checking at intervals past the written cook time until a fork slides in with no resistance, rather than trusting the clock alone.

What is kimjang (김장)?

The yearly event, usually in late November or December, where Korean families make a winter's worth of kimchi in one go. Bossam is traditionally eaten on kimjang day itself, boiled pork served alongside the kimchi that was just made.

🥬 What to wrap it in, what goes on the side, and where to buy the pork

A cold, crisp vegetable is what makes bossam work, so the wrap matters as much as the pork. Lettuce or the inner leaves of napa cabbage are the two standard choices, with the cabbage leaves first salted briefly to soften them before they go on the table.

Fresh, barely-fermented kimchi is the traditional side, and eating it with the just-boiled pork is part of what makes a kimjang-day meal feel like a celebration rather than just chores finishing up. Add a small dish of saeujeot (salted shrimp) if it's available; if it isn't, a simple dip of salt and pepper with a drizzle of sesame oil fills the same role.

Ask a butcher counter for "pork belly" by name, or specifically for samgyeopsal (삼겹살) at a Korean grocery store if there's one nearby; Boston butt, called moksal (목살) in Korean, is the other cut commonly used for this dish. Neither needs to be pre-sliced, since the whole point of bossam is boiling it as a block and slicing it yourself afterward.

Doenjang and gochujang, plus sesame oil, are worth a trip to a Korean or Asian grocery aisle rather than substituting; none of the three have a close everyday stand-in. If napa cabbage isn't sold nearby, any mild, crisp lettuce works as the wrap instead, and that swap doesn't change anything else about the recipe.

My take A pot of plain, boiled pork can look unimpressive next to a marinade with ten ingredients, and that's probably the thing that stops most people from trying it. If I were making this for the first time, I'd skip agonizing over the doenjang-and-coffee question (either version turns out fine) and focus on one thing instead. I'd check that the pork is actually cooked through before it comes out of the pot, since that's the one mistake a sauce can't fix afterward.
Go deeper
  • If you have one more day Boil the pork the day before and keep it in its own cooking liquid in the fridge overnight. Reheating it by bringing the liquid back to a simmer keeps the meat from drying out, and the slices come out just as clean the next day.
  • What locals argue about Doenjang and coffee in the pot, or neither. One Korea Herald food columnist tried the plain, low-moisture route specifically because she didn't think the additions earned their place.
  • Watch for A pot too big for the amount of water called for. A bigger pot needs more water just to cover the meat, which dilutes the seasoning unless the salt and sugar scale up with it.
FAQ
How many servings does 600 grams of pork belly actually make?
Plan on two solid portions eaten alone, stretching further as one item in a fuller spread. Doubling the batch for guests just means a wider pot so everything still sits under the surface, and judging the extra simmer time with a knife tip rather than guessing at a new clock number.
Is there any use for the leftover cooking liquid?
Save it, strained clear. Readers of this dish's reference recipe swap it in as a soup stock elsewhere, a reuse trick nowhere in the printed steps themselves.

Boil it plain or boil it with doenjang and coffee in the pot; both get you to the same place, a block of pork tender enough to slice thin and fold into a cold leaf. The only step worth being careful about is giving the pork enough time in the water before you ever reach for the knife.
