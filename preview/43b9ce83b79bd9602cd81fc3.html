Most first-time kimchi turns out too salty, too bland or strangely soft, and the cabbage almost never did it. The paste goes on in one determined session: garlic and ginger pounded down, gochugaru stirred into a thick red mash, the smell climbing into every corner of the kitchen before anyone's tasted a thing. Every household swears by its own version of that paste, learned by watching someone else's hands rather than reading a card, which is exactly why so many visitors give up and buy a jar instead.

The ratio below isn't a grandmother's secret. It's a government agency's standard recipe, with a research institute's number behind the one variable, salt, that decides whether the whole batch works.

What is gimjang (김장)?

The yearly ritual of making a winter's worth of kimchi at once, usually in late autumn. UNESCO recognized the custom on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2013, citing how the shared work reinforces family cooperation and identity. The recipe below scales a gimjang-sized batch down to one cabbage.

🧂 Salt the cabbage first, then build the paste in grams

Salt the cabbage in a roughly 15% brine for about 8 hours, turning it once or twice. This is the step that decides everything downstream, more than any amount of paste.

  1. Quarter one napa cabbage (about 2.5kg before salting), cutting partway up from the base and tearing the rest by hand so the leaves stay attached at the core.

  2. Sprinkle coarse salt generously between the thick white stem sections of each quarter, since that's where the water has to pull hardest.

  3. Mix a brine at roughly 15% salt to water, and pour enough over the cabbage to keep it fully covered.

  4. Let it sit about 8 hours, turning the quarters once or twice so the top layers salt as evenly as the bottom.

  5. Rinse each quarter two or three times in cold water, then drain it upside down until no more water runs off.

Once the cabbage is drained, the seasoning itself runs about 4.5% gochugaru and 5% jeotgal by the cabbage's weight, with the rest filled out by garlic, ginger, sugar, sesame seeds and rice paste. The Rural Development Administration's guide lists the seasoning as a share of salted-cabbage weight: 4.5% gochugaru, 2.0% garlic, 1.0% ginger, 5.0% jeotgal, 0.5% sesame seeds, 0.5% sugar and 8.5% glutinous rice paste, plus radish and chives and green onion together adding about 20% more. Scaled to roughly 2kg of salted cabbage, about one whole napa cabbage head, that works out to the table below.

What is jeotgal (젓갈)?

Salted, fermented seafood used as a seasoning rather than a topping. Saeujeot (salted shrimp) and myeolchi-jeot (anchovy sauce) are the two most common choices for kimchi, and the two aren't interchangeable gram for gram, since the liquid version seasons harder than the chunky one.

The rice paste is just glutinous rice flour and water simmered together at a 1:9 ratio until it thins to a loose gel, which gives the paste body without needing more sugar. A separate Kimchi Research Institute study at Pusan National University zeroed in on the number that actually decides the outcome: a finished salt concentration of about 2.5%, against a 2.7% figure from earlier literature, with anchovy sauce specifically making up closer to 2.2% of the cabbage's weight in its standardized version, well under the RDA's 5% jeotgal figure for salted shrimp. Use less jeotgal if you're pouring it from a fish-sauce bottle instead of spooning it from a shrimp jar.

🦠 Why kimchi turns mushy, sour or grows a white film

Kimchi ferments in three stages, and most "mistakes" just mean the jar got caught at the wrong one. A researcher at the World Institute of Kimchi describes a preparation stage where the kimchi is still basically raw, a main fermentation stage 10 to 30 days after it goes into cold storage where lactic acid bacteria multiply fastest, and an over-ripening stage after that where the bacteria count drops while acid-tolerant strains push the sourness higher.

What is golmaji (골마지)?

A thin white film that can form on kimchi once it ferments past its peak. It's harmless to eat, according to the same World Institute of Kimchi researcher, but it's also the clearest sign a batch has moved past the mildly sour stage most people are chasing.

The fix is the oldest one in the book: pack the kimchi down firmly enough that it sits under its own liquid, press out the trapped air, and cover the surface before it goes back in the fridge. The traditional cover was a layer of the cabbage's own outer leaves; a sheet of food-safe wrap does the same job today.

Temperature decides how fast a batch moves through those three stages. A dedicated kimchi fridge holds around -1°C, cold enough to slow fermentation to a crawl without freezing the kimchi, since the salt in it keeps it liquid even that cold. A regular fridge runs 2°C to 5°C, which pushes kimchi through its stages noticeably faster. No kimchi fridge? The same source suggests tucking two or three ice packs around the container and swapping them before they fully melt, as a rough stand-in for that steadier cold.

My take A first batch is really a shopping trip wearing a recipe's clothes. Track down the gochugaru and jeotgal, and the cooking itself takes an afternoon, not a weekend. If I were making this for the first time, I'd buy the jeotgal already salted and not bother simmering rice paste from a bag of flour until a second batch, since the RDA's own ratio treats it as one ingredient among seven, not the one everything rides on. Salt the cabbage properly and taste the brine before mixing in anything else. A seasoning paste can't fix cabbage that went in too lightly salted.
Go deeper
  • If you have one more day Let the finished kimchi sit at room temperature for about a day before it goes in the fridge. That's close to the preparation stage the fermentation researcher describes, and skipping straight to cold storage just delays the main fermentation stage instead of speeding anything up.
  • What locals argue about How sour kimchi should get before anyone eats it. The 10-day mark and the 30-day mark both sit inside the same "main fermentation" window by the numbers, but one tastes mild and crunchy and the other properly tangy, and households don't agree on which is right.
  • Watch for The white film. Golmaji won't hurt you, but it means the batch has moved past where most people want it, so taste before assuming it's spoiled.
FAQ
Can I skip the salted shrimp or fish sauce entirely?
Yes, and it won't spoil the batch. The result just reads thinner, since that one item is doing the savory work, not the heat. Make up the difference with a touch more plain salt instead of extra chili flakes, and expect a slightly brighter, less rounded flavor than the version in the table above.
Do I need a special refrigerator to make this at home?
No. An ordinary appliance simply can't hold the same steady, near-freezing chill a dedicated model keeps, so treat any fixed number of days as an estimate rather than a deadline, and judge whether it's ready by tasting a spoonful instead of watching the calendar.

Buy the three special ingredients in one trip since they'll outlast several cabbages, and don't let the seasoning paste intimidate you. The salting step earlier in the day matters more than anything mixed in afterward. Salt the cabbage properly, and the rest of the batch mostly takes care of itself.
