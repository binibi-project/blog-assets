A rice wreath runs ₩75,000-150,000 and ends as a food donation. After one birthday's gifts hit hundreds of millions of won, an agency said stop.

🎀 How a rice wreath becomes a food donation

A rice wreath is a stack of real rice bags built to look like a flower wreath, and the rice is what gets donated, not the wreath. Think of it as a congratulations bouquet that a charity can actually eat. Fans buy one from a florist or an online wreath shop, the bags sit propped on a frame outside a concert, album launch or press conference for a day, and then a charity organization collects and distributes the rice. The practice traces to August 2007, when fans sent the first one to Shinhwa's Shin Hye-sung for a concert, and it spread to other singers, idols and even athletes from there, according to TenAsia's history of the practice.

What is a rice wreath (쌀화환)?

A stand of stacked rice bags, built on a wreath-shaped frame with a ribbon naming the sender, sent to a celebrity's event in place of flowers. After the event it's picked up and donated, usually to feed children or families who don't have regular meals.

The company Dreamy, a wreath supplier quoted on the practice, said rice donations through its wreaths grew roughly 200% a year between 2009 and 2012. The record so far: about 32.5 tons of rice donated under TVXQ member Yunho's name at an MBC press conference on July 29, 2014. That's roughly 3,250 of the 10kg wreaths you'd order today, which is what the number actually means once you picture it stacked outside one building in one afternoon.

More recent drives run smaller and more local. In late 2024, singer Lee Chan-won's fan club delivered 700kg of rice and 350 bottles of detergent to Inwangsan Senior Welfare Center in Seoul for his birthday. A fan quoted in a 2019 Seoul Shinmun report on fandom giving said rice donations were already losing ground to pet food, sanitary pads and volunteer hours, as fan clubs looked for other ways to help.

📦 What a wreath, a lunch, or a truck actually costs

None of the three main formats is cheap, and only one of them has a public price. A rice wreath is the one you can simply order online; current listings for a 10-20kg wreath, celebration or condolence style, run ₩75,000 to ₩150,000 ($56-$112 at ₩1,344/$1).

Neither the lunch spread nor the coffee truck has a public rate card the way wreath shops do, so treat any specific price you see online for either one as unconfirmed until a vendor quotes it directly.

Boxed-lunch competition is its own small arms race: one fan-culture report says several fan clubs for the same idol often order lunches for the same shoot, and prices have climbed as each club tries to outdo the last one. Coffee trucks follow older film-set etiquette: fans, labelmates or friends send a custom-wrapped truck to feed everyone working that day, not just the star, which is part of why it reads as generous rather than showy.

🛑 When the gifts go too far

Starship Entertainment told IVE's fans on December 15, 2025 that the group would accept nothing from then on except handwritten letters. The notice said any support already being prepared would also be declined, and that the agency hoped fans' goodwill could go somewhere it was more genuinely needed, as The Korea Times reported.

The trigger was a list of birthday gifts member Jang Wonyoung's Chinese fanbase had prepared for her: luxury handbags, clothing, high-end cameras and gaming consoles, reported to total hundreds of millions of won. This is "jogong," literally "tribute," the word Korean fans use for exactly this kind of lavish, competitive gift-giving.

What is jogong (조공)?

Literally "tribute." The word fans and press use for expensive gifts sent to an idol, anything from a rice wreath to designer goods, especially when it's done to outdo other fans or fan clubs rather than just to celebrate.

Agencies increasingly send something back too, and fans don't always feel the trade was even. At BLACKPINK's 10th-anniversary meet-and-greet on August 8, 2026, 40 fans at the National Museum of Korea in Yongsan-gu, members handed out a rice cake gift set that online communities identified as an off-the-shelf product priced at $5.20 (₩6,900). Jisoo apologized on a live broadcast for how the event's short notice and tight guest list had been handled, and critics said the complaint was never really about the price, but that a 10-year milestone deserved more thought than a store-bought snack.

My take If you've ever wanted to send something and worried it would look cheap or look like you're trying too hard, you're already thinking about this the right way. The wreath and the welfare-center photo exist precisely so a modest gift reads as generous rather than small, and the money goes further than a gift kept in a dorm room ever would. My call: a rice wreath or a welfare-center donation in an idol's name is the version that ages well, because it still does something after the event ends. A birthday haul that needs its own moving truck doesn't.

FAQ
Does every K-pop agency now ban fan gifts the way IVE's does?
No, and gifts are only one slice of fandom spending anyway. Separate 2024 reporting found some top boy groups' fan-meeting raffles effectively require ₩5,000,000-10,000,000 ($3,720-$7,440) in album purchases for a shot at a 1-to-3-minute slot with the group, spending a letters-only gift policy never touches. A notice can also arrive with zero lead time, as IVE's fans found out, so it's worth a look before you commit any money.
Do fans only donate rice, or other things too?
Rice is just where it started. The same 2019 Seoul Shinmun reporting on fandom giving recorded Kang Daniel's fan club donating 128 blood-donation certificates plus about ₩9,600,000 ($7,143) to a children's leukemia foundation, and IU's fan club giving ₩100,000,000 ($74,400) to hearing-impaired support groups for her 11th debut anniversary in September 2019. The format keeps changing; the instinct to turn a celebration into a donation hasn't.

Jogong isn't going away, but the direction of travel is toward gifts that do something after the cameras leave: a donated sack of rice over a designer bag nobody will see again. If a group you follow hasn't posted its own notice yet, assume the letters-only rule could land any week and check before you spend anything.
