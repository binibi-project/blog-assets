Pepero Day: The Snack Holiday Nobody Planned

Why Koreans swap chocolate sticks every Nov. 11, who actually started it, what a box costs now, and why farmers claim the date too.

Quick answer
  • A schoolgirl rumor started it, not a company: Nov. 11 (11/11) was already nicknamed "Pepero Day" among North Gyeongsang teenagers by 1996, a year before Lotte built a marketing push around it.
  • The date has an older, legal claim on it: November 11 has been Farmers' Day since 1996, and a rice-cake "Garaetteok Day" was created in 2006 specifically to compete with Pepero for the same square on the calendar.
  • Skip the pricey bouquet box: a plain ₩2,000 box is the same snack everyone grew up giving, and most people who take part at all still spend under ₩3,000 a person.

Four ones in a row, twice over, isn't a date that means anything by itself. It took a rumor passed between middle-school girls in North Gyeongsang Province, repeated often enough that a newspaper finally wrote it down in 1996, to turn November 11 into something an entire country now marks without thinking twice: the day a chocolate-dipped biscuit stick shaped like the number "1" becomes the easiest gift in Korea. Walk into almost any convenience store in the week before, and the snack aisle has already rearranged itself into a wall of ribboned boxes, no sign required to explain why.

🍬 Where did Pepero Day actually come from?

Nobody can prove where it started, and Lotte Wellfood itself says the company didn't invent it. The most common version of the story has two middle school girls in Korea's Yeongnam region trading Pepero sometime in the 1980s or early 1990s, each hoping the exchange would help the other grow "tall and thin" like the snack. Nobody has ever produced the original article, the school, or the two girls, and the year shifts depending on who tells it.

What is Pepero (빼빼로)?

A thin, pretzel-like biscuit stick dipped in chocolate, launched by Lotte (now Lotte Wellfood) in 1983. "Pepero" comes from ppaeppaehada, a Korean word for being thin or skinny, the same idea behind the schoolgirl story.

What's actually documented is a November 1996 newspaper article describing the "Pepero Day" nickname as already popular among teenage girls in North Gyeongsang Province. Around the same time, Lotte's own sales staff in neighboring South Gyeongsang Province had noticed Pepero sales climbing every November and flagged it to headquarters. The Korea Times reports that the company adopted "Pepero Day" for its own marketing the following year, November 11, 1997, handing out free boxes in big cities, and has run a campaign around the date every year since. A Lotte Wellfood spokesperson later put it plainly: "It is not a culture created at the company level."

📈 How big does this actually get?

Lotte Wellfood expects Pepero's 2025 sales to hit about ₩241.5 billion ($182 million), the brand's best year on record. That single year is a small slice of a much longer run: since the snack launched in 1983, Koreans have bought roughly 3.7 billion boxes of it, the equivalent of about 72 boxes for every citizen in the country, for a cumulative total near ₩2.15 trillion ($1.62 billion). Lined up end to end, that many boxes would stretch about 600,000 km, or roughly 15 times around the Earth.

Think of Pepero Day the way a toy store thinks of December: one date that can carry close to half a chain's yearly total for a single product. At GS25, that's not a rough guess — 52.2% of the chain's entire 2021 Pepero sales landed in November of that year alone, with Lotte Mart (45%) and Seven Eleven (45%) not far behind.

Exports have grown just as fast. Pepero's overseas sales reached about ₩70.1 billion ($53 million) in 2024, up roughly 30% from ₩54 billion the year before, and Lotte Wellfood was targeting close to ₩90 billion ($68 million) in export sales for 2025, selling the snack in around 50 countries.

🌾 Whose day is November 11, and what should you actually give?

By law, November 11 belonged to farmers a full year before anyone marketed a Pepero Day. The government designated it "농어업인의 날" (Farmers and Fishers Day) in 1996, later shortened to "농업인의 날" (Farmers' Day), after farming advocates had lobbied for the designation since the early 1980s. The date itself is wordplay: the Chinese character 土 (soil) is built from the characters for "ten" and "one," and 11/11 repeats that "one" twice.

What is Garaetteok Day (가래떡데이)?

A rice-cake counter-holiday the Ministry of Agriculture and Nonghyup, the national farming cooperative, created in 2006 on the same November 11, for a near-identical reason Pepero Day caught on: garaetteok, the long white rice-cake sticks used in tteokbokki, also look like the digits in 11/11.

The idea was to pull shrinking rice consumption back toward something people would actually reach for on a day already built around a snack shape. In practice, Pepero Day swallowed nearly all of the attention: a 2010 Gyeonggi Ilbo report found big marts and convenience stores running Pepero promotions for a week beforehand, while a planned Garaetteok Day event to mark Farmers' Day "couldn't be found" in stores that year, and even a local Nonghyup branch skipped the garaetteok giveaway it had run in previous years.

A holiday the government built to push back against commercial excess lost, badly, to the thing it was built to resist.

Whichever side of that split gets your sympathy, nobody who actually takes part spends much on it. The only classroom tally on record, a teacher's own count from a 2010 Pepero Day, found most students spent under ₩3,000 on Pepero for friends, with only a few going over ₩10,000; the one outlier, a girl marking 100 days with her boyfriend, spent over ₩30,000 on a large basket. Recipients were mostly friends first, then teachers, then occasionally siblings or parents.

A plain box is still the cheapest way in. Choco Pepero (54g) rose from ₩1,800 to ₩2,000 on Feb. 17, 2025, part of a run that pushed the price up more than 33% since 2022. Fancier options exist above that: a recent gift-price guide puts a Daiso DIY kit around ₩3,000-4,000, a handmade "goodie bag" from roughly ₩20,000, and a dessert-cafe box or flower-and-macaron bouquet climbing past ₩30,000, though exact prices vary by shop and the guide itself isn't consistent on every tier.

My take If November 11 catches you off guard at a Korean office or your kid's school, you haven't missed some unwritten obligation. Nobody is tracking who gave what, and no rulebook says an officemate owes a bouquet-shaped box to anyone. If deciding whether to join in at all, the plain box is the one worth keeping in mind, not the flower sets or premium tins built for couples making a bigger point. What matters more than any single price tag is how modestly most people who take part actually spend.

FAQ
Is Garaetteok Day actually losing ground to Pepero Day, or has the rice-cake side caught up by now?
Not based on what turned up during research. The one close look at the gap dates to 2010, and it found almost nothing on shelves for the rice option next to the chocolate one. Nothing newer surfaced to suggest a shift, and the newer snack keeps posting bigger totals, not smaller ones.
What's the cheapest legitimate way to join in without buying a boxed snack at all?
A craft kit from a budget shop. It sits at the low end of that same price tier, costing only a few thousand won in materials, well below a packaged treat, and it ends up handmade rather than a stick pulled from a foil wrapper.

Pepero Day isn't a tradition anyone designed on purpose, and the government's own rice-cake answer to it never really stood a chance once convenience store chains got a product built for a one-week sales spike. If you want in on November 11, a single plain box for a friend covers the custom completely, and nobody will think less of you for stopping there.
