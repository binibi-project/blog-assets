Korea's Soju Culture: Why Someone Else Fills Your Glass

The two-handed pour, the 70% fill line, the head-turn for elders, and why 2025's soju shipments fell to a 27-year low. The rules and what a bottle costs now.

The green bottle lands on the table before the side dishes do, and before you've even picked up your chopsticks, someone across from you has already reached for it, tipped it toward your glass, and started pouring. You didn't ask. You didn't need to. A visitor sees a friendly gesture; a Korean at that table is running through a small, automatic checklist about age, hands, and who goes first, the kind of thing nobody explains because everybody already knows it.

🍶 Why does someone else pour first, and why do they look away?

The oldest or most senior person at the table gets poured for first, and whoever does the pouring uses both hands. Younger drinkers hold the bottle with both hands, or steady it with the right hand while the left rests on the chest, and fill the glass only to about 70%, not to the rim. A senior pouring for someone younger can get away with one hand; nobody younger tries that.

Think of the two hands as the handshake of a Korean table: the specific grip matters less than the fact that you clearly made the effort. Pouring your own drink isn't banned outright, but one guide describes it as the move people quietly avoid, since somebody else is supposed to be watching your glass before it runs dry.

What is 첨잔 (cheomjan)?

Topping off a glass that still has liquid in it. Guides describe it as an old taboo, tied to the custom of pouring a single, full cup for the dead rather than refilling it. The practical version today: wait for a glass to go empty before you refill it, and watch the table so you're the one who notices first.

Turning your face away from an elder and covering your mouth with your hand while you drink is the traditional move, especially one-on-one. Guides describe looking away from the senior person, and if there are elders on both sides, turning toward whichever one outranks the other. Nobody drinks before the toast either: you wait until everyone's glass is filled and the toast begins, then you hold your own glass lower than a senior's when you clink.

Who sits where follows the same logic. The seat farthest from the entrance, facing the door, is the 상석 (sangseok), reserved for the senior or most important person; the seat directly across is the second-highest. None of this is enforced like a rulebook, and one Seoul-based guide notes both the two-handed pour and the head-turn drop away once it's just close friends at the table — but if you're unsure of your place, using both hands and glancing away is the version that can't go wrong.

What is 상석 (sangseok)?

The "seat of honor" at a Korean table, traditionally the one farthest from the door with its back to the wall, facing the entrance. It usually goes to whoever is oldest or whoever organized the gathering, and everyone else arranges themselves around that point.

📉 Why is Korea drinking less soju than ever?

National Tax Service data shows 2025 soju shipments fell 2.8% to 793,000 kiloliters, dropping below 800,000 kiloliters for the first time, while total alcohol output hit its lowest level in 27 years at just under 2.99 million kiloliters. Beer fell even harder, down 7.2% to 1.52 million kiloliters. Asked why, 28.6% of people cited health and 27.9% cited cost, with another 18.8% saying they'd found other ways to spend their free time and 16% pointing to a changing drinking culture.

What is 회식 (hoesik)?

A company dinner, usually with drinks, that used to function as mandatory after-hours team bonding. Showing up was rarely optional and leaving before the second round could get noticed. That expectation is the piece of the culture most visibly cracking.

The hoesik is the clearest casualty. Mandatory company dinners that once ran two or three rounds deep into the night are no longer the default, and corporate culture increasingly lets younger employees spend their evenings on their own interests instead. A 2025 Opensurvey poll of 2,000 adults in their 20s and 30s found they drank 6.4 times a month on average, down from 7.04 times in 2024, and the share who'd had a drink in the past month fell 4.9 points to 67.2%.

NH NongHyup Bank's card-spending data agrees: bar visits by people in their 20s fell to an index of 48 against a 100 baseline in early 2023, while pool spending rose 59%. So what does this mean for your night out? Don't expect every table to insist you keep pace anymore; the generation doing most of the pouring is also the one cutting back hardest.

💸 What does a bottle of soju actually cost now?

A restaurant bottle in Seoul typically runs ₩5,000-6,000 (about $3.75-$4.50), climbing to ₩7,000-8,000 (about $5.25-$6.01) in pricier areas like Gangnam, while convenience stores sell the same 360ml bottle for well under half that. One bank employee told the Korea Times that ₩5,000-6,000 is typical, with Gangnam running ₩7,000-8,000 a bottle. That's a jump from the roughly ₩4,000 a bottle that was common in 2021.

Restaurant soju prices actually slipped 1.3% year-on-year by March 2025, their seventh straight monthly decline, as owners tried to win back diners. If your bottle costs more than ₩6,000, you're likely in a pricier neighborhood, not being overcharged.

FAQ
What if I don't want to drink at all?
You're on firmer ground than you'd think. The older custom was to accept a full glass and touch it to your lips for the first toast rather than refuse outright, but forcing someone to drink now carries real risk for whoever's pushing. One office worker told the Segye Ilbo that pressuring a colleague to drink now counts as grounds for discipline at his company. A polite "저는 괜찮아요" (jeo-neun gwaen-chan-a-yo, "I'm okay") while keeping your glass topped up for others works fine at most tables now.
Is there an etiquette for when the night winds down, too?
Yes, and it's less ritual than plain courtesy. The same etiquette guides say to thank whoever ends up paying rather than letting it pass unacknowledged, and to make sure anyone who's had too much gets into a taxi or a ride home before the group splits up. Nobody expects you to organize it, just to notice if nobody else has.

The rules sound like a lot until you watch them happen once: somebody reaches for the bottle, somebody else turns a shoulder before they sip, and the whole table keeps moving without anyone announcing a single step of it. Use both hands until someone tells you not to bother, and don't be surprised if the person doing the pouring is quietly drinking less than they used to.
