Korea's Two Number Systems: A Visitor's Quick Guide

Korea runs on two number systems, native for counting and Sino-Korean for money and time, and mixing them is the easiest way to sound confused.

Quick answer
  • Two systems split by job, not by size: native Korean numbers (하나, 둘, 셋) count people and objects, tell the clock hour, and give a casual age; Sino-Korean numbers (일, 이, 삼) handle money, dates, phone numbers and minutes, plus anything over 99.
  • The same sound can flip meaning: 한 분 is one honored person, but 일 분 is one minute. Same syllable, different number system, a different sentence entirely.
  • If you only remember one rule: say the hour in native Korean and the minutes in Sino-Korean (한 시 삼십 분 for 1:30), and most clock-time sentences take care of themselves.

Hold up two fingers at a convenience store counter and point at the ramen, and nobody needs a word for "two" at all. Try to say it out loud instead, and Korean hands you two different numbers to pick from: one for the ramen in your hand, a completely different one for the price printed on it. Taxi drivers, phone operators and cashiers switch between the two without a flicker of hesitation, because the choice was never really about the size of the number. It's about which part of your day you're in, counting something or paying for it, and that's the one habit worth borrowing before you land.

🔢 Which number do I actually use?

Use native Korean numbers (하나, 둘, 셋) to count people and objects and to say your age casually, and switch to Sino-Korean numbers (일, 이, 삼) for money, dates, phone numbers and anything above 99. Teuida's guide to the two systems lays out the same split: native numbers for everyday counting and the clock hour, Sino-Korean for prices, dates, floors, bus numbers and minutes.

What is Sino-Korean (한자어)?

A set of number words built from Chinese characters, the same roots Japanese and Mandarin counting also draws on. It sits alongside the older native Korean set rather than replacing it, and which one you reach for depends entirely on the word that follows the number.

The 99 ceiling isn't a rounding habit, it's a gap in the vocabulary itself. A column in the newspaper Hankyung traces it back to two now-rare native words: 온 once meant hundred, and 즈믄 once meant thousand, before Sino-Korean 백 and 천 took over both jobs. That's why Korean can count natively from 하나 to 아흔아홉 and then has nowhere left to go but Sino-Korean.

Age is where the two systems collide most visibly. The same Hankyung column calls out "사십두 살," a number stuck halfway between systems, as an awkward hybrid, and recommends picking one lane: 마흔두 살 in full native Korean, or 42세 in full Sino-Korean. Korea's own rules for which age you give someone are a separate tangle worth reading on their own.

🕐 How do you actually tell time?

Say the hour in native Korean and the minutes in Sino-Korean, so 1:30 comes out as 한 시 삼십 분, and you've covered the one clock rule that matters. A University of British Columbia Korean course gives almost the same example, 한시 십분 for 1:10, with the native 한 carrying the hour and the Sino-Korean 십 carrying the minutes. A newspaper column makes the same split and adds the probable reason: the hour only ever runs to 12, small enough that the older native words stuck, while minutes and seconds run to 60 and leaned on the newer borrowed set instead.

What is a counter word (단위명사)?

The noun that sits right after a number, like 개 (items), 명 (people), 시 (o'clock) or 분 (minutes). The counter decides which number system you use, not the number itself, which is why the same quantity can sound completely different depending on what's being counted.

The counter word does all the deciding, and one example makes that oddly clear. The same UBC course notes that 한분 (with the native 한) means one honored person, while 일분 (with the Sino-Korean 일) means one minute. Swap the number system under an identical-sounding syllable and you've swapped the entire sentence, which is the kind of mix-up that explains a confused look from a shop clerk far better than a language barrier does.

📞 What happens when you give out a phone number?

Korean phone numbers are read one digit at a time in Sino-Korean, so 010-549-7832 comes out as "공일공, 오사구, 칠팔삼이." A Korea travel guide spells out that exact reading, digit for digit, including 공 for the zero at the front. It's the same system behind two of the numbers that matter most in an emergency.

Korea's police line is 112 and its combined fire-and-ambulance line is 119, both dialed and read the same digit-by-digit way.

If the person who answers doesn't speak English, the Korea Tourism Organization's 1330 travel hotline runs from 7 a.m. to midnight and can patch into a three-way call with 112 or 119 to translate on the spot. Save all three before you need them, not while you're dialing them.

My take Getting a number system "wrong" in Korea almost never breaks anything. A clerk hears a mismatched number and just rings up the right item anyway, the way anyone would if a tourist pointed and mumbled. The one place it's worth slowing down is a number someone else has to write down exactly: your phone number, an address, an apartment buzzer code. If I were guessing on the spot, I'd default to native Korean for anything small and countable, and Sino-Korean for anything with a price tag or a digit string attached to it. You'll be right most of the time, and the one time you're not, nobody's charging you twice for it.
Go deeper
  • If you have one more day Learn the native numbers from 11 to 20 (열하나, 열둘... 스물), since birthdays, floors below five and small counts tend to land right in that range.
  • What locals argue about Whether giving your age as 42세 sounds too stiff and bureaucratic for casual conversation, or whether 마흔두 살 just takes longer to say. Neither side is wrong; it's a register choice, not a grammar one.
  • Watch for Train announcements and taxi receipts, which lean hard on Sino-Korean for times and fares. If a number on a screen looks unfamiliar, it's almost certainly reading in that system, not the one you'd use to count your bags.
FAQ
Do I actually need to get this right to be understood?
Rarely, for anything minor: a cashier still hands back the correct change no matter which form you used, since locals blend the two forms just as often in daily chat. It starts to matter the moment someone else has to write down exactly what you said out loud, like a name called for a table, or a sequence dictated during a call that has to match later.
What if someone gives me a number verbally and I don't catch it?
Ask them to type or text it instead of repeating it slower. A misheard digit in a string read quickly, like a phone number or a door code, is easy to get wrong even for Koreans, and a typed number sidesteps the whole native-versus-Sino-Korean question entirely.

None of this needs memorizing before you land, just the one habit: count with native Korean, and pay or schedule with Sino-Korean. Start listening for 시 and 분 in the same sentence on your first subway ride, and the split stops sounding like two languages and starts sounding like one with better organization.
