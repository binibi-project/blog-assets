How to Make Japchae at Home: A Recipe in Exact Grams

A japchae recipe in exact quantities, the noodle mistake that makes it gummy, and the vegetable the original recipe used before dangmyeon existed.

Quick answer
  • One noodle, six components, one bowl at the end: dangmyeon plus beef or pork and five vegetables (carrot, onion, scallion, mushroom, spinach), each cooked on its own before they meet.
  • Feeds four in 35 minutes: 15 minutes of cutting, 20 more moving between a boiling pot and a hot pan.
  • Check first: cook the noodles until they're fully soft, not al dente, since that's the one step a Western cook's instinct usually gets wrong.

Ever made a stir-fry with seven ingredients and had it come out tasting like none of them? Japchae shouldn't do that. Done right, every strand of noodle carries a little sweetness and a little sesame, the carrot still snaps, the spinach stays green instead of going to mush, and the whole plate smells faintly of toasted sesame oil before it even reaches the table.

It's built from parts cooked separately and combined at the very end, which is also why a rushed version turns into one uniform, slightly soggy pile. Get the noodles' texture right and cook the vegetables apart, and the rest of the dish falls into place on its own.

What does "japchae" (잡채) actually mean?

잡 (jap) means to mix, and 채 (chae) means vegetables, so the name itself just says "a medley of vegetables". That's a plainer claim than the dish makes today, since most people now picture the noodles first and the vegetables second.

🍜 The recipe: japchae for four

Four servings, 35 minutes end to end, and nothing in the pan for longer than a few minutes at a time. That's Korean Bapsang's home version, rated 4.67 out of 5 by more than 500 cooks who've made it.

Ingredient Amount
Dangmyeon (sweet potato starch noodles) 7 oz (about 200g)
Beef or pork loin, thinly sliced 4 oz (about 115g)
Carrot, cut into matchsticks 1 (about 85g)
Onion, thinly sliced 1/2 medium (about 115-140g)
Scallions, cut into 2-inch pieces 2
Fresh shiitake mushrooms 3-4 oz (about 85-115g)
Fresh spinach 6 oz (about 170g)
Soy sauce 3.5 tbsp
Sugar, brown preferred 3 tbsp
Sesame oil 2 tbsp
Garlic, minced 2 tsp
Sesame seeds, roasted 1 tbsp
Black pepper to taste

Mix the sauce first, then work through the pot and pan one ingredient at a time. Boil the noodles 6 to 8 minutes until fully translucent, rinse them cold, cut them into 6-7 inch lengths, and toss with 3 tablespoons of the sauce before they touch a pan.

  1. Cut the carrot into matchsticks, slice the onion thin, cut the scallion whites lengthwise and into 2-inch pieces, slice the mushrooms 1/4-inch thin, and cut the meat into thin 2-inch strips.

  2. Boil the noodles 6 to 8 minutes until soft and translucent, drain, rinse cold, cut to length, and mix with 3 tablespoons of sauce.

  3. Blanch the spinach just until wilted, shock it in cold water, squeeze it dry, and season lightly with salt.

  4. Stir-fry the noodles alone in a little oil for about 4 minutes, until they turn a bit sticky, then set them aside.

  5. Stir-fry the carrot, then the onion, then stir in the scallion, each with a pinch of salt, keeping the vegetables crisp rather than soft.

  6. Cook the beef and mushrooms together with 2 tablespoons of sauce until the meat is done, about 2-3 minutes.

  7. Toss everything together by hand, including the spinach and remaining sauce. Taste it and add a little more soy sauce or sugar if it needs it.

What is dangmyeon (당면)?

Noodles made from sweet potato starch. Boiled, they turn chewy and glass-clear. They aren't part of the dish's original form, either: dangmyeon only reached Korea in the late 19th century, arriving with Chinese immigrants after 1882, and wasn't mass-produced until the 1920s and 30s. Outside Korea it's sold as "sweet potato glass noodles" or "dangmyeon" at a Korean or Asian grocery store, or ordered online.

🥢 Where japchae usually goes wrong

The noodles are the most common failure, and it's almost always undercooking, not overcooking. A Western cook's instinct is to stop at al dente, but dangmyeon needs the opposite: 6 to 8 minutes at a full boil, until every strand is completely soft and see-through, not just softened at the edges.

The second failure is skipping the order of operations. Noodles get their own few minutes in the pan first, then the carrot and onion, then the beef and mushroom together, and only at the very end does the blanched spinach get folded in. Run everything together from the start instead, and the crisp vegetables finish just as soft as everything else.

The sauce itself is simpler to get right: it's just soy sauce against sugar, adjusted by taste at the end rather than measured once and left alone.

🛒 Buying the ingredients outside Korea

Dangmyeon is the one ingredient with no real substitute, and it's also the easiest to find. A 900g bag runs $8.09 at H Mart, marked down from $8.99, more than enough for this recipe with plenty left over to freeze.

Fresh shiitake isn't always in stock everywhere; oyster, wood ear or button mushrooms all work, and so do dried shiitake soaked until plump.

What is doraji (도라지), and why isn't it in this recipe?

Bellflower root: bitter, earthy, with a smell close to ginseng. It was one of the original ingredients in Korea's earliest documented japchae, a 17th-century version with no noodles at all. Most home versions today, including the one in this recipe, leave it out. If you want to try it anyway, look for dried, ivory-colored roots (not bleached white) at a Korean or Asian grocery store, online, or occasionally on Amazon.

My take A dish with seven components sounds like the kind of thing that eats up an evening, and that's the part that puts people off before they've cut a single carrot. If I were making this for the first time, I'd treat the separate cooking as the whole point rather than an inconvenience: it's four or five short pans, not one long one, and none of them takes more than a few minutes. Once the noodles are boiled correctly, the rest is mostly timing, not skill.
Go deeper
  • If you have one more hour Give the carrot its own pan, then the onion, then the scallion, instead of running them through together, the way the noodles already get their own step before anything else joins them. It's more dishes to wash, but each vegetable keeps its own exact cook time instead of an average of the three.
  • What locals argue about Whether japchae is a noodle dish at all. The earliest documented version, in the 17th-century cookbook Eumsik Dimibang, was vegetables only, no noodles, including bellflower root and cucumber; dangmyeon wasn't added until Chinese immigrants brought it after 1882. By that count, the noodles are the newest part of the dish, not the reason for it.
  • Watch for Texture changing overnight. The noodles keep absorbing sauce as they sit, so leftovers taste chewier the next day; Korean Bapsang says they keep 3-4 days refrigerated and reheat fine in the microwave.
FAQ
Does it matter which cut of beef I use?
Not much. The source lists sirloin, chuck tender or rib eye, or pork loin as substitutes. Whichever happens to be cheapest that week is the one worth buying, since the sauce and the vegetables carry most of the flavor anyway.
What's the egg garnish some photos show on top?
That's jidan, a garnish made by cooking egg whites and yolks into two separate pancakes, then julienning them for color on top. The source marks it optional. Banquet-style plates use it most; a weeknight bowl rarely needs it.

Nothing in this dish is hard on its own; it's just several small, short steps instead of one long one. Boil the noodles until they're fully soft, keep the vegetables in separate pans, and taste the sauce before you decide it's done. Do that much and the rest of the recipe mostly takes care of itself.
