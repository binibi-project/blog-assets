Dwaeji Gukbap in Busan: Where Locals Actually Eat It

Three Busan dwaeji gukbap shops rated 4.1+ on Google Maps, one subway line apart, from a 1956 bowl in Beomil-dong to a 24-hour Seomyeon counter.

Quick answer
  • Three stops, one subway line: start near Busan Station, end in Seomyeon, no transfers in between.
  • A bowl runs ₩8,000-11,000, and every shop on this list rates 4.1 or higher on Google Maps from well over 2,000 reviews.
  • Order by name, not by pointing: learn "국밥 하나 주세요" before you go, since these counters move fast and the menus are short.

By seven in the morning the counters near Busan Station are already full of steam, and half the people eating aren't tourists at all, they're on their way to a shift. The bowl in front of them is cloudy white, pork bone boiled down since before dawn, with a tangle of garlic chives on top that hasn't wilted yet. Nobody's talking much, and the quiet is its own kind of welcome.

Dwaeji gukbap wasn't always Busan's dish. Korea JoongAng Daily traces it to the early 1950s, when refugees from across the peninsula poured into the city during the Korean War. Beef was scarce and expensive; pork bones were what the wartime city actually had. The three stops below sit on a single stretch of Busan Metro Line 1, Busan Station to Seomyeon, so you can do all three without a transfer.

What is dwaeji gukbap (돼지국밥)?

Pork bones and meat, simmered for hours into a cloudy, milky broth, ladled over rice in a stone bowl. Scallions and garlic chives go on top; dadaegi (a spoon of chili paste) and salted shrimp go in if you want them. It's breakfast, lunch and 2 a.m. food, all at once.

🚉 Five minutes from Busan Station: Bonjeon Dwaeji Gukbap

Start at 본전돼지국밥 (Bonjeon Dwaeji Gukbap), about a five-minute walk from Busan Station on Line 1, and order the ₩11,000 (about $8.22) house bowl. The address is 부산 동구 중앙대로214번길 3-8, and the shop has been running for about 45 years as of a 2024 writeup, with no change in the broth recipe that writeup could point to. One reviewer's note stuck with me more than the price did: no unpleasant smell at all, just a clean, clear broth, which is the compliment every dwaeji gukbap shop in this city is actually chasing.

It's hard to call this one a hidden pick. It carries a 4.1 rating from 4,714 Google Maps reviews, the busiest of the three shops on this list. Say "돼지국밥 하나 주세요" (dwae-ji-guk-bap ha-na ju-se-yo, "one pork gukbap, please") and you're set; if you'd rather skip the organ meat entirely, "수육백반" (su-yuk-baek-ban), sliced boiled pork with rice on the side, runs ₩13,000 (about $9.72). English menu and exact hours weren't confirmed in what I could check directly, so screenshot the Korean name before you go.

🍲 A 1956 bowl in Beomil-dong: Halmae Gukbap

One stop north, get off at Beomil Station for 할매국밥 (Halmae Gukbap), where a bowl has cost a fair price since before most of Busan's subway existed. Choi Soon-bok, a refugee from Pyongyang, opened the shop in 1956; she ran it until she passed away in 2006, and her daughter-in-law Kim Young-hee, now 64, has kept it going since. The address is 부산 동구 중앙대로533번길 4, in Beomil-dong, the same district where Beomil Station sits on Line 1.

The menu runs to exactly three items, and the broth is made from meat alone, no vegetable filler, kept at a boil in two aluminum pots that are themselves older than 40 years. A bowl is ₩8,000 (about $5.98), whether you order 돼지국밥, 따로국밥 (rice on the side) or 내장국밥 (organ meat). It holds a 4.2 rating from 2,524 Google Maps reviews, the smallest crowd of the three but still a very real one. Exact hours and the station exit number weren't confirmed in what I could check directly; call ahead to be sure before building a schedule around it.

What is toreom (토렴)?

Pouring hot broth over rice two or three times before serving, rather than just ladling it on once. It warms the rice through without overcooking it, and it's the small, repeated motion that old-style gukbap counters are built around.

🌙 24 hours in Seomyeon: Songjeong 3-dae Gukbap

Two more stops north on the same line, Seomyeon Station puts you at 송정3대국밥 (Songjeong 3-dae Gukbap), open 24 hours, every day of the year. Its own roots trace to the same Korean War refugee wave that brought dwaeji gukbap to Busan in the first place. The address is 부산 부산진구 서면로68번길 33, close to Seomyeon Station on Lines 1 and 2, though the exact exit for this address wasn't confirmed in what I could check directly.

따로국밥 (rice served separately) is ₩9,500 (about $7.10), and reviewers describe the broth here as clear and mild rather than heavily pork-forward, a different character from the cloudier bowls elsewhere on this list. A small order of 찹쌀순대 (glutinous-rice blood sausage) adds ₩11,000 (about $8.22) if you want it alongside. It's the most-reviewed shop here, 4.1 from 4,495 Google Maps ratings, which tracks with being open around the clock in one of Busan's busiest nightlife districts.

What is ttaro-gukbap (따로국밥)?

Ttaro means "separate." Instead of rice already soaking in the broth, it arrives in its own small bowl, so you control how much goes in and the rice never turns to mush. Order it this way when you want to take your time with the bowl.

None of these three is an undiscovered secret, and that's sort of the point. A bowl this cheap only stays this good when enough regulars keep showing up to argue about it. The city itself is leaning into that argument this year: nearly 10,000 citizens nominated more than 250 gukbap shops across Busan for an official "Gukbap Week" running September 28 through October 14, with a public vote narrowing the field to a final top 10 plus three more honorees. None of the three shops on this list needed a festival to earn their review counts, though.

If a short menu with no English on it makes you nervous, these three shops are actually the easy version of that problem: two or three items, a number, and a point.

My take If you only have time for one stop, make it Halmae Gukbap. A shop that's kept the same broth method since 1956, through one succession from a mother-in-law to a daughter-in-law, has already survived harder tests than your schedule. The other two are easier to fit into a longer day since they're built around convenience, Bonjeon for a fast breakfast before a train, Songjeong for whenever hunger actually hits at 2 a.m. A bowl this cheap doesn't need you to love it unconditionally, just to show up hungry.

FAQ
Is there an English sign or picture menu anywhere on this list?
Not confirmed at any stop, and honestly, there isn't much to translate. One of these three kitchens runs its entire lineup on a wall placard with a grand total of three dishes. Save a photo of the Korean characters before you leave your hotel, then hold up your phone instead of guessing out loud at the counter.
Worried about ending up with cuts you didn't expect?
Say "순살로 주세요" (sun-sal-lo ju-se-yo, "lean cuts only, please") when you sit down. The single syllable to watch for on any wall placard is 내장: it marks the organ-heavy version, and everything printed without it defaults to plain muscle, no extra vocabulary required.

A bowl of soup this cheap rarely comes with this much history attached, but here it does: three kitchens, one subway line, and a dish that exists because a city once had nothing else to cook. Start at whichever stop fits your morning, and let the order of the other two sort itself out. Prices and hours were checked on 2026-10-06; they change, so double-check before you go.
