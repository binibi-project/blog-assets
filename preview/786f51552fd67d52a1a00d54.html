🚕 What does a Seoul taxi actually cost by the meter?

A standard taxi starts at ₩4,800 for the first 1.6 kilometers, and almost everything after that is added in small, predictable steps. Seoul's own transport portal publishes the full fare schedule: beyond that first stretch, the meter adds ₩100 for every 131 meters, plus ₩100 for every 30 seconds, so a slow-moving ride costs more than the distance alone would suggest.

Taking that same ride after 22:00 costs more, and the jump isn't flat. The surcharge runs 20% from 22:00 to 23:00 and again from 02:00 to 04:00, lifting the base fare to ₩5,800. Between 23:00 and 02:00 it climbs to 40%, pushing the base to ₩6,700.

Leaving somewhere at half past midnight is the single most expensive hour to need a taxi in Seoul. A ride that starts or ends outside the city's own boundary adds a further 20% surcharge, and the two can stack to a maximum of 60%.

What is the "out-of-city" surcharge (시계외 할증)?

A flat 20% added when a ride starts or ends outside Seoul's own administrative boundary, stacked on top of any night surcharge already running. It shows up most often on trips toward Gimpo or Incheon, or back from a day trip just past the city line.

A deluxe (모범) taxi costs more from the first meter, but the premium is smaller than it looks. The same transport portal lists a deluxe taxi's base fare at ₩7,000 for the first 3 kilometers, about 46% above the standard rate, with ₩200 added per 151 meters and per 36 seconds after that. Its late-night rate is flat too: ₩8,400 across the whole 22:00-to-04:00 window, without the standard taxi's steeper jump in the middle of the night.

What is a deluxe taxi (모범택시)?

A black taxi with a yellow sign on the roof, licensed to charge a higher fixed rate than a standard taxi. They're easier to flag at a taxi stand than to catch mid-block, and the extra fare buys a newer car more often than it buys a different driving style.

📱 Kakao T or k.ride: which app actually works for a visitor?

For a short trip, k.ride is the app built to work without a Korean phone number or a Kakao account, and Kakao T usually isn't. Korea JoongAng Daily reported that Kakao Mobility launched k.ride in June 2024 precisely because its main app, Kakao T, requires a Kakao account to log in. k.ride skips that step: a phone number, or a Google, Apple, or email account, gets a rider in instead.

The trade-off is price and choice. k.ride only offers Kakao's four premium car tiers and leaves out the cheaper general taxi option domestic riders default to. It also adds a percentage on top of the regular fare for its translation and support features, though Kakao Mobility hasn't published the exact rate. A card has to sit on file before the first ride, too; there's no option to hand cash to the driver instead.

Live, in-ride translation is the other half of the pitch. The app's own interface runs in English, simplified Chinese, traditional Chinese, and Japanese, destination search works in more than 100 languages, and a built-in translator carries the actual conversation with the driver in Korean. It launched across 14 countries at once, with Kakao Mobility aiming for 30.

🌙 Is hailing a taxi off the street still a fair deal?

Usually, yes, but a well-reported 2025 crackdown shows exactly where it can go wrong. Seoul's city government ran a 100-day campaign starting August 6, 2025, aimed squarely at drivers who overcharged or refused short rides to foreign tourists, concentrated around the airports and districts like Myeongdong.

One case from that reporting shows the size of the gap. A driver charged ₩45,000 for a roughly 10-kilometer ride from Myeongdong to Hongdae that should have cost about ₩12,000 on the meter, and was fined once city inspectors caught it. By the end of June 2025, inspectors near the capital's airports had already logged 139 overcharging cases and 109 refusals of short rides to foreign travelers.

The penalties scale fast, which is exactly why most drivers don't risk a second try. A first offense costs ₩200,000; a second adds a 30-day suspension on top of a ₩400,000 fine; a third costs the license outright, plus ₩600,000.

Booking through an app instead of hailing blind removes most of that guesswork, since a fare range is visible before the car even arrives, and the trip is logged under an account either way.

My take A taxi line outside a club at 2 a.m. is a bad place to be reading an app's terms for the first time. For a short trip with just a handful of rides planned, in your shoes the simpler move would be installing k.ride before the flight, even with its markup, since the card is already registered and nothing has to go wrong in an unfamiliar language that late. If I were staying long enough to ride taxis most days, I'd set up a real Kakao account instead and eat the one-time hassle, since the savings over weeks add up past whatever k.ride charges per ride.

FAQ
Will the driver or car be any different booking through k.ride instead of Kakao T?
No. Launch coverage maps k.ride's four tiers, Standard, Comfort, Deluxe, and Luxury, onto Kakao T's own categories, Blue, Venti, Black, and Deluxe, one for one. Only the branding on the booking screen changes; the car pulling up curbside is identical either way.
By the middle of the night, how close do the two fares actually get?
Barely, in won terms: a mere ₩1,700 stands between the two vehicle classes once the clock passes 02:00, close to one convenience-store snack. Waiting around for a specific color of car costs more minutes than it saves.

Install k.ride before the flight, or set up Kakao T if a Korean number is already in hand, and let the app show a fare range before the door even opens. That one habit clears up most of what still feels uncertain about a street-hailed cab in Seoul.
