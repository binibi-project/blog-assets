Where Locals Actually Eat Budae-Jjigae in Uijeongbu

Uijeongbu's 'original' budae-jjigae shop rates 3.8 on Google; two neighbors beat it by a point or more. Here's where locals actually sit down.

Twenty-some pots boil at once on one short block of Uijeongbu, each restaurant acting like the reason the others exist. The smell reaches the sidewalk before the menu does: ham fat, scallion, kimchi gone sour from an hour of simmering. Servers drop a fistful of ramen into a pot that's still finding its boil, and regulars go after it with chopsticks before the broth has even settled.

None of that started as a craving. It started as hunger, in a Gyeonggi town that happened to sit beside a cluster of American military bases.

🍲 Why Uijeongbu, and why not the famous shop

Budae-jjigae exists because a Korean War-era town turned American base scraps into something worth lining up for, and the street's two best-rated kitchens today aren't the one that started it. After the war left food scarce, people near Uijeongbu's U.S. Army bases worked ham and sausage smuggled off base into kimchi-and-gochujang broth. The dish kept its name, "army base stew," long after it stopped needing an excuse.

Order it today anywhere in Korea and a server will still ask whether you want instant ramen dropped in before the pot finishes boiling. Think of it as jjigae built the way the base-town years forced it to be, every ingredient crowded into one shared pot instead of spread across small side dishes. That crowding is the point: a single order still stretches to feed a fourth friend who just pulled up a chair.

Uijeongbu's Budae-jjigae Street now holds more than 20 restaurants along Hoguk-ro, a stretch the city has formally branded as a food-tourism alley. Korea Times counted "some two dozen" shops still open as of late 2024, each charging roughly ₩10,000 (about $7.45 at ~₩1,343.2/$1) a person.

What is budae-jjigae (부대찌개)?

Literally "army base stew": a shared pot of ham, sausage and kimchi in a gochujang broth, often finished with instant ramen or sliced cheese. It's considered Korean comfort food today, but it started as a post-Korean War way to stretch scarce meat.

Fame and a good bowl aren't the same promise, though. Odeng Sikdang opened the street's first pot in 1960, when founder Heo Gi-suk started out selling fish cakes before adding army-base ham to her broth, and it's still the shop most tourism sites call the birthplace of budae-jjigae.

Checked today, Odeng Sikdang carries a 3.8 rating from 2,870 Google reviews, short of this blog's 4.0-rating bar even with the heaviest review count on the street. Two doors down, Gyeongwon Sikdang holds a 4.4 from 715 reviews and Hyeongne Sikdang a 4.1 from 520.

That gap is wide enough to act on: the rest of this guide skips the famous door and walks two more shops down the block.

What does this blog's "4.0-rating, 30-review" bar filter out?

A Korean restaurant only makes this blog's recommendations if it clears a 4.0 average on Google Maps with at least 30 reviews behind it. Fewer than 30 reviews means the score doesn't count, no matter how high it runs, since a handful of five-star ratings can come from friends and family rather than a track record.

🍢 Gyeongwon Sikdang: the family that never changed the broth

Order here for the version of budae-jjigae that's changed the least: the same anchovy broth, boiled fresh daily, that the shop has used since it opened. Gyeonggi Province's own food column, written by chef and food writer Park Chan-il, profiled Gyeongwon Sikdang as keeping "almost unchanged" recipes for 44 years as of early 2024, putting its founding around 1980.

Founder Kim Myung-nam told the column she still uses imported or domestically sourced ham today, since the days of pulling ingredients straight off the base ended decades ago. Her son, Hwang Seung-hyun, now runs the kitchen, and the provincial magazine ran her shop under its "Gyeonggi's Century-Old Restaurants" column, the kind of write-up reserved for places expected to outlast a single generation.

The shop sits at 경기도 의정부시 둔야로49번길 21, reachable at 0507-1420-5464, a short walk from Uijeongbu Jungang Station's Exit 2 along with the rest of the street. Current hours and an exact menu price aren't posted anywhere this guide could verify, so call ahead rather than guess at a closing time.

Order with "부대찌개 주세요" (bu-dae-jji-gae ju-se-yo, "budae-jjigae, please"). Whether an English menu exists isn't confirmed either, so a photo of the name on your phone is the safer backup.

🍖 Hyeongne Sikdang: the other shop that opened the street

Hyeongne Sikdang was one of only three restaurants on this block when the alley started, and its Google rating has held up better than the most famous of the three. A regional newspaper traced its founder, Park Yong-bok, back to 1972, alongside Odeng Sikdang and a shop called Jumin Sikdang as the street's original trio.

Half a century later it's the steadier bet on paper: a 4.1 Google rating from 520 reviews, comfortably inside this blog's qualifying range.

The restaurant is at 경기 의정부시 호국로1309번길 9, phone 031-846-4833, with budae-jjigae priced at ₩12,000 per person (about $8.93) and larger shareable sets running up to ₩55,000 (about $40.95). Takeout and reservations are both available there.

Hours weren't confirmed in any listing this guide could check, so treat the phone number as the real answer. Order "부대찌개 2인분 주세요" (bu-dae-jji-gae i-in-bun ju-se-yo, "two servings of budae-jjigae, please") if you're splitting a pot, since most tables here are built to share.

My take A 40-minute train ride for dinner is a real ask, especially with the famous door sitting right there, its name in every write-up. Skip Odeng Sikdang's reputation and walk two doors down instead: a full point of Google rating, backed by hundreds of reviews on each side, isn't a close call. In your shoes, the ride out would end at Gyeongwon's table, not the one every blog photographs first.

FAQ
Will a foreign credit card work at either shop?
No page confirms it either way, so keep some won in cash just in case. Old, independently run spots like these commonly use terminals that accept only cards issued inside Korea. A convenience store with an ATM sits near the plaza in front of the station if you need a backup withdrawal.
What if the shop I picked has a line out the door?
Backtrack toward the station exit instead of waiting in place. The block closest to it holds a newer wave of counters that rarely build a weekend queue, since most visitors beeline straight for the two names in this guide.

Neither shop needs a tourist's approval to keep its pots boiling, but if the famous name is the only reason to pick Odeng Sikdang, the rating gap says to walk on. Go to Gyeongwon Sikdang for the broth that hasn't changed in over four decades, and to Hyeongne Sikdang if you want to say you ate at one of the street's original three.
