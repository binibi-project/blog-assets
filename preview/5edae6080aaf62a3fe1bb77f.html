What K-Pop Fans Actually Pay to Message Their Idols

DearU Bubble costs ₩4,500 a month, Weverse DM just rose to $4.59, and Weverse's newest tier isn't messaging at all. What each one actually buys you.

Quick answer
  • Three separate products, three separate prices: DearU Bubble (₩4,500/month), Weverse DM ($4.59/month) and Weverse Digital Membership ($2-$4/month) all sit in the same fandom-app space but aren't interchangeable.
  • Only two of the three promise anything from the artist: Bubble and Weverse DM sell artist messages; Digital Membership sells ad-free video and offline downloads instead.
  • Check which app the group actually uses before paying: look up the group's own official page for a Bubble link or a Weverse community, since the two systems don't cover every artist.

A text arrives at midnight styled like it's just for you, from the member whose face is still your phone's lock screen. It might be. Or it might be the same handful of words that forty thousand other phones just received, all paid for at the same monthly rate. That gap, between how these messages feel and how they actually work, is the whole fandom-messaging business, and in the past two years it has split into three separate paid products, each one a little pricier than it used to be.

📱 What exactly does DearU Bubble sell, and what does it cost?

Bubble sells an ongoing paid chat with an idol, and the base price is ₩4,500 ($3.36) a month per artist through DearU's web store. DearU launched Bubble in February 2020, and The Korea Times describes the mechanic plainly: fans pay to exchange messages with an idol in a running back-and-forth, and some subscriptions let a fan set the nickname the idol uses to address them, though exclusive content from the chat routinely leaks outside the app anyway. Within about a year of launch, subscriptions had passed 1.2 million and the service was already generating ₩5.4 billion a month.

What is Bubble (버블)?

DearU's paid messaging app. A fan pays a monthly fee per artist for an ongoing chat-style exchange with that idol. A fan who follows three members across two groups pays three separate ₩4,500 subscriptions, not one combined fee.

The price has moved since. DearU raised the in-app subscription by about 11% in July 2025 while leaving the web-store price at the same ₩4,500 per person, a gap wide enough that fans started switching to the web store to dodge the app-store markup. By the end of 2025, Bubble carried 695 registered artist accounts, and DearU's average subscriber count had topped 2 million since the second quarter of that year.

The most recent quarter actually dipped a little: 1.97 million domestic subscribers in Q2 2026, down 2% from the previous quarter and down 9% from a year earlier, which DearU's own investor materials tie to which artists currently have a Bubble channel open rather than to the app losing fans broadly. Bubble Japan moved the opposite way, up 71% year-over-year to 108,000 subscribers. None of that slowdown has shown up in the money: DearU's full-year 2025 revenue reached ₩83.835 billion (about $62.5 million), up 12% year-over-year, with operating profit up 23.5% to ₩31.395 billion (about $23.4 million), a jump the company itself credited partly to last July's price increase. Bubble also expanded abroad in 2025, launching inside Tencent Music's QQ Music app in China and reaching Hong Kong through the JOOX app in December.

💬 How is Weverse DM different, and why did the price just rise?

Weverse DM is HYBE's own answer to Bubble, built on the same one-to-many idea, and its monthly price just rose from 15 Jelly ($4.09) to 17 Jelly ($4.59). Weverse Company unveiled it in April 2023, describing it as modeled directly on Phoning, the app made for NewJeans in 2022, and on DearU Bubble. Fans buy a subscription with Jelly, Weverse's in-app currency, and get photos, videos and emoji from the artist, though Bubble had the bigger head start: it already counted 1.35 million monthly active users back in 2022, the year before Weverse DM even launched.

What is Jelly?

Weverse's own in-app currency, bought with real money and spent on subscriptions, digital goods and some Weverse Shop items. Prices inside the app are quoted in Jelly first, with a dollar figure alongside it.

The January 2026 price notice spells out exactly how the increase works: anyone who subscribes through an app market after January 20, 2026 pays the new 17-Jelly rate automatically, while people already subscribed have to actively agree to the new price before their next renewal date or the subscription simply stops. That's a small step easy to miss if you don't read the push notification carefully.

What is Phoning?

A separate messaging app Weverse Company built in 2022 just for NewJeans, before Weverse DM existed as a general product. Weverse's own announcement named it as one of the two services Weverse DM was modeled on.

Weverse DM sits inside a platform that keeps getting bigger. By January 2025, HYBE's Weverse had passed 150 million lifetime downloads, grown its user base by an average of 19% across every continent that year, and added 16 new global artist teams to reach 162 communities total, up 30% year-over-year. A platform still growing that fast has very little reason to lower a price once it's raised.

🎟️ What is Weverse Digital Membership, and is it even messaging?

No. Digital Membership buys ad-free video, offline downloads and unlimited Fan Letters, not a message from anyone, and it launched December 3, 2024 on top of Weverse's existing ₩30,000-a-year ($22.38) community membership. The official notice lists what the fee actually unlocks: a member icon and badge, membership-only content that varies by community, unlimited monthly Fan Letters, up to 30 offline VOD downloads a month, ad-free livestreams and VODs, and extras like Voice Focus and AI upscaling on the video player. None of that is a reply from an idol.

Ahead of launch, Korea Herald's reporting put the tiers at ₩2,700 to ₩5,400 a month ($2-$4), and said Weverse had emailed more than 130 partner labels on September 26, 2024 requiring them to take part, with revenue reportedly split somewhere between 40-70% to the label and 30-60% to Weverse depending on the tier. Fans weren't uniformly happy about it: the same reporting described anger that content once free elsewhere (YouTube clips, old V Live broadcasts, Instagram posts) was effectively being re-monetized through a mandatory Weverse tier.

What is a Fan Letter?

A short written message a fan can send into an artist's Weverse community feed. It isn't a private reply and the artist isn't expected to read every one; Digital Membership removes the monthly cap on how many a subscriber can send.

My take If ₩4,500 feels too small to argue about until you multiply it by three members in two different groups, that reaction is the whole math this market runs on: nobody budgets per app, they budget per artist, and the bill creeps up one subscription at a time. If I were deciding, I'd treat Digital Membership as the one to think about differently from the other two: it's the only product here that doesn't promise anything from the artist at all, just a clearer video and no ads, so you know exactly what you're buying instead of hoping a message was meant for you.
Go deeper
  • If you're weighing a subscription for the first time, remember neither Bubble nor Weverse DM is required just to follow a group: most artists also post for free on Instagram and YouTube, and a paid channel only adds the chance of a direct message on top of that.
  • What fans argue about is whether Digital Membership's mandatory rollout to over 130 labels was HYBE monetizing content that used to be free on other platforms, a complaint Korea Herald's reporting captured from the launch itself.
  • Watch for the "agree to the new price or it cancels" notice: Weverse's own January 2026 change required existing subscribers to actively accept the new rate before their next renewal, and missing that notice just ends the subscription.
FAQ
If I pay, will the message actually be written for me?
Not necessarily. HYBE's own president, Joon Won Choi, admitted to Mashable that what lands in your inbox "is not guaranteed to be personally directed to you," though he maintains a real person is typing it, not AI. The Korea Times found something similar with DearU's competing app: fans compare notes on how often they hear anything back at all, and the researcher it quotes calls the whole format "convenient but passive," meaning paying for access was never a promise of attention.
What happens if I just forget to cancel one of these?
You don't save anything by forgetting. Weverse treats silence as a yes, turning a missed opt-out into an automatic sign-off on whatever figure it just announced. On DearU's side, the cheaper storefront simply never finds you on its own; inattention quietly leaves a fan on the costlier of its two checkout routes, indefinitely.

None of these three services is a bad deal on its own; they're just not the same deal, and the bill only looks small until it's multiplied by every artist and every tier a fan actually wants. Check the price and the renewal terms on the artist's own page before the first charge, not after the second one.
