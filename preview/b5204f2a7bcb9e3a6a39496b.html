Inside Seoul's K-Pop Birthday Cafes: What Fans Actually Pay

A fan fronts ₩100,000-500,000 to rent the cafe, then guarantees 50 drinks sell, before one photocard goes out. How Korea's idol birthday cafes work.

Walk down one of Hongdae's side streets on a Saturday evening and the block looks like a gallery that forgot to open properly. A life-size banner leans against a glass door, someone's face lit from behind in gold. Balloons catch the light from a disco ball nobody asked for twice, and a playlist loops through the same six songs from somewhere behind the counter.

None of this was the cafe's own idea. For about a week, a group of fans who have never run a business took over the lease, the menu, and the music, because it's somebody's birthday and the somebody isn't coming.

What is a birthday cafe (생일카페, saengil kape)?

An ordinary cafe that a K-pop fan or fan group rents for about a week around an idol's birthday, decorated wall to wall with that idol's photos. It's also called a cup sleeve event, after the custom-printed sleeve wrapped around every drink cup.

🎂 What actually happens inside one

A fan or fan club books an ordinary cafe, decorates every wall with the idol's photos, and runs the whole event start to finish with no input from the idol's agency. Walk in and the formula repeats: streamers and balloons, a looping playlist of the idol's songs, and photos the organizer chose covering the walls.

Order a drink and the cup usually comes decorated with a sleeve or sticker made just for the occasion, and a photocard goes out with it, first-come-first-served while the stock lasts.

💸 What it costs the fan who throws one

Running a birthday cafe starts with a deposit of ₩100,000 to 500,000 (about $75-$374), paid to the cafe before a single drink sells. Most contracts also set a sales floor around 50 drinks for the event; come up short, and the organizer pays the cafe the difference out of their own pocket.

One fan's own three-month run-up to a single birthday cafe, from first booking to opening day, came to about ₩3,000,000 (roughly $2,246 at ₩1,336/$1), covering the venue, the decorations, and the custom merch.

On paper, the fan just decorates a cafe for a few days. In practice, they're the one holding the financial risk if the neighborhood doesn't show up to drink coffee for a stranger's favorite idol.

Not every cafe plays it this way. Lucky Ducky, a birthday-cafe specialist in Myeongdong, charges no rental fee at all. "We do not charge a rental fee; we operate solely on profits from beverages and desserts," owner Hynn Han said.

Fan communities complain about the opposite model, one post reading, "I don't understand why, on top of boosting their sales, we also have to guarantee a minimum order count". A Hongdae cafe owner's own advice to organizers was blunt: think carefully before you book.

📍 Where to find one, and what you'll see there

Hongdae is still the center of it: around 100 birthday cafes can be running on the same day in the streets around Hongik University Station and Hapjeong Station. For one especially large birthday, BTS's V's, the count scaled up citywide to about 50 cafes, 30 of them in Seoul.

Two other pockets of Seoul run their own version. Seongsu-dong, in the same district as SM Entertainment's office, draws cafes built for that label's artists, and the blocks near HYBE's Yongsan headquarters fill up for its own roster.

What is a "cafe tour" (생카투어, saengka tour)?

A fan's own route through every cafe dedicated to the same idol on the same day, collecting a different giveaway at each stop. One DAY6 fan told reporters her Wonpil-themed trip around Hongdae was already "my eighth stop" of the day.

Fans find new cafes the way they organize them, through social media rather than one central listing, a habit the format has carried since fan accounts first used Twitter, now X, to coordinate. Scheduling clusters hard around two months: February and September see the heaviest run of idol birthdays, so a cafe worth flying in for may already be booked solid by the time you check.

Popular cafes draw queues that start hours before opening, so the giveaway you actually walk away with can depend on how early you showed up, not how much you spent. If the banners are already coming down by the time you find the address, that's not bad luck: most runs last about a week, and some wrap sooner once the merch is gone.

The format has also outgrown K-pop. Fan groups now run the same kind of event for actors and athletes, and a Buddha's-birthday version near a Seoul temple is now in its third year.

My take If a Seoul trip got built around one specific cafe, the deposit math above would be reason enough to treat it as a bonus stop, not the whole afternoon: the event is run by other fans on a tight budget, not a company with a reputation to protect, so hours can shift or the whole thing can wrap early with no warning. Following the organizer's own post from that same week beats a listing from months back, and getting there close to opening is worth it for a big name, since the best merch tends to go first.

FAQ
Do I have to buy something to get the merch?
Yes, but it need not be the pricier set. Any purchase at the counter earns the same shot at a giveaway, so the cheapest item on the menu counts just as much as the full combo. What disappears first is never the espresso itself but the limited photocards, so walking in during a slow late-afternoon hour can leave you with nothing but caffeine.
Do I need to register or reserve a spot?
No reservation exists for visitors, only for the fan group renting the cafe. Walk-ins are the norm, but a cafe for a major act can draw a line that forms hours before opening, so the real planning is choosing an opening-time slot over a quiet midweek afternoon.

One drink is the easiest ticket into a very specific slice of Seoul fan culture, and the hardest part is just finding out which cafe matters before the week is over. Check an idol's own fan accounts for that week's addresses before building a trip around one cafe, since the map changes faster than any guide can keep up with.
