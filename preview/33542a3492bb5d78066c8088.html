Bukhansan: The Fall Hike You Reach on a T-money Card

Bukhansan is Seoul's own national park: free entry, no cable car, just a subway ride and a bus. What the trip costs, and the 2026 forecast skips.

Quick answer
  • It's the one national park on Seoul's own subway map: Line 3 to Gupabal Station, then a city bus, no KTX or long-distance bus ticket needed.
  • It's free, and there's no shortcut: admission has cost nothing since 2007, and a cable car has been proposed since 2007 too, but never built.
  • Plan around the park's own clock: check the trail control time before you commit to the summit, since rangers stop new entries hours before dark.

Ride Line 3 far enough north and the apartment towers start thinning out before Gupabal Station. Step off the bus a few stops later and the view changes entirely: granite walls rise straight out of a Seoul neighborhood, close enough that the hikers waiting at the crosswalk are already checking their boot laces. There's no other spot in a city of this size where a ten-million-person skyline gives way to real rock this fast, no transfer to a highway bus required.

The catch isn't getting in. It's timing the climb against a mountain that keeps its own hours, not yours.

What is Bukhansanseong (북한산성)?

A mountain fortress built across Bukhansan's ridgelines in the Joseon era, with gates, guard posts and wall sections still standing along the main trail. Most visitors walk straight through its gates without stopping to think of it as a fortress at all; it's just the shape the path takes.

🚇 Getting to Bukhansanseong from Seoul's own subway

Take Line 3 to Gupabal Station, Exit 2, then catch bus 34, 704 or 8772 to the Bukhansanseong stop. The ride ends at the Bukhansanseong Visitor Center at 서울특별시 은평구 대서문길 45, the main entrance on the park's western side. Subway fare runs on the network's usual distance formula: Seoul's own fare schedule sets a ₩1,550 base for the first 10 km, then ₩100 for every 5 km after that. The bus ride on, if it starts within 30 minutes of leaving the subway, folds into the same integrated fare rather than charging twice, with the city bus's own base set at ₩1,500.

From the visitor center, the main path runs past Bukhansan Elementary School to Daeseomun, the fortress's great west gate, then on through Jungseongmun gate toward Baegundong Valley. Keep going past Sanyeongnu Pavilion and the Buwangsa temple ruins, one of the park's best-known spots for maple color, before the trail reaches Amun gate and Samcheonsa Temple. If you end your day at Samcheonsa rather than backtracking, a free shuttle runs to Gupabal Station at 1 p.m., 4 p.m. and 6 p.m..

Bus 34, 704 and 8772 all run regularly through the day, but the exact first and last departures from Gupabal aren't posted anywhere in English; check Naver Map or Kakao Map's real-time arrival board at the stop rather than planning around a guess.

With no entrance fee and no cable car, transit is the only required cost of the whole day. A round trip from somewhere central in Seoul to Gupabal and back, with the connecting bus each way, runs on the fares above with no separate park charge on top.

On paper that makes Bukhansan one of the cheapest full days out of Seoul on this blog. The real cost shows up as sweat, not won: a fortress wall's worth of stairs and, if you push for Baegundae, several hundred meters of elevation with no lift to skip it.

🍁 Does Bukhansan actually turn in October, or later?

The Korea Forest Service's 2026 color map doesn't give Bukhansan its own date, so treat "sometime in the back half of fall" as the honest answer, not a specific week. The agency's map, released September 22, names Seoraksan's peak as October 20, Songnisan's as October 28, Naejangsan's as November 4 and Hallasan's as November 6, with the whole country reaching its color peak in late October. Bukhansan isn't one of the mountains the map calls out by name.

What does "peak" foliage mean?

The Korea Forest Service defines peak (절정) as the day at least half a mountain's leaves have turned, not the single best-looking day. Color keeps building for roughly a week before that point and holds for a few days after, so a visit within a week of any nearby mountain's forecast date still catches real color.

These four dates are the ones the 2026 map actually names; Bukhansan sits somewhere in the same general window, likely closer to Songnisan's date than Hallasan's given how far south Hallasan sits, but that's a guess, not the forecast. Source: Korea Forest Service via SBS Biz, checked 2026-10-07.

Buwangsa's maples, right on the main trail, are usually the first color worth the walk, since the ruins sit low in a valley that catches it early. If the forecast matters more to you than the hike itself, a call to the visitor center (02-356-6198) closer to your date will beat any guess here.

⛰️ Baegundae's granite dome, or just the valley walk?

Baegundae, Bukhansan's highest point at 836.5 meters, is the hike people mean when they say they "did Bukhansan," and it's a half-day commitment, not an hour's detour. The last stretch trades stairs for fixed ropes bolted into the rock, which hikers pull themselves up by hand over hand, and on a crowded weekend the single-file path up and down the same rock face backs up into a bottleneck. There's no food, water or restroom at the top, so whatever you're carrying up has to last the round trip.

The payoff is a bare granite dome with a South Korean flag planted at the summit and a 360-degree view of the city below, and on weekends that flag comes with a line for the photo everyone wants, so the summit isn't the quiet payoff it looks like in photos. You don't have to summit to call the day a win, though. The valley walk from the visitor center to Buwangsa's ruins and back is a flatter, shorter loop through the same fortress gates, with the fall color concentrated in the same stretch Baegundae-bound hikers rush past on their way up. Either way, the park's own trail-hours system decides your turnaround time, not your pace.

What is the 입산시간지정제 (trail entry-hours system)?

A Korea National Park Service rule setting the earliest entry and latest new-entry ("control") time for each trail, so nobody starts a climb with no way down before dark. At Bukhansan, entry opens at 04:00 year-round, and control time is 17:00 from March through November and 16:00 from December through February.

A 2007 push to add a cable car to Bukhansan, backed by a Gangbuk-gu civic group and later by a city council resolution, cleared a regulatory hurdle in 2017 when Seoul moved to a one-stop local approval process for cable car projects. It still required a full environmental impact review, and as of this post, no cable car has been built. Every meter up Baegundae is still on foot.

My take If a KTX ticket is what's been holding you back from a fall-color trip, Bukhansan removes that excuse entirely. Treat the first visit as a scouting trip: do the valley walk to Buwangsa's ruins, see how the fortress gates and the color actually sit together, and decide from there whether Baegundae's half-day climb is worth booking a full morning for next time. There's no cable car waiting to bail you out partway up, so the decision is worth making before you're already on the trail.
Go deeper
  • If you have one more hour Push past Buwangsa's ruins to Amun gate before turning back toward Samcheonsa; it's the same trail, just further into the fortress, and the free shuttle from Samcheonsa saves the walk back to Gupabal.
  • What locals argue about Whether Bukhansan should finally get a cable car after nearly two decades of proposals, or whether a mountain inside a city of ten million is exactly the kind of place that should stay walk-only.
  • Watch for The trail's control time. Rangers stop new entries at 17:00 in summer and 16:00 in winter, so a late-afternoon start can mean turning back before you reach the top.
FAQ
What should I pack that isn't obvious?
Shoes with real tread matter more than anything else on the packing list. One hiking account recommends them for a slick final stretch near the top, plus optional gloves for pulling yourself up by hand, and notes that nothing is sold once you're past the park gate: no meals, no drinks, no toilet. Sort that out before you leave the subway station, not after.
Is this place actually as busy as it sounds?
By one measure, busier than almost anywhere comparable: a 2012 count logged about 5 million annual visits here, a figure Korean media tied to a Guinness World Records entry for the most-visited national park per unit of land anywhere. That number is over a decade old now, but it explains why a clear Saturday morning still means company the whole way up.

A mountain this close to a subway line will always draw a crowd, but it still turns color on its own schedule, not the forecast's. Pick a half-day plan you'd be happy with even if the peak color is a week off either way, and you won't need to chase an exact date to make the trip worth it.
