Papago vs. ChatGPT: What Actually Works in Korea

A Seoul local who uses AI chatbots daily compares Papago, Google Translate, ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude for reading Korean signs and menus.

Five apps, one phone, and only two of them were actually built to translate a sign while you're standing in front of it. The other three just picked up translation as a side effect of getting better at everything else, and in a few specific moments, that side effect is the one that saves the trip.

A visitor lands at Incheon with Google Translate already installed and assumes that's the whole toolkit. A local giving directions in Gangnam knows it's closer to five overlapping toolkits, each good at a different kind of confusion: a menu board, a delivery notice, a landlord's text message, a government form.

📱 Papago vs. Google Translate: Which One Actually Reads Korean?

Papago wins the speed test on anything written in Korean; Google Translate wins on everything else. Both are free, and both read a photo instead of making you type.

What is AR real-time translation?

Point your phone's camera at a sign without taking a photo, and the app overlays a translation directly on the live camera view. Papago calls this AR real-time translation, and Google Translate's camera mode works the same way.

🤖 Where ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude Actually Help

A chatbot can't beat a dedicated translation app at reading a sign on the fly, but it can explain the sign once you've read it. That's the gap the usual "Papago vs. Google Translate" guides skip: none of them mention that the three big AI chatbots quietly picked up real translation features in 2026, or that one of them comes with an official warning about Korean.

OpenAI's own image-input FAQ says plainly that the model "does not perform as well" on images with text in non-Latin alphabets, naming Japanese and Korean specifically. That's not a rumor from a review site; it's OpenAI's own documentation. So a photo of a Korean lease or a hospital discharge note is exactly the kind of image where ChatGPT's read deserves a second check, not blind trust.

Where ChatGPT does pull ahead is voice. GPT-Live, the voice model now running ChatGPT's Voice mode, can hold a live spoken conversation and translate as it goes, and OpenAI rolled it out to free users as well as paying ones starting July 8, 2026. Google is building something similar, Gemini 3.5 Live Translate, which does near real-time speech-to-speech translation in more than 70 languages. The detail worth knowing: Google is shipping that model into the Google Translate app itself, not into the separate Gemini chatbot, so "open Gemini to translate" isn't quite right yet.

Claude sits a step back from both. It reads an uploaded photo in standard formats and can walk through what a document says, but it has no live voice mode and no official benchmark for Korean specifically. Treat any chatbot's photo-read of a Korean document the way you'd treat a friend's rough guess: useful for understanding the gist, not for signing anything.

💸 What It Costs to Go Beyond the Free Tier

All five apps work for free; the paid tiers only matter if you want a chatbot as your daily assistant, not just your travel translator. ChatGPT Plus and Claude Pro are both ₩26,865 (US$20) a month, though Claude also sells an annual plan at ₩22,835 (US$17) a month, billed as ₩268,646 (US$200) up front. Google's AI Pro plan runs about ₩26,851 (US$19.99) a month as of May 2026, roughly the same bracket as the other two.

None of those subscriptions are required just to translate. Papago and Google Translate stay free no matter how often you open them, and the free tier of every chatbot already includes photo uploads. The paid plans buy more daily messages and faster responses, which matters for someone using a chatbot for work every day, not for a one-week trip.

My take I use Claude, Gemini or ChatGPT most days, for work rather than for travel, and the gap between what they're good at and what Papago is good at still surprises me. When I've given directions to foreigners who stopped me on a Gangnam sidewalk, nobody ever pulled up a chatbot first; they pulled up whatever translated the sign in front of them fastest, which was almost always Papago or Google Translate. Keep one of those two as your default camera, and save the chatbots for the message or notice that needs explaining, not just converting. If I were setting up a stranger's phone before their flight, Papago and a backup plan for anything official would be the whole kit.
FAQ
Does it matter whether I type or use the camera?
Typing wins whenever the exact wording matters, like copying a prescription name for a pharmacist. A lens can misread small print or a tilted angle in a way manual entry can't. Save the camera for a quick glance at a menu board, where close enough is good enough.
Is it worth keeping more than one of these installed?
Yes, because each one fails differently. A dedicated app can't tell you why a landlord's text sounds urgent; a chatbot can't match an instant camera read while you're holding up a queue. Every option here costs nothing at its entry tier, so there's no real downside to carrying two.

Open Papago first for anything written, switch to ChatGPT's voice mode if a conversation needs to flow both ways, and save Claude or Gemini for the moment a photo needs explaining rather than just translating. Prices and hours were checked on 2026-11-26; they change, so double-check before you go.
