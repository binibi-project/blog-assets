War Memorial of Korea: Free, Huge, Easy to Underplan

What the War Memorial of Korea costs, its hours, how to reach it by subway, and the order that keeps a free afternoon from running short.

  • It's bigger than a free museum has any right to be: indoor halls plus an outdoor field of tanks and jets.

A free museum sounds like a two-hour detour, the kind of stop you pencil in between lunch and a coffee shop. The War Memorial of Korea doesn't play along.

Walk past the entrance plaza in Yongsan and the building keeps going, hall after hall, until you step outside into a field where retired fighter jets and tanks sit close enough to touch. Inside, a quieter room lists the names of soldiers who didn't come home, and that room asks something different of you than the plane you just photographed. It's the kind of place that rearranges an afternoon without warning you first.

What is the War Memorial of Korea (전쟁기념관)?

A national museum in Yongsan-gu, Seoul, built around Korea's military history, with the Korean War (1950-1953) at its center. It mixes the register of a history museum (halls, timelines, artifacts) with the register of a memorial (a wall naming the dead), which is why the mood shifts as you move through it.

🎟️ Is the War Memorial of Korea actually free?

Yes, general admission costs nothing, every day the museum is open. Seoul's own tourism media hub lists the War Memorial as a free attraction with no mention of a separate fee for the permanent exhibits. The free docent-guided tours that run at set times through the day need no advance registration either.

It's open Tuesday through Sunday, 09:30 to 18:00, with last entry at 17:30, and it's closed every Monday. If a Monday falls inside a public holiday stretch, the museum closes the day after the holiday run instead, and it also closes outright on New Year's Day, Seollal and Chuseok.

What is Yongsan?

A central Seoul district that sits between Itaewon and the Han River, named for the hill ("yong-san," dragon mountain) it's built around. It's also where Samgakji Station and the museum's address both land, so it's the name you'll see on every sign pointing here.

The one exception to "walk right in" is the children's museum wing. Mediahub says it takes online reservations that open 14 days before the visit and close once a session starts, with on-site booking as a backup if the online slots are full; weekend and holiday slots can sell out quickly.

🚇 Getting to Samgakji: the subway route and fare

Samgakji Station is the stop, and a single T-money ride there costs ₩1,550. Three subway exits reach the museum: Line 4's Exit 1 (about a 5-minute walk), Line 6's Exit 12 (about 3 minutes), and Namyeong Station on Line 1, Exit 1, about 10 minutes away as a backup option. The museum's own address is 29 Itaewon-ro, Yongsan-gu, Seoul.

The ₩1,550 figure is the current card fare across Seoul's subway system, up ₩150 since June 28, 2025, when the city raised the basic fare from ₩1,400. Paying cash for a single-journey ticket costs ₩1,650 instead. That ₩100 gap is the deposit you'd otherwise get back at a ticket machine, so a T-money card is the cheaper way to go either way.

⏰ What should I see if I only have two hours?

Go indoors first, save the outdoor planes and tanks for late afternoon, and let the War History Hall anchor whichever half you pick. Mediahub's own visiting tip splits the museum this way on purpose: the indoor galleries stay out of direct sun no matter the hour, while the outdoor field of tanks, fighter jets and missiles is more comfortable once the afternoon sun has eased off.

That order also matches how the two halves feel. Indoors, a quieter stretch displays the Allied nations memorial and plaques honoring the soldiers who died in the war; outdoors, kids run between tank treads and point up at cockpit canopies. Going in on that order, grief first, hardware after, lands better than doing it the other way around.

If you're traveling with family, mediahub's guide breaks the highlights down by who you're with: younger kids do best with the children's museum plus the outdoor field and the War History Hall; older kids can skip straight to the outdoor field and War History Hall; and as a foreign visitor, the Allied nations memorial and the fallen soldiers' memorial stones are the two sections built with you specifically in mind. For a group of 10 or more, including school groups from grade 4 up, military units and community groups, the museum takes tour bookings through its own website, 30 to 3 days ahead of the visit.

The museum sits next to the National Museum of Korea, close enough that mediahub's guide suggests combining the two into one day — which is exactly the plan that burns a two-hour visitor's whole afternoon if they try it without cutting something. Pick one half, not both, if the clock is already tight.

The outdoor field alone can eat an hour before you've set foot inside: a MiG-19 fighter, training aircraft and transport planes sit close enough to walk around on every side. If you're short on time, that field is the one section worth protecting, since it's the part a photo from the entrance plaza won't show you. A past renovation already rearranged the large weapons hall indoors, so even a repeat visitor may find part of that route has moved.

My take A free museum is an easy thing to half-trust on a tight Seoul itinerary, the kind of stop you keep flexible in case something better comes up. I'd flip that instinct here. Block out at least two hours, more if you're pairing it with the National Museum next door, because the ₩0 price tag hides a site that's genuinely large, not a quick photo op between better-known stops. If your day is already full, the outdoor field is the part to protect; you can read about the indoor halls later, but you can't photograph a MiG-19 from a travel blog.

FAQ
Can I bring a stroller, or an older relative who can't walk far?
Go ahead and bring it. The grounds are built to be both stroller- and wheelchair-accessible, and a small cafe sits inside the building with a separate restaurant near the parking lot if a short stop turns into lunch. Neither spot is reviewed here, so treat them as a backup rather than the day's meal plan.
Who actually leads the free tours, and do I need to book a seat?
No booking needed, and many of the volunteer guides once wore the uniform themselves, which changes the tone of a walk-through more than a printed sign ever could. Sessions run in Korean, with separate rounds offered for a few other languages, so a traveler who skips the tour entirely still has the posted signage as a fallback.

The free ticket is the easy part of this trip; the time budget is where visitors actually trip up. Give it the two hours it needs, go indoors before you go outside, and you'll leave with more photos of tank treads than you planned for.
