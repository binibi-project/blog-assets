Gyeonghuigung Palace: Free, Quiet, and About to Change

Gyeonghuigung is the only one of Seoul's five grand palaces that never charges admission, and a 2035 redevelopment plan could eventually change that.

Half past two on a Tuesday, and the red-maple path past Heunghwamun gate holds a couple of retirees on a bench, nobody else in sight. A few minutes' walk from Gwanghwamun Square, where Gyeongbokgung's courtyard is thick with selfie sticks most afternoons, that kind of quiet feels almost like a mistake. Gyeonghuigung earned it the hard way: dismantled under Japanese colonial rule, turned into a schoolyard for more than three decades, and still, even after restoration, the one grand palace in the city that never charges a fee to walk through. Walk through Sungjeongmun on a weekday morning and it barely feels like the same city as Gyeongbokgung.

💸 Is Gyeonghuigung really free?

Yes, every day it's open, with no ticket booth and no fee. Gyeongbokgung and Changdeokgung both charge ₩3,000 for an adult ticket. Changgyeonggung and Deoksugung charge ₩1,000, and so do Jongmyo and the Joseon royal tombs. Gyeonghuigung sits outside that whole system: it isn't managed by the Cultural Heritage Administration like the other four, it's run by the Seoul Metropolitan Government through the Seoul Museum of History, which was built right on the old palace grounds.

What are Seoul's "Five Grand Palaces"?

Gyeongbokgung, Changdeokgung, Changgyeonggung, Deoksugung plus Gyeonghuigung: the five Joseon-era royal palaces still standing in central Seoul. Herald Economy's report on Korea's unchanged heritage-site fees covers a government review, due to take effect January 1, 2027, for four of them plus Jongmyo; Gyeonghuigung isn't part of that review at all.

Fees for the other four from Herald Economy's report on Korea's unchanged heritage-site prices, checked 2026-09-25; Gyeonghuigung's free entry confirmed separately by Seoul's own media hub.

On the chart, Gyeonghuigung just looks like a rounding error next to its siblings, and that gap is the whole pitch. It also means the ₩6,000 combined-ticket math that applies to the other four simply doesn't apply here at all.

🚶 How do you get there, and what's behind the gate?

Take Seoul Subway Line 5 to Seodaemun Station, use Exit 4, and Heunghwamun gate is about a 6-minute walk. The address is 45 Saemunan-ro, Jongno-gu, for a map app that wants a specific pin. Past Heunghwamun, a second gate called Sungjeongmun leads to Sungjeongjeon, the main hall, rebuilt using excavated foundations and historical layout records after the Seoul Metropolitan Government began restoring the site in 1990.

Why did Heunghwamun gate disappear for over fifty years?

Japan's colonial government moved Heunghwamun in 1932 to Bakmunsa, a Japanese temple built to honor Ito Hirobumi, Korea's first Japanese resident-general. The gate wasn't brought back to the palace site until 1988, and even then not to its exact original spot.

That colonial-era damage runs deeper than the gate. Sungjeongjeon itself was sold off in 1926 to a Japanese temple and survives today as a hall at Dongguk University; the building standing at Gyeonghuigung now is a faithful new reconstruction, not the original. Seoul High School then occupied the whole site from 1946 until 1980, before the city bought the land back and started piecing the palace together again.

🍁 When's the best time to see it?

Late November is usually the window for the red-maple path's peak color, though a mild fall can stretch it further. Round the back of Taeryeongjeon hall and there's a plain boulder with a trickle of spring water running off it, easy to walk past if you don't already know what it is. That rock is basically the reason Gyeonghuigung exists at all: a 17th-century rumor held that royal fortune pooled in this exact ground, and King Gwanghaegun believed it enough to seize his half-brother's family home on the site and build an entire secondary palace around it, starting in 1617.

What is Seoam (서암)?

The boulder and spring behind Taeryeongjeon hall, named for the belief that it carried "royal energy." It's the origin legend behind the whole palace: Gwanghaegun had the land cleared and a new palace built here chasing that same rumor in 1617, under what was then the name Gyeongdeokgung, later renamed Gyeonghuigung in 1760.

Seeing Seoam costs nothing extra and takes about two minutes out of the walk, though skipping it means missing the actual reason this free palace is sitting where it is.

🏗️ What's changing by 2035?

Seoul's 2024 master plan turns Gyeonghuigung and four nearby public sites into a 136,000㎡ history-culture park by 2035, and the first phase has already broken ground. Work on a history garden between Heunghwamun and Sungjeongmun got cultural-heritage approval and began by the end of 2024, the Seoul Office of Education is moving out of its site next door in 2026 to free up land, and restoring Donuimun, the one Great Gate of old Seoul still missing, is planned as a longer-term goal with no fixed date yet.

None of that planning language mentions a new entrance fee, and Gyeonghuigung isn't part of the Cultural Heritage Administration's separate fee review landing on the other four palaces in 2027 either. A quiet, decade-long, multi-site construction project is also exactly the kind of thing that could change that without much warning, so treat the free, empty version described here as a now thing, not a forever thing.

My take If you're deciding whether a free stop is worth squeezing into an already packed Seoul itinerary, in your shoes, I'd say yes, but only if you're already near Gwanghwamun or Seodaemun that day. Gyeonghuigung isn't dramatic enough to build a whole separate trip around, and the real draw isn't the architecture, it's getting to hear your own footsteps inside a Joseon palace for once. That won't last once the construction speeds up, so I'd go now rather than wait for the version with a gift shop and a timed-entry line.

FAQ
Some travel blogs list a ₩3,000 entry fee. Is that outdated, or did I miss something?
It doesn't match anything official. Every government or city-run listing checked for this guide puts the figure at zero, with nothing to waive for a hanbok or a discount card because there was never a fee to begin with. Treat a stray ₩3,000 next to Gyeonghuigung's name as a leftover from a template some travel blog reused without updating, not a tip you're somehow missing out on.
Are there restrooms or a place to sit once you're inside?
Benches and rest areas run throughout the grounds, which matters more here than at most palaces, since no food or drink vendor inside Gyeonghuigung shows up on its official listings.

Gyeonghuigung won't out-dazzle Gyeongbokgung, and it isn't trying to. What it offers instead is the one thing none of Seoul's other palaces can promise anymore: a quiet walk through a Joseon throne hall that costs nothing and crowds nobody.
