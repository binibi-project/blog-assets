How to Make Doenjang Jjigae (Soybean Paste Stew)

The Korean Food Promotion Institute's doenjang jjigae for four, in grams and milliliters, with the water mix-up on the page and a miso fallback.

Quick answer
  • A Korean institute's recipe for four: the Korean Food Promotion Institute's version uses 4 Tbsp of doenjang plus beef and tofu.
  • Low heat does the work: the paste simmers gently for 10 minutes before anything else goes in, then the potatoes get 15.
  • Get the right paste: buy Korean doenjang, not miso, from a Korean or Asian grocer before you start.

Four tablespoons of brown paste, two cups of water and ten minutes on a low flame: that's the whole base of the stew. The smell when the paste first loosens into hot water is deep and salty, a little funky too. Then the potatoes soften and the tofu goes in last, and the pot turns into something you want to eat with a bowl of plain rice and nothing else. For a dish with this much depth, the ingredient list is short.

The recipe here comes from the Korean Food Promotion Institute (한식진흥원), the Seoul institute that promotes Korean food, and it's adapted below with grams and milliliters kept as the page gives them.

What is jjigae (찌개)?

A Korean stew with less broth than guk or tang, the brothier soups. The Encyclopedia of Korean Culture defines it as meat or vegetables simmered with gochujang or doenjang until only a little broth is left, and doenjang jjigae is also called tojang jjigae.

🫘 What is doenjang, and can miso stand in?

Doenjang is Korea's fermented soybean paste, and miso works only as a milder fallback. The Encyclopedia of Korean Culture describes doenjang as the solids left behind when the liquid is ladled off soybean blocks fermented in brine; that liquid becomes soy sauce. It's old, too. The same entry notes doenjang appears in the Samguk Sagi among the wedding gift items of King Sinmun of Silla.

What is meju (메주)?

Cooked soybeans shaped into blocks, then fermented and dried; both soy sauce and doenjang start from it. The Korean Food Promotion Institute's own doenjang recipe starts with meju that has aged in salt water for 40 days or more, and says the finished paste is ready to eat after about a month in the crock.

Picture miso and doenjang as cousins who grew up in different kitchens. Miso adds koji and grains, while doenjang leans on soybeans and salt, and Food Republic notes some miso is ready within a week, against six weeks or more for doenjang. The result is a paste that's sharper and grainier, and the same piece says you'd need only half as much doenjang to stand in for miso.

So what does that mean for your pot? Swap in miso and the stew comes out gentler, so taste before you decide it needs more salt. Pick up a tub of Korean doenjang before you start. Weee!, an Asian grocery delivery service, carries CJ Foods' Korean doenjang in a 2.2 lb tub.

🛒 Ingredients for four, in grams

Everything except the doenjang is in a regular supermarket. The quantities below are the institute's, for four servings.

Read the water line twice. The ingredient list says 2 cups (500 ml), but the first cooking step says 2½ cups, which works out to about 625 ml at the page's own 250 ml cup. Start with 500 ml and keep the extra half cup by the stove; jjigae is meant to be on the thick side, and it's easier to loosen a stew than to rescue a watery one.

Tofu and vegetables make up most of the weight, and the beef is only 100 g across four bowls. Here the meat seasons the broth more than it fills the bowl. If you can't find Korean green chilies, a mild green chili keeps the same fresh bite without taking over.

🍲 How do you make doenjang jjigae step by step?

Make a gentle doenjang broth first, then add things in order of how long they take to cook. Here are the institute's steps, one action each:

  1. Slice the beef thin.

  2. Cut the tofu, potato and mushrooms into 1 cm cubes.

  3. Cut the onion and green chilies into 1 × 1 cm squares.

  4. Put the water in a pot and stir in the doenjang until no lumps are left.

  5. Simmer it on low heat for 10 minutes to make the doenjang broth.

  6. Add the beef and skim off the foam as it rises.

  7. Add the potato and onion and cook on low heat for 15 minutes.

  8. Add the tofu, mushrooms, green chilies, chopped scallion, minced garlic and gochugaru, then boil a little longer.

Where it usually goes wrong is step 4. The page asks you to stir the paste into the water until no lumps are left, and a small sieve plus the back of a spoon makes that quicker. Then keep the flame low, since the page asks for low heat both for the broth and for the potatoes.

The other easy slip is adding the tofu with the potatoes. In the institute's order it goes in at the very end, followed by only a short boil.

Serve it straight from the pot with rice. The Encyclopedia of Korean Culture gives one reason why Koreans love jjigae so much: a spoonful of a well-seasoned stew makes plain, unsalted rice easy to eat. That's the pairing to aim for, so don't salt the rice.

My take A tub of brown paste that smells this strong can feel like a big commitment for one recipe, especially if nobody at home has tasted it before. Honestly? I'd make this exact version first, with 500 ml of water and every one of the 4 Tbsp, before changing anything. If it tastes too strong, that's useful information about your brand of paste, and the fix next time is half a spoon less, not a different recipe.
Go deeper
  • If you have one more hour Swap the plain water for stock. The institute's own tips suggest anchovy or clam broth, and list radish, bell pepper and kimchi among the vegetables that go well in the pot.
  • What locals argue about Whether it should be red at all. The Encyclopedia of Korean Culture lists three ways to make it: doenjang alone, doenjang with coarse chili powder, or doenjang mixed with a gochujang made for stews.
  • Watch for Summer. The same entry says gang-doenjang jjigae, a doenjang-heavy version cooked in an earthenware pot with only green chilies, is the one to make in hot weather.
FAQ
My doenjang tastes too salty. Can I fix the paste itself?
Yes, two pantry items fix it. Stir in kongaru (콩가루, soybean flour), then adjust the texture with guk-ganjang (국간장), as the institute's doenjang recipe advises. Shop-bought tubs belong chilled, it adds.
Did Korea's royal court eat this too?
A version of it, under another name. Joseon palace kitchens called jjigae "jochi" and split it into a clear kind and a doenjang-based kind, and the king's table always carried both.

For a first pot, follow the institute's order exactly and only tweak the water. Once it works, try the anchovy stock, the first stock the institute's own tips name.
