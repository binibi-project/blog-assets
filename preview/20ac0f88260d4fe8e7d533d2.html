One pot does nearly all the work here, but it earns the hour and a half it takes to get there. The ribs go in pale, swimming in a thin, ginger-scented water that smells more like a stockpot than a holiday, and for the first stretch nothing about the kitchen suggests a special occasion is coming. Then the braising liquid goes in, the lid comes off near the end, and the sauce tightens around the bone until it's dark and glossy, thick enough to coat the back of a spoon. By the time the carrots have gone soft and the smell has turned sweet and a little like roasted garlic, the dish has gone from something you'd hesitate to serve to the one people ask for by name.

Galbijjim isn't the same dish as the grilled short ribs most people picture first when they hear "galbi." Tabletop galbi is quick and smoky; galbijjim is slow and wet, built on the thicker center-cut ribs that, because of where they sit on the animal, have long cost more than other beef cuts in Korea. That price tag is part of why the dish shows up at Chuseok and other holiday tables rather than a Tuesday dinner, traditionally served from one shared, lidded bowl rather than plated out.

What is galbijjim (갈비찜)?

Beef or pork short ribs braised slowly in a soy-and-pear sauce until the meat pulls easily from the bone. It's the "holiday table" version of short ribs, distinct from grilled galbi, and the center-cut ribs it calls for have long been pricier than other beef cuts in Korea.

The ratio below follows Korean Bapsang's galbijjim, kept close to how Hyosun Ro gives it, built for 4 servings from 3 pounds of bone-in short ribs.

Searing isn't traditional here, and skipping it is the easier choice, not the lesser one. Korean home cooking parboils the ribs rather than browning them in a pan first; searing is a newer habit borrowed from Western-style braises, and the recipe treats it as optional rather than correct. If you do sear, skip the parboil below instead of doing both.

🥘 The parboil, the stock, and the 40-minute finish

The parboil isn't really about cooking the meat; it's about cleaning the liquid that becomes the sauce, the same instinct behind skimming a pot of stock before you'd call it broth. Start by rinsing the ribs and soaking them in cold water for about 30 minutes to draw out the blood, then drain before anything touches heat. Boil 5 cups of water with the onion, the ginger, the garlic, and the scallion whites, add the ribs, and skim the foam as it rises for about 10 minutes, then pull the ribs out and keep the stock. Refrigerating that stock before skimming the fat off the top lets the fat set into one solid layer that lifts away cleanly, instead of chasing grease around a hot pot.

Pour the mixed braising liquid, minus the sesame oil, over the ribs, add 2.5 cups of the reserved stock, and simmer 20 to 30 minutes depending on how thick the ribs are cut. The radish, the shiitake and the carrot go in next for about 20 minutes covered, then any optional chestnuts, jujube or ginkgo nuts for a final 10 minutes uncovered, which is also what thickens the sauce and gives the ribs their shine.

What's a hap (합)?

A lidded serving bowl traditionally used for galbijjim instead of a plate, meant for everyone at the table to reach into. The name describes the stew, but the way it reaches the table is part of the tradition too.

The sesame oil at the end is where a first attempt usually goes wrong, not the braise itself. The recipe calls for 2 tablespoons stirred in right before the heat goes off, but more than one reader found that amount overpowering and cut it back to 2 teaspoons instead. Add half the amount, taste it, then finish with the rest only if the sauce needs it.

🛒 Buying the ribs, and the pear, outside Korea

A regular supermarket's "short ribs" are usually the English cut this recipe wants, not the thin-cut strips sold for grilling. English cut comes as thick, single-bone pieces built for a long braise, while the flanken or LA-galbi cut is sliced thin across several bones for a quick grill; Weee!'s own listing for a 1.5 lb package of bone-in short ribs, priced at $23.99, labels its cut "Flanken Style," which is the one worth skipping for this dish and saving for a barbecue instead.

English cut vs. flanken cut: what's the difference?

English cut is sliced parallel to the bone into thick, single-bone pieces, the shape a long braise like this one wants. Flanken, also sold as LA galbi, is cut thin across several bones at once, which is why it's the one you grill instead of braise for an hour or more.

The Asian pear is the one ingredient without a close substitute nearby in most Korean grocery aisles abroad, but Korean Bapsang's own notes say a Bosc pear or an apple works in its place, and regular mirin stands in for the Korean rice wine without changing the method. Neither swap is in the ingredient table above because it changes the sauce slightly sweeter or slightly sharper, but both get a finished dish close enough to serve without apology.

If a Weee! order or a Korean grocery run isn't an option, ask a regular butcher counter for "English-cut beef short ribs" by name; it's a standard cut, just not always labeled in a way that matches a Korean recipe card.

My take Three pounds of a cut that costs more than most other beef at the counter is a real commitment before the stove's even on, and it's fair to want some certainty it'll turn out. If I were making this for the first time, I'd buy the English cut specifically, follow the parboil instead of searing, and hold the sesame oil back until the last minute to taste before committing to the full two tablespoons. The ribs forgive almost everything else; the sauce, once it's over-seasoned, doesn't.

FAQ
What should I actually serve alongside it?
Plain steamed rice, no substitutes: the whole point of a thick, glossy sauce is a bowl underneath to catch it. Add one or two simple banchan on the side, cucumber kimchi or seasoned spinach work well, to cut the richness rather than compete with it. A heavy, special-occasion stew like this doesn't need a crowded table around it.
Can I prep this a day ahead for guests?
Yes — stop as soon as the marinade is poured over the parboiled meat, then slide the whole pot into the fridge overnight instead of finishing it that evening. Pull it out the next day, bring it back to a simmer, and drop in the vegetables only once it's bubbling again; nothing about the flavor suffers for the wait.

Buy the cut by name instead of guessing, and let the parboil do the cleanup work the braise can't undo later. Taste the sesame oil before committing to all of it, and the rest of the recipe takes care of itself.
