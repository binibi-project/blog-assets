How to Make Haemul Pajeon (Korean Seafood Pancake)

Korean Bapsang's haemul pajeon for four: cup and tablespoon amounts, the wet-seafood mistake that ruins the crisp, and where to buy buchim garu abroad

Quick answer
  • Hyosun's Korean Bapsang recipe for four: a thin batter of buchim garu and ice water, loaded with scallions and whatever seafood you have.
  • The soggy pancake is almost always water's fault: wet seafood and thick batter are the two things that stop it from crisping.
  • Fix it before you fry: drain every wet ingredient and keep the batter thin, thinner than a Western pancake batter.

Ever made a Korean pancake at home and ended up with a greasy, floppy disc instead of something crisp? The gap between the two is smaller than it looks, and it has almost nothing to do with the seafood you picked.

A good haemul pajeon comes off the pan lacy and golden at the edges, with the scallions still holding some bite underneath a layer of shrimp or clam. The whole thing takes about half an hour, start to finish, and most of that is just letting the pan do its job.

The recipe below is adapted from Korean Bapsang, the site run by the Korean-American home cook Hyosun, with her cup and tablespoon amounts kept as she gives them.

What is jeon (전)?

Korea's broad category of batter-coated, pan-fried food. Wikipedia's entry on pajeon describes it as a kind of jeon where scallion is the main ingredient, made from a batter of eggs, wheat flour, rice flour and scallions, with a lighter texture than a Chinese scallion pancake because the batter is thinner.

🦑 What actually goes into haemul pajeon?

"Haemul" just means seafood, and the recipe leaves the exact mix up to you. In Korean, a seafood pajeon is called haemul pajeon (해물파전), and Wikipedia names oysters and shrimp as common picks, alongside squid or clams, for the batter and toppings. Hyosun's version calls for 1 to 1½ cups of any combination, cut bite-size, plus 1 to 2 bunches of scallions and an optional egg and red chili.

Picture an American pancake: batter first, maybe a few blueberries dropped in after. Haemul pajeon runs the opposite way. Converted to one unit, there's more seafood in a batch than dry pancake mix. That ratio is the whole point: this is a seafood dish held together by a little flour, not a flour dish with seafood scattered on top. Skimp on the seafood to save money and you haven't made a thinner pajeon, you've made a different dish.

What is buchim garu (부침가루)?

A pre-mixed Korean pancake flour, already seasoned with salt and usually a little garlic or onion powder. CJ's Beksul version, the brand Hyosun's recipe calls for, sells on the Korean grocery delivery service Weee! in a 2.2 lb bag for $5.49.

No buchim garu nearby? Hyosun's own substitute is a dry mix of ¾ cup flour, 2 Tbsp rice flour, 2 Tbsp potato or cornstarch, 1 tsp garlic (minced or powder) and ½ tsp salt, which makes about 1 cup of mix. Use regular rice flour, not the sweet kind; she notes sweet rice flour turns the pancake sticky instead of crisp.

🍳 How do you make haemul pajeon step by step?

Drain everything wet, keep the batter thin, and fry in two batches. Here's Hyosun's method, one action per step:

  1. Drain the seafood and scallions well, and pat the chili pepper dry too. Wet ingredients are the single biggest reason a pajeon turns out soggy.

  2. Halve the scallions crosswise, and split any thick white parts lengthwise.

  3. Toss the drained seafood with the sesame oil.

  4. Whisk the pancake mix with the ice-cold water, adding more a tablespoon at a time. The target is a batter thinner than a Western pancake batter but thicker than a crepe batter; on the thin side gives a crispier result.

  5. Stir half the seafood into the batter.

  6. Heat 3-4 Tbsp of vegetable oil in a non-stick pan over medium-high heat, then ladle in half the batter and spread it into a thin round.

  7. Lay the scallions over the batter in a single layer and press them in. Add the remaining seafood and a few chili slices.

  8. Spoon half the beaten egg on top if using, then cook 3-4 minutes until the bottom turns golden brown. Lower the heat if it's browning too fast.

  9. Flip, add 2-3 Tbsp of oil around the edges, and cook another 3-4 minutes. Repeat with the rest of the batter for the second pancake.

Where it usually goes wrong is step 1, not step 8. A soggy pancake almost never means the pan was too cool; it means the seafood or the scallions were still wet when they hit the batter. A thick batter is the other common culprit, so err toward thin rather than toward a Western-style pancake batter.

Serve it hot, sliced into wedges, with the dipping sauce on the side. Wikipedia names this sauce cho ganjang (초간장), a slightly sweet mix of soy sauce, vinegar and sesame oil served with pajeon generally, close to the soy-vinegar version in the table above.

🌧️ Why do Koreans eat this on a rainy day?

Ask around and you'll hear it's the sound, not the weather, that pulls people toward a pan of pajeon. Nanam Myszka, a Korean cook interviewed by America's Test Kitchen, says her mother and grandmother both say the same thing when rain starts: time for pajeon and makgeolli. Her own theory is that the sizzle of batter hitting hot oil echoes the tap of rain on a roof, and the same piece notes that pajeon ingredients and makgeolli sales both tend to rise on wet days.

What is dongdongju (동동주)?

An unfiltered, lightly fizzy rice wine close to makgeolli, usually poured from a bottle or kettle into small metal cups. It's a common partner for pajeon on a rainy table, the way America's Test Kitchen describes the makgeolli pairing.

A second, older story claims a specific birthplace. Wikipedia says Dongnae pajeon takes its name from Dongnaeeupseong, a Joseon-era fortress in what's now Busan, and that legend credits Dongnae residents with throwing scallions while repelling Japanese soldiers during the Imjin War, 1592 to 1598. Wikipedia itself flags this as legend, not documented history, so treat it as the kind of story Koreans tell rather than a sourced fact. The same entry says the dish reached the king's table and grew popular once Dongnae's own market took off.

My take A bag of mixed seafood isn't cheap wherever you live, and that makes a first attempt feel riskier than it should. If I were starting out, I'd skip the optional egg and chili the first time and put all the focus on two things: draining the seafood bone-dry and keeping the batter thin. Nail those, and the toppings are the easy part to add on the second try.

  • If you have one more hour Make your own pancake mix instead of buying it: ¾ cup flour, 2 Tbsp rice flour, 2 Tbsp potato or cornstarch, 1 tsp garlic and ½ tsp salt stands in for 1 cup of buchim garu.
  • What locals argue about Whether the rainy-day habit is really about sound, about farmers stuck indoors, or just an excuse to drink makgeolli; none of the explanations are backed by a documented origin, and most Koreans seem happy to repeat all three.
  • Watch for The water temperature. Hyosun's batter calls specifically for ice-cold water, not tap temperature, since that's part of what keeps the crust crisp.
FAQ
Can I make this without seafood?
Yes. Hyosun suggests thin strips of onion, carrot or cabbage in place of seafood, and in a later reply added that thin strips of chicken or pork work too. The batter and scallions stay the same either way.
Why does the restaurant version always taste crispier than mine?
Oil quantity, mostly. Hyosun writes that a generous pour gets a shallow-fry result, and in a reply she adds that restaurants go heavy enough on oil that the technique is "almost deep frying". Nervous about flipping a pan that oily? Her card offers a workaround: make each one in a smaller size instead of one large round.

Buy the seafood mix you can actually find fresh, not the one a recipe photo makes you want. Get the batter thin and the seafood dry, and the rest of the dish mostly takes care of itself.
