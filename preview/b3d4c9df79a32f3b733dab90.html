Four ingredients for the dough, seven for the filling, and almost nobody in a Korean kitchen measures either one by the time they've made a few hundred. Flour dusts the counter, the pleating happens almost without looking, hands moving faster than the conversation around them, and by the time the first tray goes into the steamer the whole apartment smells like garlic and sesame oil before anyone's tasted a single one.

That rhythm comes from repetition, not a secret. The ratio below comes from a 2003 magazine feature that asked three named grandmothers directly for their own mandu recipes, one regional style each, and the kimchi filling is the one that travels best for a first attempt. Twenty to thirty minutes of dough rest is the only step worth timing, and it's the same reason pizza dough wants a rest before you stretch it: the gluten needs a minute to relax before it'll roll out thin without springing back.

What is jjin-mandu (찐만두)?

Mandu cooked by steaming rather than boiling or pan-frying. The wrapper comes out soft and a little glossy, and it's the gentlest of the three methods on dumplings you've just shaped, since nothing drags across a hot pan or churns in boiling water.

🥟 A kimchi-pork filling by the gram, not by feel

The dough is four ingredients, and the filling is seven, each one measured rather than eyeballed. Lady Kyunghyang's 2003 recipe calls for 2 cups of flour, 1/3 teaspoon of salt, 4 tablespoons of water and 1 egg for the wrapper dough, rolled thin and cut into rounds about 6cm across.

Dough Amount
Flour 2 cups
Salt 1/3 tsp
Water 4 Tbsp
Egg 1

Mix into a dough, rest 20-30 minutes in a covered bowl, then roll thin and cut into 6cm rounds. Source: Kyunghyang's Lady Kyunghyang, grandmothers' recipe collection, 2003.

The filling is where the kimchi comes in. 200g of kimchi, 50g each of ground beef and pork, 50g of bean sprouts and a sixth of a block of tofu, seasoned with a tablespoon each of minced green onion, sesame salt and sesame oil, a half-tablespoon of minced garlic, and salt and pepper to taste.

A torn wrapper almost always means something in that filling was still wet, not a folding mistake. The same feature gives a squeeze-dry step for every watery ingredient: chop the kimchi fine and wring its liquid out by hand, mash the tofu and squeeze it dry, then blanch the bean sprouts in salted water, rinse them, and squeeze and chop them too.

Skip any one of those steps and the dough has to absorb the difference. Wet filling soaks straight into the wrapper from the inside, which is what makes it go translucent and sticky, prone to splitting the moment you try to pleat it, long before it ever touches a pan or a pot of water.

What is mandu-pi (만두피)?

The wrapper itself, sold pre-made at any Korean grocery store. It's thinner than a Chinese egg roll wrapper and closer to a gyoza skin, which is exactly why it tears if the filling inside is doing extra work holding in liquid.

There's no shortcut around the squeezing. Twist the kimchi and the bean sprouts in a clean kitchen towel over the sink until the water stops running, and press the tofu between your palms until it crumbles dry rather than wet. It feels like overkill the first time. It isn't.

🍳 Steamed, boiled, fried: one dough, three dinners

The same filling works in a steamer, a pot of soup, or a hot pan, and nothing about the recipe above changes. Steamed mandu (찐만두) keeps the wrapper soft; boiled mandu (물만두) dropped into broth makes mandu-guk, the dumpling soup eaten at Seollal; and pan-fried mandu (군만두) gets a crisp, browned bottom in a little oil.

For a crunchier, occasion version, the same 2003 feature also deep-fries a tofu-and-chicken mandu: dumplings go into 200°C oil until crisp, then drain, finished in a quick sweet-and-sour sauce. That's more effort than a weeknight needs, but the oil temperature is worth borrowing if you ever do pan-fry a batch and want the bottom properly crisp rather than just browned.

🛒 Buying the wrapper outside Korea

A 14oz (398g) pack of round dumpling wrappers runs $2.99 at H Mart, sold refrigerated under its own Haioreum brand and labeled specifically for mandu. One pack easily covers a full batch of the filling above, with skins left over for a second round.

If there's no Korean grocery nearby, a gyoza wrapper is the closest substitute: round, a similar thickness, and sold in most supermarkets with an Asian foods aisle. A square wonton or egg roll wrapper will work in a pinch, but it tends to be thinner and more delicate, so handle it gently and expect a slightly different bite once it's cooked.

My take The part that actually goes wrong on a first try is almost never the folding. It's reaching for a handful of chopped kimchi straight from the jar without draining it first, because measuring feels like it's slowing you down. If I were making this for the first time, I'd weigh the filling ingredients once, squeeze everything dry before it touches the bowl, and only worry about making the pleats look neat on the second tray. The first tray is for learning what the dough can hold.
Go deeper

  • What locals argue about Beef, pork, or both. This recipe splits it 50g and 50g, but plenty of home cooks use just one meat, and some skip meat entirely for a vegetable-and-tofu version with the same squeeze-dry rules.
  • Watch for Wrappers drying out while you shape the rest of the batch. Keep the stack covered with a damp cloth or plastic wrap, since a dry edge won't seal no matter how carefully you pinch it.
FAQ
What should I actually dip these in?
Soy sauce cut with rice vinegar, roughly equal parts, is the everyday default at most Korean tables, sometimes with a few drops of sesame oil or a pinch of chili flakes stirred in right before serving. Skip bottled sweet chili or duck sauce; both read as more Chinese-restaurant than homestyle, and they'll overpower the seasoning already built into this filling rather than complementing it.
Can I freeze the filling instead of the shaped dumplings?
You can, but it's the less useful option. Freezing the mixed filling on its own still leaves you rolling dough or thawing wrappers later, while freezing shaped, uncooked dumplings means a future dinner is a direct trip from freezer to steamer or pot, no prep in between.

Weigh the filling the first time, even if it feels unnecessary. Squeeze the kimchi, the tofu and the sprouts until your hands are tired, and the rest of the recipe takes care of itself.
