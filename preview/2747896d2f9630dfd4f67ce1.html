Insadong: Free to Walk, Expensive to Keep

Insadong's street is free, but its antique shops pay rent that's doubled since 1987. What to see, and what tea and a kimchi museum ticket cost.

Quick answer
  • Walking costs nothing: Insadong-gil, Ssamziegil's handicraft mall and Jogyesa Temple two minutes away are all free to enter, every day of the week.
  • The real spend is small and specific: a sit-down cup of tea runs ₩6,000-12,000, and the one paid stop worth the detour, Museum Kimchikan, is ₩5,000 for an adult.
  • Pick your day on purpose: go on a weekday if you actually want to browse, since Sunday is when the street closes to cars and the crowd shows up to match.

Turn off the main road at Anguk Station and the city's noise drops by half within a few steps. Hanji lanterns hang over doorways in red and yellow, a brush drags across paper somewhere behind an open shopfront, and the smell that follows you down the alley is roasted barley tea, not exhaust. Insadong has looked like Seoul's one preserved street for so long that it's easy to miss how much work keeps it that way, and how much that work now costs the people still doing it.

The catch isn't getting in. It's what happens to the shop next door when its lease comes up.

What is hanji (한지)?

Traditional Korean paper, hand-made from mulberry bark and strong enough for lanterns, window screens and fans. It's still sold by the roll on Insadong-gil, in colors that go well beyond the plain white most visitors picture.

🚇 Getting there, and what the day actually costs

Ride Line 3 to Anguk Station and leave by Exit 6: Insadong-gil opens off the main road within a couple of minutes on foot, no ticket needed for the street itself. A one-way T-money fare is ₩1,550 (about $1.17), or ₩1,650 for a single-journey paper ticket. Jongno 3-ga Station on Lines 1, 3 and 5 works too, from Exit 5, and Jonggak Station on Line 1 reaches the street's other end from Exit 11.

Three stops along the street cost nothing to enter. Insadong-gil itself is just a street. Ssamziegil, the spiral-ramp handicraft mall at 44 Insadong-gil, is free to browse and open daily 10:30am-8:30pm. Jogyesa Temple, across the main intersection from Exit 6, charges no admission either, though its exact daily hours aren't confirmed since sources disagree on whether the grounds run 24 hours or the main hall keeps a shorter window.

The one paid stop worth building into the day is Museum Kimchikan, a block off the main street: ₩5,000 (about $3.77) for an adult, ₩3,000 for ages 8-18 and ₩2,000 for ages 3-7, closed Mondays, Lunar New Year, Chuseok and Christmas. A hands-on cabbage-kimchi workshop costs an extra ₩25,000 (about $18.86) on top of the ticket.

Insadong-gil is also one of just 13 street segments Jongno-gu runs as permanent car-free streets, alongside Seosunra-gil near Jongmyo and the "Street of Youth" in Gwancheol-dong. The district hasn't published Insadong-gil's exact weekend hours the way it recently did for Seosunra-gil's, so treat "Sunday" as the safe bet rather than a fixed clock.

🍵 Is a sit-down tea actually worth ₩10,000?

Yes, if you treat it as the day's one real stop rather than a quick coffee run: a cup at an Insadong tea house costs ₩6,000-12,000 and usually comes with a small traditional sweet on the side. Think of it less like ordering a drink and more like renting a seat for half an hour, since the dessert and the quiet hanok room are part of what the price buys.

What is a chatjip (찻집)?

A traditional Korean tea house, often set inside a converted hanok. Expect fruit and herbal teas like omija (five-flavor berry) or yuja (citron) rather than espresso, served in traditional ceramic cups.

A ₩6,000 cup and a ₩12,000 cup usually aren't the same drink: rarer brews or anything with extra fruit or nuts sit at the top of that range, while a plain omija or citron tea tends to land closer to the bottom. Either way, the real cost of rushing isn't the won, it's the thirty minutes you don't take to actually sit down.

🖌️ Why "For Rent" signs keep showing up on Insadong-gil

They show up because rent has outrun the shops the street was built to protect: Hong Soo-hi, who runs the ink-brush shop Koohasanbang, says his rent has more than doubled since he took it over in 1987. Many of the area's art-supply stores have already closed, and empty storefronts can sit for a year or two before anyone new moves in.

Myung Sin Dang, an ink-brush shop open more than 30 years, now draws about 30% of its customers from tourists rather than the calligraphy students it once served, and Ahwon Gongbang, a metal-crafts shop six sister artists have run since 1983, is still going a block away. Seoul named Insa-dong a cultural district in 2001 specifically to keep shops like these in place, but the same reporting calls that protection "ineffective".

What is a "cultural district" (문화지구)?

A status under Korean law meant to protect clusters of small cultural businesses, usually through disclosure rules and modest grants rather than any cap on rent. Insa-dong was one of the first areas to get the label, back in 2001.

Ssamziegil is part of the same math rather than separate from it: a small ground-floor shop of about 55 square meters inside the mall runs roughly ₩8,000,000 (about $6,036) a month. Insadong is still commonly cited as home to about 40% of the country's antique shops and galleries and 90% of its traditional stationery shops, a concentration built up long before the current closures and exactly what they're chipping away at.

That's the quiet tension under every souvenir stall on this street: the same crowds that keep Insadong famous are also the reason its rent keeps climbing.

My take The ₩8,000,000-a-month line is the one worth remembering here, not the tea prices. If I were spending an afternoon in Insadong, I'd treat the shops that have been open for decades, the ink-brush counters and metal-craft stalls behind the newer storefronts, as the actual point of the walk, and treat Ssamziegil's spiral mall as the fun ten minutes on the way out rather than the whole visit. The quickest way to make sure Insadong still looks like this in five years is to spend your won at the shop that's been there since before you booked the trip, not the one that opened last spring.
Go deeper
  • If you have one more hour Jogyesa Temple sits right across the main intersection from Anguk Station's Exit 6, a free stop founded in 1395 and renamed to its current name in 1954. Ten minutes north of the main street, Nagwon Arcade still packs a few hundred shops selling everything from the gayageum to electric guitars into one building, open most days 9am-8pm.

FAQ
How do I tell a genuine Korean craft from an import?
Look for a sticker, not a sign. City officials once rolled out a labeling program flagging goods actually produced in Korea, after finding tourists kept carrying home cheap Chinese knockoffs instead. A clerk happy to point one out, or to say plainly that a piece was imported, is doing your authenticity check for you.
Is there a quieter version of this same afternoon?
Kyung-in Museum of Fine Art waits five minutes past Ssamziegil's gate, at 11-4 Insadong 10-gil: six galleries swap their lineup each Wednesday, and its courtyard tea garden, Dawon, keeps visitors clear of the sidewalk entirely, cups poured at a quiet table instead of a crowded counter.

Insadong earns its reputation honestly: free to walk, free to look at Jogyesa's gate, and cheap enough with one cup of tea that a half-day here barely dents a travel budget. Spend the museum ticket money on Museum Kimchikan if you only have room for one paid stop, and spend what's left at a shop old enough to remember when the rent was lower.
