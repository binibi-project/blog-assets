What a Korean Street Address Is Actually Telling You

Korea's building numbers count distance, not order, and the old lot-number address disappeared in 2014. Here's how to actually read one.

Quick answer
  • The number is a distance, not a rank: it rises by two for every 20 meters along the road, so a bigger number just means farther from the start.
  • One address can cover a small village: Korea's biggest apartment complexes often share a single road-name address across dozens of buildings.
  • Save the whole address, not just the number: screenshot the full road name and building number together, since the number alone can be hundreds of meters off.

The first time you stand in a Seoul alley staring at a blue sign, it looks like it should make sense and somehow doesn't. A number climbs on one side of the street and a different number climbs on the other, and the building you want is apparently thirty higher than the one you just passed. A Korean friend glances at the same sign and reads it the way you'd read a mile marker, not a name on a mailbox. That's the whole trick: once you know what the number is counting, the alley stops being a puzzle.

📍 Why doesn't the building number match the one next door?

The number counts distance, not sequence. Along any Korean road, a "basic number" rises by two for every 20 meters, with odd numbers assigned to one side of the street and even numbers to the other. A building's number comes from the basic number closest to its main entrance.

Think of it as a tape measure laid along the sidewalk rather than a queue number at a bakery. Multiply the number by ten and you get roughly the distance in meters from where the road begins. That's not an approximation someone made up for tourists: a research institute's own housing guide uses Gangnam-daero as the example, since the avenue runs 6.99 km and its building numbers top out around 699, exactly ten meters per step.

So two numbers that look close on a page, say #8 and #92, can sit 800-some meters apart on the same street, while #92 and the entrance directly across from it can be only a crosswalk away. That math assumes a straight, named stretch of road; a curve, a dead end, or an alley too small to name throws it off. A hyphenated number, like 12-1, marks exactly that kind of small or short stub, borrowing the main road's number with a suffix instead.

What is a basic number (기초번호)?

The reference number assigned every 20 meters along a road, rising by two each step and split odd/even between the two sides. It's the raw unit behind every building number in the road-name system, whether or not a building actually sits at that exact point.

On paper that looks like a clean swap, but the two systems weren't retired on the same day everywhere. English Wikipedia's entry on Korean addressing notes that road-name signs started going up nationwide from July 29, 2011, years before the old lot numbers actually stopped being valid at the end of 2013, so plenty of buildings, utility bills and even some street furniture quietly carried both for a while.

🏢 Why do giant apartment blocks share one road address?

One name can cover a small village. Korea's largest apartment complexes are often boxed under a single road-name address, so the number on the main gate won't tell a stranger which of a dozen towers, let alone which entrance, they actually need.

Songpa-gu's Jamsil Elle's complex is the case a 2014 report used to make the point: 5,678 households spread across the site, yet the whole complex answers to just two road names, Olympic-ro 95 and Olympic-ro 99. Buildings 130 through 136 actually face Seokchon Lake-ro and Hangang-ram-ro, but their official address still starts with Olympic-ro, about 500 meters from where those buildings stand.

That wasn't a one-off mix-up. The same count found 19,542 apartment complexes nationwide, 88% of the total, carrying only a single road-name address. The ministry grouped them that way for administrative convenience, and current rules let any resident request an individual road name for their own building, though few bother.

What is jibun (지번)?

The old lot-number address, built from a neighborhood name plus a survey-order lot number. It told you roughly which block a building sat on, but said nothing about which road faced it or how far along that road it was, which is the confusion the 2014 switch was meant to fix.

✉️ What changed with the postal code?

It got shorter, and it got tied to the road, not the mail carrier. Korea swapped its 6-digit postal code, in use since 2000, for a 5-digit one on August 1, 2015, built on the new road-name system. An address copied from an older site or document may still carry the wrong one.

Digit position What it identifies
First 3 digits The city, county, or autonomous district
Last 2 digits A sequential number within that area

About 34,000 of these codes exist nationwide, each tied to fixed landmarks like roads, rivers, and railways rather than a postal route. Source: Jeonbuk Ilbo, checked 2026-11-12.

Korea Post wrote to all 20,800,000 households nationwide twice during 2014 to warn them before the switch, and the warning worked: a year after launch, 96 of every 100 pieces of mail already carried the new 5-digit code. Mail still printed with the old 6-digit code started drawing a non-standard postage surcharge from August 1, 2016.

My take If a string of unfamiliar Korean words and numbers makes you want to just hand your phone to a driver, that's the right instinct. The system is logical once you know the trick, but it wasn't built to be decoded on the spot in an alley with a suitcase. Save the full address, not just the building number, and let a map app's pin do the counting a basic number was always meant to do. That's the part worth remembering over the arithmetic.

  • What locals argue about Whether the system is actually finished: the government's 2015 budget for road-name and building-number work fell 32% to 5.77 billion won (about $4.31 million at ₩1,338.73/$1), down from 8.47 billion won in 2014, even as 88% of apartment complexes still shared one address.
  • Watch for faded or missing building-number plates. Gwangju alone counted 127,848 of them in 2020, many installed around 2011 and worn down by rain and sun since, with replacement stuck in a dispute over whether the city or the building's owner should pay.
FAQ
Do I ever actually need the old lot-number (jibun) address?
Mostly just for property paperwork. Land and building-title records kept the jibun lot number, since registration for land itself never moved onto the new format. A tenant or a tourist can go years without ever needing it; a buyer closing on an apartment can't skip it.
What if my driver still can't find the door?
Send a live GPS pin instead of reading the address out loud. A string of unfamiliar syllables is easy to mishear over a phone speaker, and even a perfectly correct address still only narrows a visit down to one stretch of pavement, not a specific gate, floor, or tower entrance.

An address that reads like a ruler still beats one that reads like a raffle ticket, even if it takes a block to get used to doing the math. Next time someone hands you a Korean address, save the whole string, road name and building number together, and let the map count the meters for you.
