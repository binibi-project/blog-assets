Jeonju Hanok Village: The KTX, the Cost and One Catch

The KTX reaches Jeonju Hanok Village in under two hours. Gyeonggijeon's fee, the free Saturday parade, and the bibimbap price gap locals argue about.

Quick answer
  • The KTX is the easy way in: ₩31,600 one way from Yongsan, as fast as 1 hour 37 minutes, with 24 trains running the route daily.
  • The village itself costs nothing to walk: Gyeonggijeon charges ₩3,000 and Jeondong Cathedral is free, but the streets between them are open to anyone.
  • Skip the bibimbap right inside the gate: walk past the walls for the same bowl at roughly half the price.

Seven hundred-some hanok roofs crowd together under one skyline, tile after dark tile, and the first thing you notice walking in from Pungnammun Gate isn't a sign or a ticket booth. It's the quiet.

A grandmother sweeps her own front step at seven in the morning, same as she would in a village with no tourists at all. The smell of someone's breakfast rice drifts out over a low stone wall, and visitors in rented hanbok won't show up on these lanes for another hour yet. This is Jeonju before the crowd arrives, and it's worth timing your train for.

What is a hanok (한옥)?

A traditional Korean house, built with a wooden frame, a tiled or thatched roof, and heated floors warmed from underneath. Jeonju's village has roughly 700 of them still standing in one neighborhood, which is what makes it rare rather than just old.

🚄 How do you actually get to Jeonju?

A standard KTX seat from Yongsan Station runs ₩31,600 one way, and the fastest train covers it in about 1 hour 37 minutes. Not every train is that quick; the slower ones on the same Jeolla Line route take closer to 1 hour 49 minutes, so it's worth checking the specific departure before you book. Twenty-four trains run the route each day, the first leaving Jeonju at 06:25 and the last at 23:11, arriving in Yongsan just before 1 a.m.. That fare already reflects the KTX-SRT fare merger: Korail folded the two systems together on September 1, 2026, cutting KTX prices by an average of 10% to match SRT's; Yongsan-Gwangju Songjeong dropped from ₩46,800 to ₩42,000 as one example.

If the KTX seats you want are sold out, an express bus from Dong Seoul Terminal is the backup, not the upgrade. It runs about 3 hours 28 minutes, roughly double the fastest train, for ₩24,500 in premium class or ₩16,700 standard, with 17 trips a day between a 06:00 first bus and a 22:10 last one. Unless the train is genuinely full, the extra two hours on a bus isn't worth the savings. From either Jeonju Station or the bus terminal, city buses and taxis both run to the hanok village.

🏯 What's actually worth paying for inside the walls?

Only two stops inside Jeonju Hanok Village charge admission, and the walk between them costs nothing. Jeonju Hanok Village's official site lists Gyeonggijeon (경기전), the shrine King Taejo's descendants built in 1410 to hold his portrait, as open 10 a.m. to 6 p.m. year-round, with adult admission running ₩1,000 to ₩3,000 depending on residency and free entry on Culture Day, the last Wednesday of every month. Pay the full rate as a visitor and you're still looking at the price of a convenience-store snack for a courtyard that founded a dynasty's memory.

What is Gyeonggijeon (경기전)?

A Joseon-dynasty shrine built to house a portrait of Taejo, the first king of Joseon and a native of the Jeonju region. It's the reason Jeonju calls itself the "root" of the dynasty rather than just another old town.

Jeondong Catholic Cathedral, a short walk from Gyeonggijeon's gate, charges nothing at all. Built between 1907 and 1914 in a Romanesque and Byzantine style that the city's own tourism office calls one of the finest examples of cathedral architecture in Asia, it's open 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. outside of mass times. Jeonju Hyanggyo, the old Confucian school a short walk further in, is also free, open the same 10-to-6 hours.

If you're there on a Saturday between April and October, time your visit for 3 p.m. Every Saturday from April 18 through October 31, 2026, a free street parade called Nosang Nori-ya runs down Taejo-ro from Namcheon Bridge to Gyeonggi Hall, with a dragon-flag performance and drumming, plus a comic street play, and visitors can join the procession itself. Check the date before you go, since the season ends at the end of this month.

What is hanbok (한복)?

Traditional Korean dress, worn today mostly for photos and festivals rather than daily life. Renting a set to walk the village in is one of the most common things visitors do here, and rental shops cluster thickly in the blocks around Gyeonggijeon.

💸 What does the day actually cost, and where's the catch?

The KTX round trip is most of the bill, and the one real trap is where you sit down to eat. A ticket, one shrine fee, one bowl of the city's namesake dish and a coffee add up to far less than the train alone, as long as you don't eat at the first table you see.

The train is non-negotiable, but the meal isn't. Walk past the walls that ring the hanok cluster, and the same bowl of bibimbap drops by roughly half. That gap is old enough that it's less a one-time markup than just how the village prices itself now.

My take A day here is easy to overpack: the shrine, the cathedral, a hanbok rental, maybe the Saturday parade too, all squeezed around one KTX round trip. Jeonju's one of the cities I've actually been to in the last two years, and if I were doing it again, I'd protect the parade time if it lines up with a Saturday rather than try to fit in everything else first. I'd also walk past the walls for lunch instead of sitting down at the first table I saw. The bowl's the same either way; only the rent is different.

FAQ
Do I need to book anything before I show up?
Not for an individual or a small group. Volunteer-led walking tours leave at set times through the day, and parties under 20 can simply join one without registering ahead; only larger groups need to arrange a slot one week to two months out. Bring a translation app regardless, since most of those tours run in Korean.
How much should I budget for a hanbok rental?
Enough flexibility to compare a couple of storefronts before handing over cash. Pricing isn't standardized shop to shop the way museum admission is, with separate hourly and full-day options and extras like hair styling charged on top, so a quick look at two or three doors near the entrance beats renting from whichever one you reach first.

Book the Yongsan KTX first, since a sold-out fast train is the one thing on this trip you can't fix by walking further. Spend the savings from lunch on a second coffee instead of a pricier bowl inside the gate.
