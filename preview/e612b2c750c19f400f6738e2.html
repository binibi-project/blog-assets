Kongguksu in Seoul: Where Locals Actually Eat It

Three Seoul kongguksu shops rated 4.0+ on Google Maps: the 1962 City Hall original, its Yeouido counterpart, and a Gangnam buckwheat specialist.

  • The bowl looks the same everywhere, but the noodles, the beans and even what's shaken onto the table differ shop to shop.

By noon on a sweltering July day, the line outside a plain door behind Seoul's old central post office spills past the building next to it, and almost nobody in it is checking a phone. They're watching the door, because what's waiting past it looks less like lunch than dessert: a pale, thick soy-milk broth, poured cold over wheat noodles, the kind of bowl you end up holding with both hands just to feel the chill through the metal. A Korean grandmother doesn't need convincing that this counts as a meal and not a milkshake, and the first spoonful is usually what convinces everyone else.

What is kongguksu (콩국수)?

Wheat noodles served cold in a thick, unseasoned broth made from blended, boiled soybeans. It's a summer dish that's filling and cooling at the same time, and it arrives plain enough that you're expected to season it yourself at the table.

🏛️ Jinju Hoegwan: the 1962 original behind City Hall

Start at 진주회관 (Jinju Hoegwan) in Seosomun-dong, where a bowl runs ₩16,000 (about $11.97) and comes with no garnish at all, just broth and noodles that blend wheat flour with bean flour and potato starch for extra chew. GQ Korea's roundup of Seoul's kongguksu shops says the shop opened in 1962 and is now run by a third generation of the same family, and it's one of 50 restaurants on Seoul's official Future Heritage list of old eateries, filed there specifically for its kongguksu. The address is 서울 중구 세종대로11길 26, near City Hall Station; the exact exit isn't confirmed, so a map app beats guessing once you're above ground.

Kongguksu is a seasonal dish here, only on the menu from March through November, and the kitchen runs a prepaid system, so settle the bill before you sit down. Hours run weekdays 11:00-21:00 with a last order at 20:30, and 11:00-20:00 on Saturdays, with Sundays closed. Google Maps rates it 4.2 from 3,577 reviews, checked today.

🌉 Jinju-jip: Yeouido's half of the "twin peaks"

In Yeouido, 진주집 (Jinju-jip) is the shop GQ Korea names alongside Jinju Hoegwan as one of Seoul's two defining kongguksu addresses, and it uses only domestic beans. It sits in the basement of a Yeouido department store at 서울 영등포구 국제금융로6길 33, a few minutes from the district's office towers. A touch of salt goes into the broth itself to round off the raw-bean edge before it ever reaches the bowl, the noodles are chewier than the soft style elsewhere, and it comes with a side of sweetened bossam kimchi rather than the everyday kind.

What is bossam kimchi (보쌈김치)?

Whole cabbage leaves wrapped around a stuffing of smaller vegetables and pear, with seafood sometimes added, fermented as a single bundle instead of being cut into bite-size pieces the way everyday kimchi is. It's usually a little sweeter, and worth ordering on its own even outside this dish.

Hours run weekdays 10:00-20:00 and Saturdays 10:00-19:00. The bowl's exact price and the nearest subway exit aren't confirmed; Yeouido Station and Yeouinaru Station are both within a short walk of the building, so budget extra time and check the current price before you order. Lunchtime draws a crowd of nearby office workers, so a 2pm visit beats noon if you'd rather sit than stand in line. Google Maps rates it 4.2 from 3,798 reviews, checked today.

🥢 Matjarang: Gangnam's buckwheat specialist, open the latest

In Gangnam, 맛자랑 (Matjarang) swaps the usual wheat noodles for buckwheat made to order, and GQ Korea counts it among Seoul's "big three" kongguksu addresses. The address is 서울 강남구 선릉로 524, 107호; the nearest subway station isn't confirmed, so check a map before heading out. It's open every day of the year, 11:30-21:50, the latest closing time of the three.

The bowl's price isn't confirmed. An undated online estimate puts it near ₩15,000, but without a dated source behind it, don't take that number to the counter. Google Maps rates it 4.1 from 152 reviews, checked today.

What's the difference between wheat and buckwheat (memil) noodles in kongguksu?

Most kongguksu comes with plain wheat noodles: pale in color, chewy in the bite. A buckwheat version swaps in memil-myeon, the same gray-brown noodle used in mul-naengmyeon, which is nuttier and a little more fragile once it sits in the broth.

On paper, all three bowls look like the same pale soup, but the beans and noodles behind that color aren't interchangeable. Jinju Hoegwan blends bean flour and potato starch straight into its noodles, Jinju-jip salts its broth before it ever reaches the table, and Matjarang skips wheat for buckwheat entirely. If you only have time for one stop, pick based on which of those differences actually matters to you, not on which name you recognize.

Order with "콩국수 하나 주세요" (kong-guk-su ha-na ju-se-yo, "one kongguksu, please"). None of the three has a confirmed English menu, so it's worth screenshotting the Korean name before you go. At Jinju Hoegwan specifically, pay first; the bowl follows.

🧂 Taste the broth plain before reaching for either shaker once you sit down, since both camps are right often enough that the only wrong move is guessing blind. The split between "소금파" (the salt camp) and "설탕파" (the sugar camp) comes up at almost every kongguksu table in Korea: salt is generally said to bring out the bean's savory side, while sugar adds a sweeter depth. Some shops set both shakers on the table and expect a bite or two first, rather than seasoning on sight.

My take Gangnam lunches blur together after a while, and I eat out around Samseong and Yeoksam often enough that a kitchen staying open until 21:50 actually changes my plans, not just my mood. Matjarang's buckwheat noodles are the version I'd go out of my way for on a weekday, since most of what's nearby shuts its kitchen well before nine. If I only had one afternoon and wanted the bowl that made the Future Heritage list, though, I'd still start at Jinju Hoegwan — a dish that's survived 64 years with no garnish at all has earned the detour.

FAQ
Is this safe for someone avoiding dairy?
Mostly, yes. A soybean-based broth like this carries no dairy at all, though a few modern plates finish with a sliced hard-boiled egg or shredded ham, so say "계란 빼주세요" (gyeran ppae juseyo, "no egg please") if that matters to you.
Does missing kongguksu season still make a trip to Jinju Hoegwan worthwhile?
Not at all. A mixed meat-and-vegetable hot pot and a fried rice loaded with kimchi both stay available year-round, giving a reason to walk in even after the star bowl leaves the lineup.

A bowl this plain shouldn't survive six decades of Seoul's restaurant turnover, and yet here are three kitchens still selling it at a premium over almost anything else on their own menus. Start at Jinju Hoegwan if the history is the draw, and save Matjarang for a Gangnam afternoon when nothing else nearby is still serving lunch.
