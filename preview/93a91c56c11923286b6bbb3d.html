Do You Need to Tip in Korea? The Law Says No

Korea's 2013 price law already bars a separate tip line. The 2023 taxi-app pilot, 2025's viral tip boxes, and the one 20% exception, explained.

Quick answer
  • No, and the receipt proves it: Korean law has required one final, all-inclusive price on restaurant and cafe menus since 2013, so there's no separate tip or service line to add money to.
  • Businesses that tried anyway got buried in backlash: a taxi app's 2023 tip button, a pizza shop's forced "thank-you" charge, and two 2025 tip boxes all went viral for the same reason.
  • One spot still adds 20% on top: check the receipt at a five-star hotel bar or fine-dining restaurant before you assume the menu price is final, since that's the one corner where tax and service really do stack.

Slip a server in Seoul a few extra bills and watch gratitude turn into visible discomfort instead. Korea never built its service economy around the idea that a good server, driver or stylist needs your spare cash to feel seen, and that absence runs deeper than habit. It's baked into the price tag itself: the number printed on a menu or ticking up on a meter is treated as a finished contract, not an opening offer. That hasn't stopped people from testing the rule anyway, and nearly every attempt in the last few years has ended the same way, loudly, in public, on social media.

🧾 Why the Receipt Already Has the Answer

Korea settled the tipping question by law, not by custom: since 2013, every restaurant and cafe has had to show one final price that already includes VAT and any service charge, so there's nothing separate left to tip against. Before that, bigger restaurants often printed "VAT 별도" (VAT not included) under their prices, so the number on the menu and the number on the receipt could be two different things. The government's fix, a rule known as the final-price display system, took effect on January 1, 2013, with a grace period that ran through April, and became fully enforced from May 1, 2013.

What is 최종지불가격 표시제 (choejong jibulgagyeok pyosije)?

Korea's "final payment price" labeling rule. Since May 2013, a restaurant, cafe or bakery can't print a price and then add VAT or a service charge after the fact; the number on the menu has to already be the number on the receipt.

The same instinct shows up outside menus, too. Korean restaurants mostly didn't charge for delivery before the 2010s, until Kyochon Chicken became one of the first major chains to start billing for it in 2018, and now almost every delivery order carries a fee. The difference is that the fee shows up before you pay, not after. Korea's rule for "who covers the extra cost" is always the same: put it on the price tag, never spring it on the customer once the plate is already empty. That's exactly why a tip request after the fact reads as a rule violation here, not a kindness.

🚕 When a Taxi App Tried Adding One Anyway

Korea's biggest taxi-hailing app ran its own tipping experiment in July 2023, and riders turned on it almost immediately. Kakao Mobility began piloting a "감사 팁" (thank-you tip) button on July 19, 2023, for rides booked through its affiliated service tiers (Black, 모범, 벤티, 블루, 펫).

What was Kakao T's "감사 팁" (gamsa tip)?

A 2023 pilot feature on Korea's largest taxi app. After a rider gave a driver a 5-star rating, a pop-up let them add a small tip as a thank-you, charged straight to their card on top of the metered fare.

The mechanics were narrow: only after rating a driver five stars could a rider choose ₩1,000, ₩1,500 or ₩2,000 (about $0.75 to $1.51 at today's rate), and the full amount, minus the card fee, went straight to the driver. Kakao Mobility said it had discussed the idea with its franchise council the year before as part of a "shared growth" push, and that any driver caught pressuring a rider to tip would be penalized.

Riders weren't convinced. Two separate pollsters ran surveys within weeks of the launch, and both found the same lopsided result from different angles.

Both surveys ran in the weeks after the July 2023 launch, as reported by The Korea Herald on August 27, 2023; neither publishes its sample size.

The worry wasn't really about ₩1,000. A 36-year-old Seoul office worker told the paper the feature looked like "a covert fare hike," and consumer-science professor Lee Eun-hee argued Kakao Mobility should cut its own commission instead of asking riders to cover driver pay through tips. One 37-year-old rider, Choi Yu-jin, admitted she still dropped ₩1,000 bills into a bar's tip jar around the same time, not because she wanted to, but because she didn't want to look cheap in front of the staff. That's the whole tension in one person: a system built on "no one expects it" starts to wobble the moment a jar, or a button, suggests otherwise.

💵 2025's Viral Tip Boxes, and the One Place Tipping Already Lives

Two more attempts to normalize tipping blew up online in 2025, and both stayed just inside the law's one loophole: a voluntary tip jar isn't illegal, only a forced service charge is.

Dates and outcomes per Twig24's reporting, checked 2026-10-21. "About ₩5,000" is the restaurant's own suggested amount, not a required charge.

The pizza case shows how far "mandatory" can stretch before it becomes illegal rather than just unpopular. The app's only clickable order option was "잘 먹을게요" (I'll enjoy the meal) at ₩2,000, while the free alternative, "안 먹을게요" (I won't eat), couldn't actually be selected, so paying the ₩2,000 tip was the only way to order at all. A second add-on charged ₩500 to skip writing a review, with the review itself worth a free spaghetti dish. After the setup went viral on June 24, 2025, the chain's headquarters apologized the next day for the damage to its name and said it would pursue a breach-of-contract suit, even though the shop's franchise agreement had already ended two years into its term.

Some five-star hotel bars and fine-dining restaurants are the one real exception, and they don't frame it as a tip at all. Those receipts can carry a 10% VAT charge plus a 10% service charge, or bongsaryo, stacked on top of the listed menu price, a pattern one Seoul-focused travel guide describes as standard at that tier. It's not a new problem: a 2015 guest complaint over a Seoul hotel's summer package showed exactly how that stacks up, with a ₩350,000 room rate turning into a ₩718,300 final bill once the room, the restaurant meal and even the minibar beers each picked up their own separate VAT and service lines. The guest's own words still sum up the surprise: "I didn't know this hotel charged the service fee and minibar VAT separately".

My take Frequently eats out in Gangnam, Samseong, Yeoksam and Apgujeong; visits Michelin-listed restaurants fairly often, and that's exactly where this 20% line shows up, never at the neighborhood spots next door. If I'm sitting down somewhere that's clearly playing in that league, I glance at the menu footer before I order, because that's the one place in Korea where the number on the page isn't the number on the receipt. Everywhere else, the advice is almost boring: order, eat, pay what's printed, and don't reach for your wallet a second time. Korea didn't leave tipping out by accident, and trying to fix that for the staff usually just makes everyone at the table uncomfortable.
Go deeper
  • If you have one more hour before your next Korean meal, screenshot the receipt. If a casual restaurant printed a separate VAT or service line on it, that's the one thing the 2013 rule says shouldn't be there, and it's worth asking the counter about.
  • What locals argue about is whether Kakao T's tip button was ever really about driver pay. Professor Lee Eun-hee's complaint was that the company should have cut its own commission first, and the fact that tips only applied to Kakao's own affiliated fleet left some riders suspecting it was a recruiting tool for that fleet as much as a reward for drivers.
  • Watch for a tougher line on hotels. Korea's Culture and Tourism Institute published a report on tourist-hotel service charges in February 2025, and in June 2026 the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism outlined a Tourism Promotion Act plan that would deduct 30 grading points, three times the existing penalty for a fire incident or other illegal act, from any hotel caught collecting an unjustified fee.
FAQ
Why can a hotel legally add that 10-and-10 charge when a restaurant down the street can't?
Different industry, different rulebook, not a loophole anyone snuck in on purpose. A noodle shop or a bakery answers to food-sanitation pricing rules, while a hotel is licensed instead as a lodging business, a separate category that researchers only recently started treating as its own puzzle worth solving. One sells a meal, the other sells a stay, and Korea happens to regulate those two industries on separate tracks.
Does tipping actually buy better service here?
Maybe, in one narrow academic case. Researchers at Soonchunhyang University looked at Japanese restaurants operating in Korea and found a real link between how generously a diner tends to tip and how much polish they expect in return, plus a second link between staff who get tipped and the quality they end up delivering. That's one cuisine at one set of restaurants, not evidence for slipping cash to your taxi driver.

Korea answered the tipping question with a price tag, not a guideline, and every attempt to reopen that question since has run straight into the same wall of public annoyance. Carry small won bills for convenience, not for tipping, and save the second look at your receipt for anywhere that calls itself five-star.
