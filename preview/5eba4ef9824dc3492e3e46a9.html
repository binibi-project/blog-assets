What a K-Pop Lightstick Really Costs, and Where to Buy One

A ₩49,000 BTS lightstick has resold for up to ₩300,000 this year. Real prices, official stores, and what the thing does at a concert.

Quick answer
  • The sticker price is honest, the resale price isn't: official lightsticks run about ₩50,000, but a sold-out version can resell for six times that within minutes of being listed.
  • Buy from the label's own storefront, not a reseller: Weverse Shop, or the agency's own site, is where the price on this page actually applies.
  • Pack it right: take the batteries out before a flight, and call it a "lightstick," not the model name, at security.

The lights come up half a beat before the first note does, and for a second the whole bowl is one color, then another, then a shape nobody in the room controls with their own hands. It looks spontaneous. It isn't.

Every one of those sticks is a small radio, and somewhere behind the stage a production laptop is telling all of them, all at once, what to do next. That's the part most first-time concertgoers don't expect: the lightstick in your hand isn't a souvenir you wave, it's a device the show is talking to.

What is a lightstick (응원봉, eungwonbong)?

A group-specific cheering wand, sold officially by the artist's agency. Early versions just glowed one fixed color; most made since the mid-2010s pair over Bluetooth with an app, so a production team can change every stick in the venue together, mid-show.

💡 Why does a ₩49,000 lightstick resell for ₩300,000?

Because official lightsticks sell out in minutes, and a real one is the only thing that works at the actual show. BTS's current ARMY Bomb, Ver.4, went up for pre-order through Weverse Shop's own notice on February 8, 2026, with shipping starting March 23, priced at ₩49,000, about $32. It sold out twice within minutes of going live. Buy it from Weverse's international storefront instead of the Korean one and the same stick lists at $64, no won price shown at all. That gap alone is a preview of how much a reseller can charge before anyone even calls it scalping.

By March, The Korea Herald reported, resale listings on the secondhand app Bunjang were running ₩100,000-200,000, with some sales reported as high as ₩300,000, roughly 500% over retail. Bunjang's own search data showed interest in the stick up 438% month over month and 1,764% year over year, driven by a BTS comeback concert at Gwanghwamun Square and an April show in Goyang. Some listings sold within 30 minutes of going up. "Ahead of BTS' comeback concert, fans are actively preparing for the event, and even driving demand for older versions of the band's light sticks that are no longer widely available," Bunjang said.

On paper, that price tag is nothing next to a concert ticket. In practice, if you don't catch the pre-order window, the only lightstick left by showtime is the one a stranger is reselling for triple.

The lightstick itself isn't a 2010s invention, and it replaced a much lower-tech habit. Before there were official sticks at all, 1990s fandoms cheered with colored balloons: H.O.T's fans carried white, Sechs Kies yellow, g.o.d sky blue, Shinhwa orange. The first official lightstick came from a solo act, Se7en, in 2008, shaped like a "7"; Big Bang's yellow crown-shaped "Bang Bong" followed soon after and effectively started the modern lightstick era.

The jump to Bluetooth came from SM Entertainment in 2016. Fans entered their seat number through a smartphone app, and the system synced every paired lightstick in the venue to the concert's own sound and lighting, turning the whole crowd into a single, remote-controlled display. Think of it less like a glow stick and more like a stadium scoreboard made of thousands of tiny screens, each one somebody's hand.

What does "syncing" actually change?

Color, brightness, and blink pattern, pushed out from the production booth in real time. A lightstick with no signal just holds whatever setting you left it on; a synced one moves with the show, section by section, sometimes down to a single member's color during a solo stage.

The tech kept moving after that. Ateez introduced a motion-sensing version in 2024: wave it faster, and the LED color and flash speed change with your hand. HYBE has also supplied synced lightsticks to Team Korea at the Paris Olympics, taking the concert trick to a stadium that had nothing to do with K-pop. Prices across most official sticks still land close to where they started: around ₩50,000, about $35.

🛍️ Where to buy the real thing

Buy from Weverse Shop or the agency's own storefront: the physical options in Seoul have thinned out. Weverse Shop (shop.weverse.io) is the widest single channel now, listing official lightsticks for BTS, NewJeans and other HYBE-label acts under one storefront. SM Entertainment's current flagship store, Kwangya@SMTOWN, sells its artists' official goods from the basement of D Tower in Seongsu, a short walk from Seoul Forest Station Exit 4 on the Suin-Bundang Line, open 10:30-20:00.

One option that used to top every visitor's list is gone. HYBE Insight, the Yongsan exhibition space, has been closed since January 15, 2023; HYBE's own notice says it ended operations there and promises a return "in a new, more developed space," with no date given. If an itinerary still lists it, cross it off.

Check the storefront's own country setting before you pay, since the same product can carry two very different price tags depending on whether you're buying through the domestic or international version of a shop, exactly like the gap between Weverse's two storefronts above.

🎇 Concert etiquette: what your lightstick is doing in the crowd

Bring the official one, or don't bother bringing one at all. Only a lightstick paired to that specific act's app joins the synced show; anything else just sits there glowing its own color while the section around it changes. That's the whole etiquette question, really: an unpaired stick doesn't ruin anyone's night, it just means yours won't turn with the crowd's.

If you're flying in for the show, there's one more thing worth sorting out before you pack. See the FAQ below for what's actually inside the stick and why it rarely causes trouble at security.

My take Watching a resale listing double overnight is a specific kind of frustrating, because the price didn't change, your access did. If a trip is built around one specific tour, I'd treat the official pre-order window as the real ticket and set a calendar alert for it, the same way you'd treat concert presale itself. Paying triple on a resale app for a stick you'll wave for two hours is a call only you can make, but I would not build a travel budget assuming you'll get one that way.
Go deeper
  • If you have one more hour before the show check whether your specific tour's venue actually requires a paired lightstick for the synced segments, since a plain unsynced one still works fine for waving along.
  • What locals argue about whether the yearly "Ver." upgrades are a real technical improvement or a new reason to buy the same stick again; both sides point at the same Bluetooth spec sheet.
  • Watch for pre-order announcement dates on Weverse Shop. For a comeback tour, that's the actual moment the item becomes scarce, weeks before resale prices show it.
FAQ
Is this kind of markup normal for every act, or just BTS?
Treat it as one case study, not a rule. This piece follows a single product tied to a single tour date, and while most groups' gear clusters near the same ballpark figure, how far a scalped unit climbs depends on that particular act's own release timing and how tight the stock actually runs. A quieter act with plenty left on shelves won't see anywhere near the same jump.
Will screening staff give me trouble over the item's name?
Rarely, and it's an easy fix either way. Pop the cells out before your bag goes through the scanner, since that's the part screeners actually flag, and if anyone asks what it is, describe it plainly rather than reaching for its full product title.

A cheap item that behaves like an expensive one the moment it sells out says less about the lightstick than about how tight the supply window really is. My next check-in on this is whenever the next version ships, to see whether resale prices spike the same way or whether agencies start printing more stock in response.
