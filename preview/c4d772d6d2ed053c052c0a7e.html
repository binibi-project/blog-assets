Does Your Foreign Card Work in Korea? When You Need Cash

Korean card payments cover 75.5% of transactions, but traditional markets and your phone's wallet are where that confidence can fail you.

Quick answer
  • Your card works almost everywhere with a chip or a tap: convenience stores, cafes, department stores and taxis all take Visa and Mastercard without a fuss.
  • Traditional markets and small stalls still lean on cash: the government is pushing them to add card readers, but plenty of vendors haven't gotten there yet.
  • Carry a small cash buffer before you land: keep about ₩30,000-50,000 in cash and learn to spot a "Global ATM", so a cash-only stall never catches you short.

The first tap always feels like a small miracle. You hold your card to a GS25 register, it beeps green before you've even looked up, and Korea's reputation as a cashless country suddenly makes sense.

Then, an hour later, an ajumma at a market stall waves the same card away. "카드 안 돼요," she says, card doesn't work here, and you're standing over a tray of tteokbokki doing math on money you didn't bring. Both moments are completely normal, and neither one means you did something wrong.

What is a Global ATM?

A cash machine marked "Global ATM" or "Global Service," usually with Visa, Mastercard, Plus, Cirrus, UnionPay or JCB logos on the screen or the casing. These are the ATMs built to read a foreign-issued card; an ordinary Korean bank ATM without that label often won't.

💳 Where your card just works

Convenience stores and cafes almost never turn a foreign Visa or Mastercard away, and taxis rarely do either. Korea's own numbers back this up: in the Bank of Korea's latest payment survey of 3,500 adults, released in May 2025, credit cards covered 46.2% of in-person payments in 2024, debit cards another 16.4%, and "mobile card" apps 12.9%. Add it up and card-based payment of some kind covered 75.5% of transactions, cash only 15.9%. That split flips hard by age: shoppers 60 and older still paid in cash 30.2% of the time, next to just 2.7-5.5% for everyone in their 20s through 50s.

Taxis are a special case worth knowing by name. Under Article 16 of the Act on the Development of the Taxi Transportation Business, a driver whose car has a card reader installed cannot refuse your card or deny you a receipt. Refuse once and the fine is ₩200,000; a second time adds a 10-day license suspension, a third adds 20 days. Card readers have been standard in Seoul taxis since the early 2000s, so this almost never comes up in practice.

🧺 Where you'll want cash

Traditional markets are the one place a card can genuinely fail you. The Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism and the Korea Tourism Organization launched a "K-Tourism Market Smile Campaign" in June 2026 specifically to push fixed pricing and credit-card acceptance at cash-reliant stalls, starting at Busan's Haeundae Market, one of 11 traditional markets in a state-backed improvement program since 2023. Seoul's Mangwon and Gyeongdong markets, Daegu's Seomun Market and Jeju's Dongmun Market are named in the same push.

The campaign exists because, as of today, it's needed. Big markets like Gwangjang are mixed: some stalls have a card machine, plenty don't, and travel guides consistently flag Gwangjang and Busan's Jagalchi as places where a handwritten sign or a shrug is your actual answer. That's one traveler guide's read, not an official count, so treat it as a reason to carry cash rather than a rule for every stall.

What is a traditional market (재래시장)?

A permanent, roofed cluster of small, independently run stalls (food, produce, hardware, clothes) that predates the convenience-store era and still runs mostly cash-to-hand. Think of it as Korea's version of an old-town market hall, minus the uniform pricing a department store gives you.

🏦 Backup cash, backup plastic

Look for the words "Global ATM" or "Global Service," not just any ATM. Those machines, often inside 365-day automated bank corners or tucked into a CU, GS25, 7-Eleven or emart24, are built to read foreign cards; a plain Korean bank ATM frequently rejects them outright. Expect a local operator fee of roughly ₩3,500 at Woori and ₩4,200-5,000 at Hana, on top of whatever your own card issuer charges, plus a per-transaction limit somewhere between ₩300,000 and ₩1,000,000 depending on the machine and your home bank.

What is DCC (dynamic currency conversion)?

A prompt that offers to show your withdrawal in your home currency instead of won. It sounds like a convenience, but the exchange rate it quotes is set by the ATM operator, not your bank, and usually runs worse. Choosing KRW and letting your own bank convert it is almost always cheaper.

Don't plan your whole trip around your phone's wallet either. Apple Pay launched in Korea on March 20, 2023, for Visa and Mastercard plus Hyundai Card's own local cards, but at launch only about 10% of the country's 2.9 million retail terminals had NFC switched on. One travel guide notes some visitors have gotten a foreign Visa or Mastercard to work through Apple Pay here, but calls it inconsistent rather than guaranteed, and warns your home bank may still add its own foreign-transaction fee on top.

The average Korean already carries more than six cards and uses one close to 100 times a year, yet contactless tap-to-pay adoption is still, in GlobalData's words, "relatively modest" next to how saturated cards already are. Korea got comfortable with the chip-insert and the signature pad long before it got comfortable with the tap.

What is NFC (tap-to-pay)?

The short-range wireless chip that lets a phone or a contactless card pay by touching a reader instead of inserting it. A terminal needs its own NFC reader switched on for this to work, and the chip in your card or phone isn't enough on its own.

My take If you're the kind of traveler who keeps a mental tally of your cash, Korea will mess with that instinct for about a day before it clicks. I'd default to tapping the card everywhere and keep one ₩50,000 note folded in a pocket you forget about, purely as a reserve for the one stall that says no. When a Global ATM screen asks whether you want it in your own currency, say no every time; that single screen is where people quietly lose the most money on this trip.
Go deeper
  • If you have one more day Call your own card issuer before you fly and ask what it charges on a won purchase. That one call matters more than any single ATM fee, since it applies to every tap and swipe of the whole trip.
  • What locals argue about Whether a country this saturated with cards (six-plus per person, used almost 100 times a year) should still lean so hard on inserting a chip instead of tapping it. GlobalData calls contactless adoption here "relatively modest" even as card use keeps climbing.
  • Watch for Seoul's plan to let foreign contactless cards tap directly on city buses, with Tmoney testing the system through the end of 2026 toward a 2027 start, before it expands to Incheon and airport buses.
FAQ
Does a strange error at a Korean register mean my card is actually declined?
Not necessarily. Some older Korean point-of-sale terminals throw a chip or magnetic-stripe read error on a perfectly good foreign card; the fix is to ask the cashier "탭으로 될까요?" (tap-eu-ro dwel-kka-yo, "can I tap it?") and hold the card to the reader instead of inserting it. If that also fails, it's worth trying a second card before assuming the first one is the problem.
Should I still pick up a T-money if my bank plastic already taps for everything else?
Yes, because a turnstile won't scan anything except a transit card. Grab a T-money card at a kiosk near any station entrance, feed it a few spare notes, and you're set for buses and trains alike, with none of the coin-counting a one-off paper ticket forces on you.

For one trip, the rule that actually saves you trouble is simple: tap your card first, and only reach for cash once something says no. Markets, small stalls and the occasional older taxi are the places that'll ask, and now you know why.
