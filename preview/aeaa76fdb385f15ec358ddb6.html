How to Make Bungeoppang (Korean Fish-Shaped Red Bean Pastry) at Home

Beyond Kimchee's 150-gram batter method, the filling mistake that tears the shape, and why a street bungeoppang now runs ₩1,000-1,500 a piece.

Quick answer
  • Pour it, don't scoop it: the usual mistake is batter as thick as pancake mix; it needs to run like heavy cream to coat the mold evenly.
  • Fill the body, not the tail: about 1.5 tablespoons of red bean paste goes in the center only, since the tail has no room for it.
  • Act first: let a finished piece sit a minute before you bite into the tail, since the filling stays hot long after the shell looks done.

Most homemade bungeoppang turns out dense and a little eggy, nothing like the thin, crackling shell from a street cart, and a thicker batter isn't the fix. It's the cause. Get the batter thin enough to pour, not thick enough to scoop, and the rest follows on its own: butter browning in a hot mold, red bean paste turning glossy and soft inside a shell that snaps at the tail. That smell belongs to one specific kind of evening in Korea, the first real cold snap that sends everyone's hands looking for something warm to hold.

It also doesn't need a trip to Korea to make. The mold is the one piece of equipment worth tracking down, and once it's on the stove, the rest of this recipe is closer to pancakes than to anything that needs a pastry background.

What is bungeoppang (붕어빵)?

Literally "carp bread," named for its fish-shaped shell rather than any fish inside it. It reached Korea in the 1930s and is generally traced to Japan's taiyaki, a similar pastry shaped like a sea bream instead of a carp.

🥮 Beyond Kimchee's 150-gram batter method

Whisk 150 grams of cake flour with 40 grams of sweet rice flour, 4 grams of baking powder, 18 grams of sugar and a pinch of salt, then loosen it with 300 ml of milk and 28 grams of melted butter until the batter pours like heavy cream. Beyond Kimchee's recipe serves 8 in about 35 minutes total: 5 minutes to mix the batter and roughly 30 at the mold.

What is chapssal-garu (찹쌀가루)?

Sweet rice flour, made from glutinous rice. A small amount mixed into a batter gives it a slight chew that plain wheat flour alone doesn't have.

Heat the mold over medium-low heat and brush a thin layer of oil into each fish-shaped cavity before the first pour. Fill a cavity about 70% full with batter, add about 1.5 tablespoons of red bean paste to the center, then cover the filling with a little more batter so it's sealed inside. Overflow at the seam is normal and gets trimmed off later.

Close the mold and flip it, cooking about 3 to 4 minutes on the first side and 2 to 3 more after the flip, flipping again as needed until both sides are deep gold. Most molds are built for a gas burner, so an induction or electric cooktop may need a portable gas stove instead.

🐟 Where this recipe usually goes wrong

The filling belongs in the body of the fish, not the tail, and overfilling either section is the most common way to tear the shell. A Korean-American recipe that measures closer to street-vendor portions calls for about 2 teaspoons of red beans per piece, spooned in only after the first layer of batter covers the eyes, with the tail left empty on purpose.

A mold that isn't hot enough is the second usual problem: batter sticks to a cool mold instead of releasing cleanly, so give it a full minute or two to heat before the first pour.

What is taiyaki?

Japan's sea-bream-shaped cousin of bungeoppang, usually filled the same way with sweet red bean paste. A taiyaki pan, or even an ebelskiver pan sold for Danish pancakes, works as a substitute mold when a bungeoppang-specific one isn't sold nearby.

Let a finished piece sit for a moment before the first bite, since the red bean paste inside holds heat well past the point where the shell looks cool enough to handle. Breaking it open at the tail first lets the steam out before it catches your tongue.

💸 Why one now costs ₩1,000-1,500 on the street

A single bungeoppang in central Seoul districts like Gangnam and Jongno often runs ₩1,000 to 1,500, as of December 2025, up from the standard "3 for ₩2,000" (or "2 for ₩1,000") deal that was common the year before. Domestic red beans' wholesale price reached ₩721,800 per 40kg as reported in December 2025, 33.8% higher than the ₩539,400 recorded on November 12, 2024, and vendors cite that cost directly, alongside rising flour, sugar and LPG fuel prices.

My take A bungeoppang mold can look like a one-trick gadget sitting in a drawer eleven months of the year, and that's the real reason most people never make this at home rather than any step in the recipe itself. If the mold is the hesitation, I'd treat the first batch as a test: make one round, see if it gets used again before next winter, and only then decide whether it earns a permanent spot in the cupboard. If a mold isn't in the cards at all, a taiyaki or ebelskiver pan gets you most of the way there without a special purchase.

FAQ
Where do I actually find a bungeoppang mold if I don't already own one?
Check a Korean or other Asian grocery store before ordering blind online: the mold is sold there as regular kitchenware, not as a specialty import, which usually means a lower price and no shipping wait. A taiyaki or ebelskiver pan works too if neither option turns one up.
Where do I find red bean paste, or a substitute, outside Korea?
Most Korean and other Asian grocery stores stock sweetened red bean paste canned or in tubs, usually near the rice flour and other baking items, which is an easier starting point than cooking dried beans down from scratch. If it's nowhere nearby, custard or even a chocolate-hazelnut spread works as a non-traditional stand-in filling.

Buy the mold only if a few batches a winter sound like something worth doing again, and borrow a taiyaki pan if you're not sure yet. The one step that actually decides whether this recipe works is a thin batter and a hot mold, not the brand of pan underneath it.
