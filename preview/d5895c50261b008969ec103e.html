Where Locals Actually Eat Jjajangmyeon in Incheon Chinatown

The building that invented jjajangmyeon is a museum now. Two real kitchens up the street still serve it: a ₩11,500 bowl from the inventor's own granddaughter and a ₩10,000 white jjajang found almost nowhere else.

The most famous jjajangmyeon address in Korea doesn't actually serve jjajangmyeon anymore. Climb the street from Incheon Station's Chinatown gate and the building where the dish was first sold sits behind museum tickets, not a counter. A Shandong migrant named Woo Hee-gwang opened a guesthouse there in 1907, renamed it Gonghwachun in 1912, and watched his noodle shop close for good in 1983. What's left a few doors up is better than a plaque: a woman still frying scallions in the same neighborhood, and she happens to be his granddaughter.

Two shops carry that street's real weight today, and neither is the building with his name still painted on the trademark. One keeps his bloodline in the kitchen. The other reinvented the sauce itself, swapping out the black bean paste everyone expects for one made from white beans.

What is jjajangmyeon (짜장면)?

Wheat noodles tossed in a thick, dark sauce of caramelized black bean paste, pork and vegetables. Korean cooks adapted it from the Chinese dish zhajiangmian after Shandong laborers brought the recipe to Incheon's port in the late 1800s, and it became Korea's everyday order-in dish.

🥢 Sinseungbanjeom: the granddaughter's uni-jjajang

Order the ₩11,500 uni-jjajang (유니짜장면) at 신승반점 (Sinseungbanjeom), where ground pork gets worked straight into the sauce instead of left sitting on top of a fried-egg garnish. The shop sits at 인천광역시 중구 차이나타운로44번길 31-3, right where the street starts climbing from the Chinatown gate across from Incheon Station. Its owner, Wang Ae-ju, is the granddaughter of Woo Hee-gwang himself.

It's open 11:00-21:00 daily, with a break from 15:30 to 16:40 and last orders at 14:50 and 20:20. Show up right after a long lunch and you'll find the door locked until mid-afternoon.

What is uni-jjajang (유니짜장)?

A variation where the pork and vegetables are chopped fine and cooked directly into the chunjang paste, rather than stir-fried and spooned on top the way regular jjajangmyeon is served. The sauce turns thicker and more savory, closer to a meat ragu than a topping.

tvN's Wednesday Food Talk (수요미식회) opened its jjajangmyeon episode here on April 1, 2015, the first stop of the night. TV exposure can mean a queue at peak meal times, so it's worth arriving outside the lunch and dinner rush.

Say "유니짜장 하나 주세요" (yu-ni-jja-jang ha-na ju-se-yo, "one uni-jjajang, please"). If you want to split a second dish, the small chapssal-tangsuyuk (sweet-and-sour pork) runs ₩30,000.

🥡 Is Yeonggyeong's white jjajang worth the detour?

Yes, if a sauce that isn't black is reason enough to walk a little further: 연경 (Yeonggyeong)'s ₩10,000 하얀짜장 (white jjajang) swaps the usual caramelized black bean paste for one made from white beans. The restaurant is at 인천광역시 중구 차이나타운로 41, a few minutes further up Chinatown's sloped main street from Sinseungbanjeom. The color is the whole point here, not a gimmick: without the caramelized paste, the sauce comes out pale and milder, closer to a savory cream sauce than the sweet, dark version most visitors already know.

It's open 10:30-21:30 every day with no break time listed. That makes it the easier of the two to fold into a late lunch or an early dinner without checking a clock first.

What makes jjajang "white" (하얀짜장)?

Regular chunjang is made by caramelizing black soybean paste, which is what turns jjajangmyeon's sauce dark brown. Swap in white bean paste instead and skip that caramelizing step, and the sauce stays pale and comes out milder, with less of the usual sweetness.

Say "하얀짜장 하나 주세요" (ha-yan-jja-jang ha-na ju-se-yo, "one white jjajang, please"). Reach the shop at 0507-1389-7894 if you want to check a wait before you climb the hill.

🚶 Getting there without losing an hour to the line

Exits 1, 2 and 3 of Incheon Station all put you in front of Chinatown's main gate, so there's no wrong exit to take. The station is the western end of the Gyeongin Line, part of Seoul's Line 1, and sits right next to Chinatown itself.

Neither shop's listing confirms whether foreign cards work, so carry cash as a backup either way. Both sit close enough to the gate that backtracking for an ATM won't cost you your place in line.

My take Both bowls cost about the same, so the real choice is what you're craving, not which shop is "better." If I were picking, I'd go richer and meatier when jjajangmyeon should taste exactly like the version you already know, just made with more care, and go pale and white when the whole point is trying something that doesn't exist on most other Korean-Chinese menus. Weigh that against your own appetite rather than a stranger's ranking.
Go deeper
  • If you have one more hour The Jjajangmyeon Museum, inside the original Gonghwachun building, opens 09:00-18:00 Tuesday through Sunday and charges adults ₩1,000 (about $0.74); it's closed Mondays.
  • What locals argue about Who actually gets to use the Gonghwachun name. The original shop closed in 1983, and a company unconnected to the founder's family trademarked "공화춘" in 2004, while his real granddaughter runs her own shop a few doors away under a different name.
  • Watch for Sinseungbanjeom's 15:30-16:40 break. Walk up right after lunch expecting a late bowl and you'll find the door locked until Yeonggyeong's dinner crowd is already seated.
FAQ
How do I know Sinseungbanjeom isn't just a famous address coasting on its location?
Korea's Blue Ribbon Survey, an independent restaurant guide, has picked it every single year since 2013. A years-long streak like that is a harder bar to fake than a cluster of five-star reviews near a landmark.
Will stairs or a stroller be a problem at either stop?
Only possibly at Yeonggyeong, whose dining room sits on the second floor; multiple visitor write-ups describe both an elevator and a staircase up, so a stroller or a cane shouldn't be an issue either way. Its window seats also face the Chinatown stairway landmark locals call the Emperor's Steps, worth claiming if one's free.

Neither shop needs this post to stay busy, and the walk between them is short enough to just pick whichever door has the shorter line that day. Go to Sinseungbanjeom if the name on the family tree matters to you, and save Yeonggyeong for the day you want jjajangmyeon to surprise you.
