How to Make Hotteok (Korean Sweet Pancakes) at Home

Beyond Kimchee's gram recipe for eight hotteok, the step that tears the dough and lets molten sugar out, and where to buy the harder flour abroad.

Quick answer
  • One dough, eight pancakes: Beyond Kimchee's gram amounts for both the dough and the filling, with a 1-2 hour rise before any of it goes near a pan.
  • The real danger isn't a burnt outside: it's the molten sugar filling tearing through a pancake pressed too thin and burning your mouth or hand.
  • Act on it: press each pancake to about half an inch thick, not flatter, and pinch the dough fully closed before it hits the oil.

Ever bitten into a hotteok straight off a winter street cart and felt the syrup inside scald your tongue before you'd even registered the crisp shell? That's not bad luck. A yeasted dough gets wrapped around sugar that turns to liquid the moment it hits a hot pan, then sealed shut so the whole thing stays molten until you bite in. Seoul's evenings are only just turning cold enough for the carts to reappear, and the home version skips the queue entirely if you weigh the dough and filling instead of guessing at them.

The recipe below is adapted from Beyond Kimchee, the site run by the Korean-born cookbook author Hyegyoung K. Ford, known as Holly, with her gram amounts kept as she gives them.

🥞 What goes into hotteok, measured not guessed?

The dough is eight ingredients and the filling is five, every one of them weighed rather than scooped by feel. Beyond Kimchee's version mixes all-purpose flour with a little sweet rice flour, proofs instant yeast straight into the dry mix, and binds everything with warm milk and a spoon of oil.

Dough Amount
All-purpose flour 2½ cups (375g)
Sweet rice flour (chapssal-garu) ½ cup (40g)
Sugar 1 Tbsp (12g)
Instant yeast 2 tsp (6g)
Baking powder ½ tsp (2g)
Salt 1 tsp (6g)
Lukewarm milk 1⅓ cups (315ml), more if needed
Oil 1 Tbsp (15ml), plus more for frying

Source: Beyond Kimchee, Hotteok recipe card, checked 2026-10-21.

What is chapssal-garu (찹쌀가루)?

Sweet or glutinous rice flour, ground from short-grain sticky rice. It's the ingredient that makes hotteok dough chewy and a little springy instead of just bready; swap it out for plain rice flour and the pancake turns crisper but loses that chew.

The filling is simpler: two kinds of sugar, cinnamon, a binder to keep it from turning to pure syrup, and whatever nuts you have on hand.

Filling Amount
Light brown sugar ⅓ cup (70g)
Granulated sugar ⅓ cup (60g)
Cinnamon 1 tsp (3g)
Fine breadcrumbs (or 1 tsp flour) 1 Tbsp (8g)
Chopped peanuts or other nuts 4 Tbsp (30g)

Source: Beyond Kimchee, Hotteok recipe card, checked 2026-10-21.

The breadcrumbs matter more than they look. Without a binder, the sugar just melts into pure liquid inside the dough, which is exactly the filling that tears a pancake open; with it, the filling thickens into something closer to a syrup you can actually contain.

🔥 Where hotteok actually goes wrong

A torn pancake, not a burnt one, is the usual disaster, and it happens when the dough gets pressed too thin. Beyond Kimchee's own recipe warns that the filling "will be extremely hot" straight out of the pan, and that pressing a pancake flatter than about half an inch is what lets that molten sugar tear through the dough and escape.

Here's the method, one action per step:

  1. Whisk the flours, yeast, baking powder, sugar and salt together.

  2. Add the warm milk and oil, and stir for 1-2 minutes until the dough turns sticky.

  3. Cover and let it rise until doubled, about 1-2 hours.

  4. Mix the filling ingredients in a small bowl.

  5. Divide the risen dough into 8 portions.

  6. Flatten one portion in an oiled hand, add about 1½ tablespoons of filling, and pull the edges up to seal it closed.

  7. Set it seam-side down on a greased plate, and repeat with the rest.

  8. Heat a generous amount of oil over medium-low heat, and cook each pancake seam-side down for 30 seconds.

  9. Flip it, then press it to about half an inch thick with a hotteok press or a spatula.

  10. Cook 1-2 minutes per side until golden, lowering the heat if it's browning too fast.

What is a hotteok press?

A flat stainless-steel disc on a short wooden handle, used to flatten the filled dough once it's flipped in the pan. A regular spatula does the same job if you don't have one, pressed down evenly so the pancake doesn't end up thinner on one side.

That "extremely hot" warning isn't an accident of this one recipe. Koreans describe a noisy, chaotic scene as "호떡집에 불 났다," the hotteok shop caught fire, a phrase Wikipedia traces to Chinese merchants in Korea arguing over what started a fire at one of their stalls.

What does "호떡집에 불 났다" mean?

Literally "a fire broke out at the hotteok shop," used the way English speakers might say a place "exploded" with noise or chaos. Wikipedia says hotteok reached Korea with Chinese merchants who settled there around the late 19th century, including some who stayed on after the unrest of the Imo Incident, and the idiom is said to come from one of their own stalls catching fire.

🛒 Buying the harder ingredient outside Korea

A $2.99, 1-pound bag of Koda Farms Mochiko sweet rice flour, sold at H Mart, covers several batches of this dough. H Mart lists the 16oz bag at $2.99, marked down 25% from $3.99, checked 2026-10-21. The recipe only needs 40g per batch, so one bag stretches across more than ten batches of dough.

No sweet rice flour nearby? Korean Bapsang's version, a separate cup-measure recipe for the same dish, says it can simply be left out or swapped for plain rice flour, and that the author finds plain rice flour gives a crisper pancake instead of the chewier bite sweet rice flour adds. Skip the specialty flour on a first attempt rather than hunting for it, and judge the texture after you've made a batch.

My take A yeast dough and a filling that's been described as capable of leaving a burn reads like more trouble than a weeknight snack is worth, and that's a fair reason to hesitate. If I were making this for the first time, I'd let the dough take the full two hours rather than rush the rise, and I'd leave the first pancake on a plate for a full minute before biting in. The dough is forgiving; the filling, straight out of the pan, is not.
Go deeper
  • If you have one more day Beyond Kimchee's own notes say the dough can rest in the fridge overnight if it's sealed tightly, so you can mix it the night before and only shape and fry the next day.
  • What locals argue about Chewy versus crisp. Sweet rice flour makes the dough springier, while plain rice flour gives a crisper result, and home cooks split over which one actually makes a "real" hotteok.
  • Watch for Hotteok is traditionally a cold-weather food, and Wikipedia notes it's usually eaten in winter. Beyond Kimchee adds that a finished pancake turns tough once it cools, so eat it the same sitting rather than letting a batch sit out.
FAQ
Can I skip all of this and buy a packet mix instead?
Sure, once you know what you're aiming for. Samyang, Ottogi and CJ each sell a boxed version in Korea, already combined with its own sweet-and-nut or sesame mix, so all that's left to do is add water and head for the stove. A box won't teach a first-timer how a properly sealed edge should feel, though, which is the whole point of trying it by hand at least once.
Is there a way to cut back on how sweet it turns out?
Yes, without touching the sealing technique. A published alternative trims the mix to 1/3 cup (71g) packed dark brown sugar, 3 tablespoons (37g) granulated sugar, 3/4 teaspoon cinnamon and 1/4 teaspoon salt. That pinch of salt isn't seasoning anything; it's there purely to balance the sweetness.

Make the full batch of eight even if you're cooking for one. Korean Bapsang notes hotteok freezes well and reheats fine in a toaster or air fryer, so extras don't go to waste just because they cool down first. Weigh the flour and the filling the first time, and give that filling a full minute to cool before anyone bites in.
