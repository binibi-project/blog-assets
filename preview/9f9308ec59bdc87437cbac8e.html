Dakhanmari in Seoul: Where to Eat the Whole-Chicken Pot

Four Seoul dakhanmari shops, three in Dongdaemun's chicken alley and one in Gongneung, with pot prices, hours, subway exits and how to order.

Quick answer
  • Start in the Dongdaemun alley: three of these four shops share one lane between Jongno 5-ga and Dongdaemun stations.
  • Budget ₩27,000-33,000 a pot, plus ₩1,000-3,000 for each noodle, rice-cake or potato add-on.
  • Save the noodles for last: say "칼국수 사리 주세요" once most of the chicken is gone, so they cook in the richest broth.

The pot lands on the burner looking almost too plain. One whole chicken, a few slices of potato, a handful of scallions, broth so clear you can see the bottom of the basin. Then the table gets busy: someone mixes the dipping sauce, someone starts pulling the chicken apart, and by the time the noodles go in, the broth has turned cloudy and rich. It's a meal that needs people around it, which suits a dish born as market food.

What is dakhanmari (닭한마리)?

Literally "one whole chicken." A whole bird is boiled at your table in a wide metal basin with its broth and vegetables. Unlike baeksuk (whole boiled chicken), nothing is stuffed inside the bird (no ginseng, jujube or glutinous rice). You dip the meat in a sauce you mix yourself, then cook noodles or rice cakes in what's left.

The dish grew out of Dongdaemun's markets. Seoul's city news site traces it to chicken kalguksu sold to traders at Dongdaemun and Pyeonghwa markets, and says dakhanmari became widely known from 1978. The name, by the city's account, came from customers rushing for buses at the old Dongdaemun terminal in the 1970s, who would walk in and just shout "dakhanmari!". Once the chicken became the main event, the noodles slid to the end of the meal, where they still sit today.

🐔 Jinokhwa Halmae: the alley's oldest pot

For a first dakhanmari, go to 진옥화할매원조닭한마리 (Jinokhwa Halmae Wonjo Dakhanmari) and order the ₩33,000 (about $24.61) pot. It's at 서울 종로구 종로40가길 18, a five-minute walk from Exit 9 of Dongdaemun Station (Lines 1 and 4). It's open every day 10:30-01:00, last order 23:30, and the annex opens at noon.

The shop's own listing says it opened in 1978 in the food alley of Dongdaemun Jonghap Market. A 2025 Wikitree report calls it the oldest dakhanmari restaurant left near Dongdaemun, notes it started as a chicken kalguksu place, and says the building was rebuilt in February 2009 after a fire. It has more than 8,000 visitor reviews on Naver Map, which tells you how often visitors make this their first stop. Google Maps rating 4.0 from 8,487 reviews (checked 2026-10-12).

Add-ons are cheap and worth it: rice cakes, noodles, scallions or mushrooms are ₩2,000 (about $1.49) each, potatoes ₩3,000 (about $2.24), and a bowl of rice ₩1,000 (about $0.75).

What does sari (사리) mean on the menu?

The dictionary meaning is a coil of noodles (or rope, or thread). On a dakhanmari menu it has stretched to mean any add-on you drop into the pot: 국수사리 (noodles), 떡사리 (rice cakes), 감자사리 (potato). Order them one at a time as the broth reduces.

Here's how a pot usually goes. You eat the chicken first, dipped in a sauce you mix from chili powder, soy sauce, vinegar and mustard to your own taste. If you want the broth brighter, add kimchi and let it boil a little longer; then the noodles go in last.

🍜 Which shop in the alley should you pick?

If Jinokhwa's line looks long, walk a few doors to 원할매소문난원조닭한마리 (Wonhalmae Somunnan Wonjo Dakhanmari, signed 원소닭) or 명동닭한마리 (Myeongdong Dakhanmari); both charge the same ₩33,000 for a pot. Seoul city counted nine dakhanmari shops in this one alley in 2017, ranging from a two-year-old shop to originals past 30, all cooking the same way but with their own broth and sauce. Find the lane through Sinjin Market, the stretch between Jongno 5-ga and Dongdaemun stations; turn in at the alley lined with grilled-fish shops.

Wonhalmae is at 서울 종로구 종로40가길 25, a five-minute walk from Exit 5 of Jongno 5-ga Station (Line 1). It's open 11:00-23:00 Monday to Saturday and 11:00-22:30 on Sunday. Its own listing describes it as a third-generation shop and says it has appeared on SBS's Baek Jong-won's Three Kings (백종원의 3대천왕) and Tasty Guys (맛있는 녀석들). TV exposure can mean a queue at peak meal times, so it's worth arriving outside the lunch and dinner rush.

A two-person set runs ₩37,000 (about $27.59), noodles are ₩2,000 and rice cakes ₩1,000. Google Maps rating 4.0 from 1,472 reviews (checked 2026-10-12).

Myeongdong Dakhanmari is in the same alley despite the name, at 서울 종로구 종로40가길 14, about five minutes from Dongdaemun Station Exit 9. Its pot is listed as 엄나무닭한마리, made with eomnamu (prickly castor-oil tree), one of the broth ingredients that, as Wikitree notes, varies from shop to shop. It's open daily 10:00-00:30. On paper the three alley shops look identical, same price and same block. The real difference is the broth, which each shop makes its own way; Myeongdong Dakhanmari is the one that names an extra ingredient on its menu. Pick by the hours you'll actually be there, then by the broth.

🚇 Is Gongneung's dakhanmari worth the trip?

Yes, if you're already in northeast Seoul: 닭한마리 공릉본점 (Dakhanmari Gongneung Bonjeom) charges ₩27,000 (about $20.14) a pot, ₩6,000 less than the alley. It's at 서울 노원구 동일로 1020, a four-minute walk from Exit 4 of Taereung Station (Lines 6 and 7). It opens 11:30-22:00 with last order at 20:55, closes every Tuesday, and takes a 15:00-17:00 break on weekdays only.

The shop's listing says it uses only fresh, never-frozen chicken, and that its spicy dipping sauce is full of chili seeds, with cold water kimchi (mul-kimchi) as the standard side. Noodles here are ₩3,000, and an extra half chicken is ₩14,000 (about $10.44). With over 5,000 visitor reviews on Naver Map, it's the neighborhood's own answer to Dongdaemun, not an outpost of it. Google Maps rating 4.3 from 1,233 reviews (checked 2026-10-12).

Ordering is the same everywhere: "닭한마리 하나 주세요" (dak-han-ma-ri ha-na ju-se-yo, "one dakhanmari, please"), then "칼국수 사리 주세요" (kal-guk-su sa-ri ju-se-yo) for the noodles at the end. An English menu isn't confirmed at any of the four, so screenshot the Korean names before you go.

Turning up and finding a line is its own small letdown.

My take The worry with dakhanmari is that it looks like a group dish, and a big metal basin of chicken can feel like too much commitment on your first night in Seoul. It isn't. Bring one other person, go to Jinokhwa outside the meal rush, and let the shop's rhythm teach you the order: chicken, sauce, kimchi, noodles. Save Gongneung for a trip when you're already up in Nowon and want the same pot for less.

FAQ
Is dakhanmari spicy? Can kids eat it?
Not by itself. Wikitree notes the dish isn't fiery, and that some restaurants in Japan already sell it under its Korean name, probably because it resembles mizutaki, Japan's own chicken hot pot. For children, ladle out a small serving early, then let grown-ups doctor their dip.
Can I call ahead before crossing town?
Yes, and phoning is smartest before the ride out to Nowon: 02-972-7459 reaches the Taereung shop, so a quick call saves a wasted Tuesday. In the alley, 02-2275-9666 is the 1978 kitchen, 0507-1315-2078 the third-generation one, and 02-2266-8249 the place on 종로40가길 14.

Dakhanmari got its name from people in a hurry, but it rewards the opposite. It's a pot built for sharing, cheap enough to order on a whim and slow enough to keep a table talking. Go to the alley first, and if you fall for it, the Gongneung pot is a good excuse to see a part of Seoul most visitors never do.
